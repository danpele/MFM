Black–Scholes and the Greeks

The Greeks: One Identity, All Sensitivities

    Question for the room Press ▶: volatility σ rises from 10% to 50%. What happens to the gamma of the three-month call at S = K = 100?
    Answer

    It falls from 0.078 to 0.016, almost like 1/σ, while vega stays almost constant (0.194 at σ = 10%, 0.197 at σ = 50%). At K = 100 and σ = 20%, the one-month call has the largest gamma (0.069, against 0.039 at three months and 0.019 at one year) and the fastest time decay (theta −0.043 a day), and the one-year call has the largest vega (0.381 per volatility point). Vega per unit change in σ is ΓS²στ. Per percentage point of volatility, vega is ΓS²στ/100: for the three-month call at S = K = 100 and σ = 20%, it equals 0.197.

    Delta Hedging in Practice

    Discrete Delta Hedging: Algorithm and Error

      Question for the room With daily rebalancing, the standard deviation of the error is 9.5% of the premium. How much more often must the hedge be adjusted to halve it?
      Answer

      About four times as often. Doubling N approximately halves the error variance, not its standard deviation: the standard deviation falls like 1/√N (the estimated log-log slope is −0.48). Halving the standard deviation requires approximately four times as many rebalancings: from 63 to 252 over the three months. In the simulation, the standard deviation falls from 9.5% to 4.8% of the premium. Transaction costs, however, grow with N: like N with a fixed cost per rebalancing, roughly like √N with costs proportional to the traded value.

      Selling Volatility at 20%: Three Outcomes

        Question for the room A three-month call is sold at 20% implied volatility and hedged daily. On what share of the paths does the seller gain if realised volatility is 25%?
        Answer

        In this simulation, the seller gains on 2.2% of the paths. Mean P&L is −0.99 per option and its standard deviation is 0.68. The first-order approximation is vega × (implied volatility − realised volatility), or 0.197 × (20 − 25) ≈ −0.99. Daily rebalancing leaves a discrete hedging error, so some paths still produce positive P&L. With realised volatility of 15%, the mean is +0.98, positive on 99.8% of the paths.

        Implied Volatility and the Smile

        Three Sources of a Smile

          Question for the room Press ▶: the Heston correlation ρ moves from −0.9 to +0.9. What happens to the Heston smile?
          Answer

          The tilt reverses: at ρ = +0.9, the Heston implied volatility is 10.7% at K/S = 0.8 and 28.2% at K/S = 1.2; at ρ = 0 the smile is nearly symmetric (23.7% and 22.4%). On the slide (ρ = −0.7), the highest implied volatility at K/S = 0.8 comes from the Merton jumps: 31.0%, against 16.3% ATM, close to the historical distribution (30.6%, against 13.9% ATM). Measured from the ATM level, the historical wing rises the most: 16.7 points, against 14.7 for Merton and 12.0 for Heston. Heston with ρ = −0.7 gives 28.6%, and its right wing is the lowest (14.6% at K/S = 1.2), because a negative correlation tilts the smile to the left.

          Bitcoin Options and the Volatility Surface

          The Bitcoin Smile

            Question for the room Press ▶ through the nine expiries. What do you expect from checking g(k) ≥ 0 on the grid k ∈ [−1.5, 1.5]?
            Answer

            On the tested grid, the minima are negative for 2 October (−0.017) and 27 November (−0.009), although the errors of these fits are small (0.23 and 0.12 volatility points). A small fitting error does not guarantee absence of arbitrage. Passing this grid check is not a global guarantee either: for 9 October, g(−2.5) ≈ −0.242 outside the grid. In shape it is a smile, not a skew: both wings rise, because Bitcoin can jump up as well as down. Puts are still slightly dearer than calls at |k| = 0.15: the skew IV(k = +0.15) − IV(k = −0.15) is negative at all 9 expiries, from −0.1 points (10 days) to −2.4 points (66 days). The root mean squared error of the SVI fits is at most 0.70 volatility points.

            What the Market Prices for Bitcoin

              Question for the room Under the risk-neutral measure Q, what is the probability that Bitcoin ends the 38-day maturity below 0.7F, where F is that maturity’s forward price?
              Answer

              The estimated probability is 1.24%, with a 95% bootstrap interval of [1.16%, 1.27%], about 9 times the log-normal value. The 0.14% value comes from the log-normal benchmark with the ATM volatility of 36.4%. The 1.08% value omits the 0.16 percentage points extrapolated below the lowest quoted strike (0.47F). They are part of the estimated probability, but depend on the SVI extrapolation.

              The VIX and the Variance Risk Premium

              The VIX, 1990–2026

                Question for the room How often was the VIX above 30 between 1990 and 2026?
                Answer

                VIX exceeded 30 on 7.94% of the 9,284 days. The 0.81% figure corresponds to the threshold of 50, and 37.01% to the threshold of 20. Exceedance frequency depends on the chosen threshold. The mean is 19.4, and the maximum, 82.7, was reached on 16 March 2020. Spikes are sudden and decay slowly, as in GARCH models.

                Implied vs Realised Volatility, S&P 500

                  Question for the room On what share of days is the VIX above the volatility realised over the next 21 trading days?
                  Answer

                  On approximately 86% of days; 14% corresponds to the opposite event. An unbiased forecast does not automatically imply a 50% exceedance frequency. Over the full sample, the mean premium is 4.09 volatility points (Newey–West standard error 0.27), or 113 %² in variance, with t = 5.5. Negative values occur when subsequent realised volatility exceeds VIX; the largest losses occur during stress episodes: the worst window, starting on 20 February 2020, has −72.0 points. From 2020, the mean volatility difference is 3.82 points, with Newey–West SE 0.90 and t = 4.25. The mean variance difference is 73.90 %², with SE 79.90 %² and t = 0.92.

                  Is the VIX a Good Forecast?

                    Question for the room In the regression of realised variance on VIX², the separate tests do not reject α = 0 and β = 1. What can we conclude from these two results alone?
                    Answer

                    The separate tests do not determine the joint test’s result. Here, the estimates have correlation −0.90 and both deviations from the null are negative (α̂ = −51.9, β̂ − 1 = −0.14). Using this covariance, the Wald test gives χ²₂ = 41.7 and p ≈ 8.9·10⁻¹⁰. The VIX explains R² = 0.37 of the subsequent realised variance, past realised variance only 0.29.

                    Options with Zero Days to Expiry

                    Gamma and Time Decay Near Expiry

                      Question for the room How much larger is the ATM gamma one trading day before expiry than one month before?
                      Answer

                      At S = K = 100, r = 0 and σ = 16%, gamma is 0.396 for τ = 1/252 and 0.086 for τ = 21/252. The ratio is approximately √21 ≈ 4.6: the φ(d₁) factor changes very little here. A 1% move in the last day changes delta by about 0.4; the relation dΔ ≈ Γ·dS is a local approximation. The values 0.167 and 0.030 are −theta per calendar day, evaluated with one and 30 calendar days remaining.

                      Case Studies: Option-Implied Bounds and Volatility Expectations

                      Case Study: the Bound and the Realised Premium

                        Question for the room The realised 12-month excess return is below the Martin bound on 33% of dates. Does this contradict the validity of the bound?
                        Answer

                        No. The bound restricts the conditional mean Eₜ[Rᵉ], not each realised return. D₁ = 0 at all horizons means that the test does not reject validity; it does not prove it. For tightness, the asymptotic tests reject, but the finite-sample p-value at 12 months is 8.3%, so that test does not reject at 5%. Mean slackness at 12 months is approximately 5.18 percentage points a year in the paper, including dividends, and 2.91 for the price index in the figure. The correlation between the bound and the realised return is only 0.15.

                        How the VIX Responds to a Shock to Expected Variance

                          Question for the room Keep the same Cholesky ordering, with expected variance first. Press ▶ to extend the sample from December 2005 to August 2026. How does the sign of the impact response of the VRP change?
                          Answer

                          The point estimate is negative in every sample: −69 %² up to 2005, −254 %² up to 2008. The pointwise 95% band includes zero only for the sample ending in 2006. In the full sample, the response is −169 %², with a band of [−300, −27]: VIX² rises by only 55% of the shock to expected variance. The VRP response turns positive from month 2 and peaks in month 3 (+30 %²). The weights point the same way: φ̂ = 0.49 for VIX², against 0.11 for next month's realised variance. The result is conditional on the Cholesky ordering and the estimated HAR series; it does not by itself establish a trading rule or identify slow-moving expectations.
