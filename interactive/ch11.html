From Random Walk to Brownian Motion

From Random Walk to Brownian Motion: Donsker's Theorem

    Question for the room Is the maximum of a path observed only at daily closes smaller or larger than the maximum of the continuous path?
    Answer

    Smaller, on average. A coarse grid misses the maximum reached between observations: P(max W_n > 1) is 0.220 with n = 5 and 0.316 with n = 2,000, against the Brownian limit 2(1 − Φ(1)) = 0.3173. Donsker’s theorem is a limit result: the gap closes slowly, at rate 1/√n.

    Quadratic Variation and Itô Calculus

    Quadratic Variation: Simulation and Data

      Question for the room What happens to Σ|ΔW|^p as the grid gets finer, for p below 2, equal to 2 and above 2?
      Answer

      The mean of the sum is n^(1−p/2)·E|Z|^p. For p < 2 the sum grows without bound (the total variation, p = 1, reaches 205 with 65,536 intervals), for p = 2 it tends to T = 1 (1.006 on the finest grid), and for p > 2 it tends to zero. Only the exponent 2 gives a non-degenerate limit, hence (dW)² = dt.

      Geometric Brownian Motion

      How Precisely Can We Estimate the Drift?

        Question for the room With 10 years of daily data, how precisely is the drift of the S&P 500 estimated?
        Answer

        The 95% interval has a half-width of about ±11 percentage points (1.96 standard errors; the standard error σ/√T is 5.7 points), however fine the grid: the 2,520 daily returns do not reduce it. On 10-year windows the log drift ranges from −6.0% (2009-03) to 15.8% (2000-09); with this precision the swings cannot be safely attributed to changes in the drift; a precision of ±1 point would need 1,243 years of data.

        Does GBM Fit the S&P 500?

          Question for the room How many days beyond ±4 standard deviations should appear in 9,245 days if returns had the Normal distribution?
          Answer

          About 0.59 = 9,245 · 2(1 − Φ(4)), counting both tails; one tail would give 0.29. The data have 62, and the worst day lies at −11.3 standard deviations; the excess kurtosis is 10.9. The ACF of |r| is 0.27 at lag 1 and 0.14 at lag 50, while the GBM band stays below 0.024.

          Mean Reversion: the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck Process

          Vasicek on the 3-Month Treasury Bill Rate

            Question for the room Does a positive κ̂ over 1954–2026 show that the short rate is mean-reverting?
            Answer

            No. κ̂ = 0.101 (SE 0.051) means a half-life of 6.8 years, and the daily AR(1) coefficient is 0.99960, almost a unit root. Over subperiods κ̂ is −0.034, 0.213 and 0.032, and two of the three intervals include zero. The asymptotic interval over 1954–2026 barely excludes zero (0.101 − 1.96 · 0.051 = 0.001), but near a unit root the distribution of κ̂ is not Normal and is biased upwards; the bootstrap test of the random-walk null gives a p-value of 0.287.

            Jumps: the Merton Model

            Merton Fit to the Return Distribution

              Question for the room The Merton model fitted to the S&P 500 has about 102 jumps a year. Are these crashes?
              Answer

              No. The jumps are small (standard deviation 1.44%) and account for 71% of the variance; the model uses them to mimic changing volatility. The implied excess kurtosis is 3.7 against 10.9 in the data, and the most extreme days stay beyond both curves. The jumps still improve the fit: with λ = 0 the S&P 500 log-likelihood falls from 29,500 to 28,291.

              Models against the Data

              Which Model Reproduces the S&P 500?

                Question for the room Which of the three models reproduces the volatility clustering of the S&P 500?
                Answer

                Only Heston, with the VIX-based parameters: its simulated ACF of |r| follows the data, while GBM and Merton give autocorrelations around zero: Poisson jumps are independent over time, so Merton’s fat tails do not cluster. Merton matches the Hill statistic (2.70 against 2.56) and the skewness (−0.32 against −0.36). Heston, simulated with 390 steps per day, gives a skewness of −0.21. No model reaches the excess kurtosis of the data, 10.88.

                Case Studies: Rough and Persistent Volatility, Drift Bursts

                Case Study: Replication on SPY, Δ = 1 Day

                  Question for the room Fitted on the first 12 lags, can a short-memory OU process reproduce the autocorrelation of SPY log-volatility at lag 100?
                  Answer

                  No. Fitted on the first 12 lags, OU has a half-life of 11 days; 100 days are about 9 half-lives, so the implied autocorrelation at lag 100 is 0.002, against 0.13 in the data. The Cauchy class gives β̂ = 0.26 and β̂* = 0.24, both below 1, consistent with long memory; the variogram gives α̂ = −0.32 (OLS) and −0.35 (NLLS), a path rougher than Brownian motion. With ▶, the fit on 60 lags gives a half-life of 36 days and an OU autocorrelation of 0.149 at lag 100, but the fitted curve gives 0.48 at lag 1 against 0.73 in the data: a single exponential cannot follow both the fast decay at the first lags and the slow tail.

                  Second Case Study (2/6): Drift Bursts in SPY, 5-Minute Bars

                    Question for the room Of the 109 days with max |T_t| > 2.91, how many are drift bursts?
                    Answer

                    The procedure does not answer this. Under the random-sign null, which keeps the absolute returns but removes the drift, jump asymmetry and leverage, 73.84 such days are expected on average (at most 100 in 200 replications). The excess of 35.16 days is descriptive and does not estimate the number of true drift bursts; nor is 5% the share of false alarms among the flagged days: it is the probability that a day without drift exceeds the threshold. The reversal, present on 65% of the 80 days with a full window after the peak, is descriptive too: the peak is chosen in hindsight, and under the null with the same selection the share is 56.5% on average; the slope is only 0.03, and on E-mini S&P 500 trades the paper finds −0.26.
