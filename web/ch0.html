The chapter asks two questions: which financial comparisons can we trust, and how precisely do we know them. Lo (2002) poses the second question for the Sharpe ratio: the means and volatilities of returns are not observed but estimated, so any ranking built from them also carries their estimation errors. The chapter covers market structure, the history of exchanges and of financial models, the course data and the basic measurements: returns, the compound annual growth rate (CAGR), annualised volatility and drawdown, each with its precision.

Over the common window 2 January 2015 – 18 September 2026, the S&P 500 with dividends has a CAGR of 13.7% and an annualised volatility of 17.6%, gold 11.8% and 16.3%, and Bitcoin 60.6% and 66.7%. The Sharpe ratios are 1.05 for Bitcoin, 0.82 for the S&P 500 and 0.76 for gold, each with a standard error (SE) of about 0.29. The standard error of the S&P 500 annualised mean return, estimated from 11.7 years of data, is 5.1 percentage points.

The difference between the Sharpe ratios of the S&P 500 and gold, computed by a bootstrap on common days, is +0.06, with 95% interval [−0.65, +0.88]. The data are compatible both with an advantage of 0.65 for gold and with one of 0.88 for the S&P 500, so the comparison is inconclusive. None of the 10 pairs of non-crypto assets stays significant after a Bonferroni correction. The risk–return chart describes the sample; it does not rank expected returns.

Observation Times and Price Noise (2/2)

Slide 14 in the PDF
  • Different observation times split the same news across two days (Appendix)
    • correlations of assets with different closes are attenuated
  • Bid–ask bounce: trades alternate between bid and ask
    • observed price changes are negatively autocorrelated, even when the efficient price is a random walk
    • the Roll model: consecutive price changes have a negative covariance, \(\operatorname{Cov}(\Delta p_t, \Delta p_{t-1}) = -s^2/4\) (Roll (1984); derivation in the Appendix)
    • \(p_t\): the observed trade price; \(\Delta p_t = p_t - p_{t-1}\): its change; \(s\): the bid–ask spread; a wider spread gives a more negative covariance
  • Spreads, impact and fragmentation add noise to observed prices
    • most visible at high frequency (Chapters 9 and 10)

    Measurement and Precision

    Simple and Log Returns

    Slide 25 in the PDF
    • This section answers
      • how do we measure growth and risk?
      • how precisely do we know the answer?
    • The simple return \(R_t\) and the log return \(r_t\)
      \[ R_t = \frac{P_t}{P_{t-1}} - 1, \qquad r_t = \ln\frac{P_t}{P_{t-1}} = \ln(1+R_t) \]
      • \(P_t\): the closing price (or index level) on day \(t\)
    • Simple returns aggregate across assets: a portfolio with weights \(w_i\) has \(R_{p,t} = \sum_i w_i R_{i,t}\)
    • Log returns aggregate over time: the \(h\)-day log return is the sum \(r_{t+1} + \dots + r_{t+h}\)
    • For small moves the two nearly coincide, \(r_t = R_t - R_t^2/2 + \dots\)
      • for a 50% fall they differ a lot: \(\ln 0.5 = -69\%\)

    Compounding and the CAGR

    Slide 26 in the PDF
    • A price index (S&P 500, BET) ignores dividends
      • BET: the main index of the Bucharest Stock Exchange
      • the total-return index (BET-TR) and the adjusted close reinvest them
    • Compounding: daily simple returns multiply, log returns add
      \[ P_T = P_0 \prod_{t=1}^{T} (1 + R_t) = P_0\, e^{\,r_1 + \dots + r_T} \]
    • CAGR: the constant yearly rate that turns the first price into the last one
      \[ \mathrm{CAGR} = \Big(\frac{P_{\text{end}}}{P_{\text{start}}}\Big)^{1/Y} - 1, \qquad \ln(1 + \mathrm{CAGR}) = \frac{1}{Y}\sum_{t=1}^{T} r_t \]
      • CAGR: compound annual growth rate
      • \(Y\): the sample length in years, calendar days\(/365.25\); the ratio \(P_{\text{end}}/P_{\text{start}}\) is the multiple
      • the second form: the CAGR is the sum of log returns per year, expressed as a simple rate
    • Example: S&P 500 (price index), 2 Jan 2015 – 18 Sep 2026
      • \(Y = 4\,277/365.25 = 11.71\)
      • the multiple \(\times 3.72\) gives CAGR \(= 3.72^{1/11.71} - 1 = 11.9\%\)

    Annualised Volatility

    Slide 27 in the PDF
    • The subscript \(d\) marks a daily quantity, the subscript \(A\) an annualised one
      • \(A\) is also the number of returns per year actually observed
    • Annualised volatility: \(\hat\sigma_d\) scaled by \(\sqrt{A}\)
      \[ \hat\sigma_A = \hat\sigma_d\sqrt{A} \]
      • \(\hat\sigma_d\): the sample standard deviation of daily log returns
      • why \(\sqrt{A}\): the one-year log return is the sum of \(A\) daily returns; if these are uncorrelated with constant variance, the variance of the sum is \(A\sigma_d^2\)
      • with autocorrelated daily returns the scaling is no longer exact (variance ratio, Appendix)
    • Example: SPY has \(T = 2\,944\) daily returns over \(Y = 11.71\) years, so \(A = T/Y = 251.4\)
      • SPY: the SPDR S&P 500 ETF, with dividends

    Arithmetic Mean, CAGR and Volatility Drag

    Slide 28 in the PDF
    • The annualised arithmetic mean \(\hat\mu_A = A\,\bar R\) exceeds compound growth
      \[ \ln(1 + \mathrm{CAGR}) \approx \hat\mu_A - \hat\sigma_A^2/2 \]
      • \(\bar R\): the sample mean of daily simple returns
      • the reason: \(r_t \approx R_t - R_t^2/2\), and the mean of \(R_t^2\) is about \(\sigma_d^2\)
      • volatility drag: at the same arithmetic mean, higher volatility lowers compound growth
    • SPY, 2015–2026: \(\hat\mu_A = 14.39\%\) and \(\hat\sigma_A = 17.53\%\) (from simple returns)
      • so \(14.39\% - 17.53\%^2/2 = 12.85\%\)
    • SPY's CAGR is 13.7%, and \(\ln(1.137) = 12.84\%\)
      • the approximation reproduces the observed compound growth
    • The price index has a CAGR of 11.9%: a gap of 1.8 percentage points (pp) a year
      • the gap comes mainly from reinvested dividends, net of the ETF's fees and tracking differences

    Drawdown and Maximum Drawdown

    Slide 29 in the PDF
    • Volatility does not say how much an investor lost from the best earlier moment
      • the order of returns matters
    • Drawdown: the percentage distance below the running peak
      \[ DD_t = \frac{P_t}{\max_{s \le t} P_s} - 1 \le 0, \qquad \mathrm{MDD} = \min_t DD_t \]
      • MDD (maximum drawdown): the deepest fall from a peak over the whole sample
      • duration of a drawdown: the days until the price exceeds the earlier peak again
    • Example (Seminar 0, A4): prices 100, 120, 90, 110, 130, 100
      • the running peak 100, 120, 120, 120, 130, 130 gives \(DD_t\): 0, 0, \(-25\%\), \(-8.3\%\), 0, \(-23.1\%\)
      • MDD \(= -25\%\), although the last price is above the first

    How Precisely Do We Know an Expected Return? (1/2)

    Slide 30 in the PDF
    • Assumption: the daily simple returns \(R_t\) are iid
      • true mean \(\mu_d\) and standard deviation \(\sigma_d\)
      • \(T = AY\) observations
    • Estimator: \(\hat\mu_A = A\,\bar R\), with \(\bar R = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T} R_t\)
    • Standard error (SE): the typical distance between \(\hat\mu_A\) and the true annual mean
      \[ \operatorname{SE}(\hat\mu_A) =\frac{A\,\sigma_d}{\sqrt{T}} = \frac{\sigma_A}{\sqrt{Y}}, \qquad \sigma_A = \sigma_d\sqrt{A} \]
      • the SE falls only with the square root of the number of years
      • 95% confidence interval: \(\hat\mu_A \pm 1.96\,\operatorname{SE}\)

    How Precisely Do We Know an Expected Return? (2/2)

    Slide 31 in the PDF
    • SPY, 2015–2026: \(\hat\mu_A = 14.4\%\), \(\sigma_A = 17.5\%\), \(Y = 11.71\)
      • \(\sigma_A\) from simple returns; 17.6% from log returns
      • \(\operatorname{SE} = 17.5\%/\sqrt{11.71} = 5.1\) pp
      • 95% confidence interval \([4.3\%,\ 24.4\%]\)
    • Question for the room: to halve this SE, which would you rather have?
      • four times as many observations (hourly instead of daily data)
      • or four times as many years
    • Answer: four times as many years (about 47 instead of 11.7)
      • the SE depends only on the span \(Y\), not on the sampling frequency (Merton (1980))

    The Sharpe Ratio Is an Estimate

    Slide 32 in the PDF
    • Sharpe ratio (SR): the mean return per unit of risk, with the risk-free rate set to 0
      • per day \(\mathrm{SR}_d = \mu_d/\sigma_d\) (mean and standard deviation, SD, of daily simple returns); annualised \(\mathrm{SR}_A = \sqrt{A}\,\mathrm{SR}_d\)
      • \(\widehat{\mathrm{SR}}_A\): the value estimated from the sample
    • Its standard error, for iid returns following the Normal distribution (Lo (2002)):
      \[ \operatorname{SE}(\widehat{\mathrm{SR}}_A) = \sqrt{\frac{1 + \mathrm{SR}_A^2/(2A)}{Y}} \approx \frac{1}{\sqrt{Y}} \]
      • with daily data the term \(\mathrm{SR}_A^2/(2A)\) is tiny (\(0.82^2/504 \approx 0.001\)), so the SE depends almost only on the number of years \(Y\)

                        Slide 77 in the PDF

                        Case Study: Where Eq. (5) and the Pass-Through Come From

                        Slide 81 in the PDF
                        • Assumptions behind eq. (5) (Section V.A)
                          • every active investor has the same own elasticity \(\bar{\mathcal{E}}\)
                          • those who turn passive are representative of active investors
                        • Step 1: put eq. (4) into the holdings-weighted average (eq. 2)
                          \[ \mathcal{E}_{agg} = \alpha\,(\bar{\mathcal{E}} - \chi\,\mathcal{E}_{agg}) \quad\Rightarrow\quad \mathcal{E}_{agg} = \frac{\alpha\,\bar{\mathcal{E}}}{1 + \chi\alpha} \]
                          • active holdings have weight \(\alpha\), passive ones contribute 0
                        • Step 2: divide by the all-active value \(\bar{\mathcal{E}}/(1+\chi)\), at \(\alpha = 1\)
                          \[ \frac{\alpha(1+\chi)}{1+\chi\alpha} = \alpha + (1-\alpha)\,\frac{\chi\alpha}{1+\chi\alpha} \]
                          • the result is eq. (5) written as one fraction
                        • Step 3: the elasticity of \(\mathcal{E}_{agg}\) with respect to \(\alpha\) is the pass-through of eq. (28)
                          \[ \frac{d\ln \mathcal{E}_{agg}}{d\ln \alpha} = 1 - \frac{\chi\alpha}{1+\chi\alpha} = \frac{1}{1+\chi\alpha} \]
                        • Example, \(\chi = 1\) and \(\alpha = 0.5\)
                          • eq. (5) gives \(0.5 \times 2/1.5 = 0.5 + 0.5 \times 0.5/1.5 = 0.67\)
                          • the pass-through is \(1/1.5 = 0.67\), so a 1% fall in \(\alpha\) lowers \(\mathcal{E}_{agg}\) by about 0.67%
                        • If the most elastic investors turn passive first, the representativeness assumption fails (Seminar 0, C3)

                        The Roll Model of Bid–Ask Bounce

                        Slide 89 in the PDF
                        • Model: \(p_t = m_t + (s/2)\,q_t\)
                          • \(m_t\): the efficient price (a random walk); \(s\): a constant spread in price units
                          • \(q_t = \pm 1\): independent, equally likely trade signs, unrelated to \(\Delta m_t\)
                        • Then \(\Delta p_t = \Delta m_t + (s/2)(q_t - q_{t-1})\) and only the shared \(q_{t-1}\) links consecutive changes:
                          \[ \operatorname{Cov}(\Delta p_t, \Delta p_{t-1}) = -\frac{s^2}{4}\operatorname{Var}(q_{t-1}) = -\frac{s^2}{4}, \qquad \hat s = 2\sqrt{-\hat\gamma_1} \]

                          • \(\hat s\) solves \(\gamma_1 = -s^2/4\)
                        • Limits (Roll (1984); Chapter 10)
                          • if \(\hat\gamma_1 > 0\) the estimator is undefined (\(\hat\gamma_1 = 0\) gives \(\hat s = 0\))
                          • other dependence also moves \(\hat\gamma_1\): not every negative autocovariance is bounce

                          • the short rate reverts to the level \(\theta\) but can become negative
                        • CIR (Cox, Ingersoll & Ross (1985)): \(dr_t = \kappa(\theta - r_t)\,dt + \sigma\sqrt{r_t}\,dW_t\)
                          • the rate's volatility shrinks as the rate approaches zero, so the rate does not become negative
                        • HJM (Heath, Jarrow & Morton (1992)): models the whole forward-rate curve directly
                          • no arbitrage fixes each rate's drift as a function of its volatilities
                        • Notation
                          • \(r_t\): the short rate at time \(t\) (here a rate, not a log return); \(dr_t\): its change over a short interval \(dt\)
                          • \(\kappa > 0\): the speed of mean reversion; \(\theta\): the long-run level
                          • \(\sigma\): the volatility of the rate; \(dW_t\): the increment of a Brownian motion, Normal with mean 0 and variance \(dt\)
                          • the drift \(\kappa(\theta - r_t)\) is positive when \(r_t < \theta\) and negative when \(r_t > \theta\), so the rate is pulled towards \(\theta\)

                        Volatility Models

                        Slide 98 in the PDF
                        Robert F. Engle, 2022 Photo: Jon Demske (2022); CC BY-SA 4.0; Wikimedia Commons
                        • \(r_t\): daily return (demeaned), \(\sigma_t^2\): its variance conditional on the past
                        • ARCH (Engle (1982)): \(\sigma_t^2 = \omega + \alpha r_{t-1}^2\)
                          • today's variance depends on yesterday's squared shock
                        • GARCH (Bollerslev (1986)): \(\sigma_t^2 = \omega + \alpha r_{t-1}^2 + \beta\sigma_{t-1}^2\) adds the lagged variance
                          • \(\omega > 0\): a constant; \(\alpha \ge 0\): the reaction to yesterday's shock; \(\beta \ge 0\): the persistence of variance
                          • \(\alpha + \beta < 1\): the variance reverts to its long-run level \(\omega/(1 - \alpha - \beta)\)
                        • RiskMetrics (J.P. Morgan, 1994; J.P. Morgan/Reuters (1996)): \(\sigma_t^2 = \lambda\sigma_{t-1}^2 + (1-\lambda)r_{t-1}^2\)
                          • an EWMA (exponentially weighted moving average) of squared returns; \(\lambda = 0.94\) for daily data
                          • the decay factor \(\lambda \in (0,1)\): a larger \(\lambda\) means a longer memory
                          • the return \(k\) days back gets weight \((1-\lambda)\lambda^{k-1}\): a GARCH(1,1) with \(\omega = 0\) and \(\alpha + \beta = 1\)

                        Risk Measurement and Regulation (1/2)

                        Slide 99 in the PDF

                        Classical Monte Carlo versus Quantum Amplitude Estimation (1/2)

                        Slide 106 in the PDF
                        • Target: \(\mu = \E[f(X)]\), the mean discounted payoff \(f\) of a random market scenario \(X\)

                          • \(|0\rangle\), \(|1\rangle\): the states of the objective qubit; \(|\psi_0\rangle\), \(|\psi_1\rangle\): the register states paired with them
                        • A Grover-type operator applied \(m\) times makes the error fall as \(O(1/m)\)
                          • \(O(1/\varepsilon)\) calls of the pricing circuit (Montanaro (2015); Rebentrost, Gupt & Bromley (2018))

                        \(\sqrt{h}\) Scaling and the Variance Ratio

                        Slide 111 in the PDF
                        • Variance of an \(h\)-day return (the sum of \(h\) daily returns \(r_{t+j}\)), for stationary returns with variance \(\sigma^2\):
                          \[ \operatorname{Var}\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{h} r_{t+j}\Big) = h\sigma^2\Big[1 + 2\sum_{j=1}^{h-1}\Big(1 - \frac{j}{h}\Big)\rho_j\Big] \]
                          • \(\rho_j\): the correlation of returns \(j\) days apart; the weight \(1 - j/h\): the share of such pairs
                          • \(\rho_j > 0\) raises the \(h\)-day variance above \(h\sigma^2\), \(\rho_j < 0\) lowers it
                        • The \(\sqrt{h}\) rule is exact when returns are uncorrelated with constant variance
                          • independence is not needed
                        • Variance ratio \(\mathrm{VR}(h) = \operatorname{Var}(h\text{-day return})/(h\sigma^2)\)
                          • 1 for uncorrelated returns, below 1 under mean reversion, above 1 under momentum
                          • for an AR(1) with lag-1 autocorrelation \(\rho\), \(\mathrm{VR}(h) \to (1+\rho)/(1-\rho)\) as \(h\) grows
                        • S&P 500, 2000–2026: \(\hat\rho_1 = -0.10\) and \(\widehat{\mathrm{VR}}(20) = 0.75\)
                          • scaling daily volatility by \(\sqrt{20}\) overstates the 20-day (monthly) risk
                          • use the whole autocorrelation function or aggregate the returns directly (tests in Chapter 2)

                        The Sharpe Ratio, Delta Method, Fat Tails, Dependence and Selection (1/2)

                        Slide 113 in the PDF
                        • Delta method: if \(\sqrt{T}(\hat\theta - \theta) \xrightarrow{d} N(0, \sigma^2)\) and \(g\) is differentiable
                          • then \(\sqrt{T}\,\big(g(\hat\theta) - g(\theta)\big) \xrightarrow{d} N\big(0, g'(\theta)^2\sigma^2\big)\)
                          • \(\theta\): a parameter, \(\hat\theta\): its estimator from \(T\) observations; \(\xrightarrow{d}\): convergence in distribution as \(T \to \infty\)
                          • a first-order Taylor expansion, \(g(\hat\theta) \approx g(\theta) + g'(\theta)(\hat\theta - \theta)\), carries the standard error from \(\hat\theta\) to \(g(\hat\theta)\)
                        • Applied to \(\mathrm{SR}_d = \mu_d/\sigma_d\), for iid returns following the Normal distribution (Lo (2002)):
                          \[ \sqrt{T}\,(\widehat{\mathrm{SR}}_d - \mathrm{SR}_d) \xrightarrow{d} N\big(0,\ 1 + \mathrm{SR}_d^2/2\big) \quad\Rightarrow\quad \operatorname{SE}(\widehat{\mathrm{SR}}_A) = \sqrt{\frac{1 + \mathrm{SR}_A^2/(2A)}{Y}} \approx \frac{1}{\sqrt{Y}} \]
                          • the 1 comes from estimating the mean, \(\mathrm{SR}_d^2/2\) from estimating the standard deviation

                        Case Study, Demand System and Estimation (2/2)

                        Slide 136 in the PDF
                        • \(\xi\), \(\zeta\): loadings on \(\mathcal{E}_{agg,k}\) and on its interaction with \(X_k\)

                        Mini-Case, the HAC Standard Error

                        Slide 141 in the PDF
                        • Daily spreads \(d_t\) are serially correlated and their variance changes over time
                          • so the iid formula \(s_d/\sqrt{T}\) misstates \(\operatorname{SE}(\bar d)\)
                        • The Newey–West (HAC) estimate adds the autocovariances up to lag \(L\)
                          \[ \widehat{\operatorname{Var}}(\bar d) = \frac{1}{T}\Big[\hat\gamma_0 + 2\sum_{l=1}^{L}\Big(1 - \frac{l}{L+1}\Big)\hat\gamma_l\Big] \]
                          • with weights that decline linearly
                          • \(\hat\gamma_l\): the sample autocovariance of \(d_t\) at lag \(l\) (\(\hat\gamma_0\): its variance); \(T\): the number of daily spreads
                          • the declining weights keep the estimate non-negative; \(L = 0\) gives back the iid variance
                          • positive autocorrelation raises the SE, negative autocorrelation lowers it
                        • The lag \(L\) is stated in the pre-registration
                          • the common rule \(L = \lfloor 4(T/100)^{2/9} \rfloor\) gives \(L = 9\) for \(T = 5\,884\)
                          • report also the results with \(L/2\) and \(2L\)
