Harvey, Liu & Zhu (2016) show that, once hundreds of factors have been tried on the same data, the usual hurdle \(t > 2\) no longer separates discoveries from the luck of selection: a new factor needs \(t > 3\). Machine learning brings flexible, nonlinear functional forms and hundreds of candidate predictors to finance, and with them the same problem on a larger scale. Financial data have a very low signal-to-noise ratio and a single, serially dependent history. On daily S&P 500 returns over 2000–2026, the autocorrelations of returns are small, the lag-1 autocorrelation is \(-0.099\), while the autocorrelations of absolute returns are large and persistent: risk has predictable structure, direction hardly any.

The chapter's case study asks the question directly: can the 5-day direction of the S&P 500 be predicted? On 6,269 daily observations, four models (a penalised logit, a random forest, gradient boosting and a neural network) are validated on the same 5 folds, with purging and embargo. No model beats the "always up" benchmark, whose accuracy is 58.1%. The logit reaches 57.7% accuracy because it predicts "up" on 98.3% of days. In the 2010–2026 walk-forward backtest, the GBM strategy has a Sharpe ratio of 0.33 and buy-and-hold 0.66.

Most false results come from the process, not the algorithm. On a target with nothing to predict, K-fold with shuffled observations reports 69.4% accuracy, purged K-fold with embargo 49.6%. On a grid of 1,284 Bitcoin trading rules, the selected rule has a Sharpe ratio of 2.02 in-sample and 0.03 out-of-sample. The chapter therefore builds, step by step, the features, labels, validation and tests that separate a real prediction from the best of many trials.

What Can Machine Learning Predict in Finance?

Machine learning (ML) estimates a prediction function from data with few restrictions on its form. The chapter applies it to financial returns, where the signal is weak and the history is short and serially dependent. Two failures of the research process dominate in this setting (López de Prado, 2018). Look-ahead bias is the use of information that was not available at the decision date. Selection bias is the reporting of the best of many trials as if it were the only one. The first section separates what is predictable in a return series from what is not, and states what a small predictive gain is worth.

Features are computed from the bars with information available at the decision date only, and some of them keep part of the memory of the price level. Labels define what the model learns: the triple-barrier label and the meta-label are two alternatives to the plain sign of a future return. The second row of the diagram is where most errors are caught or missed. Validation uses purged cross-validation (CV) with an embargo, which keeps the training and test sets from sharing information. Each stage answers a narrow question. Features must use only information available at the decision date. Validation measures what still holds on observations the model has not seen. Inference decides whether a difference from a benchmark is larger than sampling error and the cost of trading.

Why Machine Learning in Finance, and Why It Is Hard

Slide 4 in the PDF

The promise of ML in finance has three parts:

  1. Flexible, non-linear functional forms find interactions between predictors without the analyst specifying them.
  2. Hundreds of candidate predictors are available, the "factor zoo" of Chapter 3.
  3. Alternative data, such as text, images, order books and on-chain data, add further predictors.

The constraints are as strong. The signal-to-noise ratio of returns is very low, and there is a single, serially dependent history that cannot be re-run. Markets are non-stationary: regimes change, structural breaks occur and prices react to the behaviour of market participants. Labels are not given but must be constructed, and errors cost real money. Together these features create a large risk of overfitting, that is, of fitting noise.

The Adaptive Markets Hypothesis of Lo (2004) describes predictability as transient: it appears, is arbitraged away and re-appears. A model that worked yesterday is therefore a hypothesis, not a law; market efficiency is the subject of Chapter 2. The hard part of the work is not fitting a model but not fooling oneself, which requires correct labels, correct validation and honest backtests.

What Is Predictable? Direction vs. Risk (1/2)

Slide 5 in the PDF

Which part of a financial series is predictable can be seen first in its autocorrelations. The autocorrelation at lag \(k\) is

\[\rho_k = \mathrm{Corr}(x_t, x_{t-k}),\]
(13.1)

computed here for the daily log return \(r_t = \log P_t - \log P_{t-1}\) and for its absolute value \(|r_t|\), where \(P_t\) is the closing price on day \(t\). Under the hypothesis of no autocorrelation, each estimate \(\hat\rho_k\) lies within \(\pm 1.96/\sqrt{T}\) with probability 95%, where \(T\) is the number of observations. For \(|r_t|\) the band requires a finite fourth moment, which Chapter 1 questions.

On the S&P 500, the autocorrelations of returns are smaller than those of absolute returns. Even so, 13 of the 40 return autocorrelations lie outside the band, and at lag 1 the estimate is \(\hat\rho_1 = -0.099\). Three qualifications apply. The band is not simultaneous over 40 lags; it is not robust to heteroskedasticity; and a coefficient outside the band does not show that a trading rule is profitable after costs.

The autocorrelations of \(|r_t|\) are large and persistent. The magnitude of returns, that is, risk, has predictable structure, while their direction has very little. ML therefore has its best chance where such structure exists: volatility, risk, liquidity and the cross-sectional ranking of assets.

    Evidence, and the Economic Value of a Small \(R^2_{OOS}\)

    Slide 7 in the PDF

    The broadest comparison of ML methods for returns is that of Gu, Kelly & Xiu (2020). They use monthly returns of individual US stocks over 1957–2016 and compare linear models, penalised regressions, principal component regression (PCR), partial least squares (PLS), random forests, gradient boosting and neural networks. Their measure of fit is the out-of-sample (OOS) \(R^2\),

    \[R^2_{OOS} = 1 - \frac{\sum_t (r_t - \hat r_t)^2}{\sum_t r_t^2},\]
    (13.2)

    where \(r_t\) is the realised return in month \(t\) of the test period and \(\hat r_t\) the forecast made with information up to \(t-1\). The denominator in (13.2) is the squared error of the zero forecast \(\hat r_t = 0\): \(R^2_{OOS} > 0\) when the model beats it and \(R^2_{OOS} < 0\) when it does worse.

    The monthly values in their Table 1 are small. A three-layer network reaches 0.40%, gradient boosting 0.34% and a random forest 0.33%. Ordinary least squares (OLS) with 3 predictors reaches 0.16%, while OLS with all 900+ predictors gives −3.46%. Trees and networks win through non-linear interactions, and the dominant predictors are momentum, liquidity and volatility.

    The benchmark matters as much as the model. Welch & Goyal (2008) show that most published predictors lose to the historical mean. Gu, Kelly & Xiu (2020) and Chapter 14 use a zero forecast. What a monthly \(R^2\) of a fraction of a percent is worth to an investor is derived in Further Topics, following Campbell & Thompson (2008).

    Section summary

    Returns have almost no predictable direction, while their magnitude is strongly persistent. Out-of-sample gains for return forecasts are measured in tenths of a percent of \(R^2\), and their size depends on the benchmark, the zero forecast or the historical mean. The next section introduces the models that produce such forecasts and the penalties that keep their variance under control.

    Machine Learning Methods

    The toolbox has three parts:

    1. a loss that measures the error of a prediction;
    2. a penalty that limits the complexity of the fitted function;
    3. a validation scheme that chooses the strength of the penalty.

    Five model families fill it: penalised linear models, single trees, random forests, gradient boosting and neural networks. With a weak signal, controlling the variance of the estimate matters more than adding flexibility, and the bias–variance decomposition makes this precise.

    Loss, Penalty and Five Model Families (1/5)

    Slide 8 in the PDF

    Supervised learning chooses, within a model family, the function that fits the training data best after a penalty for complexity:

    \[\hat f = \arg\min_{f \in \mathcal{F}} \; \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} L\big(y_i, f(\mathbf{x}_i)\big) + \lambda\,\Omega(f).\]
    (13.3)

    Here \((y_i, \mathbf{x}_i)\), \(i = 1,\dots,n\), are the target and the feature vector of observation \(i\), and \(\mathcal{F}\) is the model family, for example all trees of depth 3. The loss \(L\) is the squared error \((y - f)^2\) or, for a probability \(f\) that \(y = 1\), the log-loss \(-[y\log f + (1-y)\log(1-f)]\). The term \(\Omega(f)\) penalises complexity with weight \(\lambda \ge 0\): \(\lambda = 0\) means no penalty, and a large \(\lambda\) gives a simpler model.

    The weight \(\lambda\) cannot be chosen by (13.3) itself, since the training loss always prefers \(\lambda = 0\). It is chosen by \(K\)-fold cross-validation on folds \(\mathcal{P}_1, \dots, \mathcal{P}_K\):

    \[CV(\lambda) = \frac1K\sum_{k=1}^{K} \frac{1}{n_k}\sum_{i \in \mathcal{P}_k} L\big(y_i, \hat f^{(-k)}_\lambda(\mathbf{x}_i)\big), \qquad \hat\lambda = \arg\min_\lambda CV(\lambda),\]
    (13.4)

    where \(n_k\) is the number of observations in fold \(k\) and \(\hat f^{(-k)}_\lambda\) the model fitted on the other \(K-1\) folds. Each observation is predicted by a model that has not seen it. Whether this is enough for financial series, whose neighbouring observations share information, is the subject of the section on honest validation.

    Loss, Penalty and Five Model Families (2/5)

    Slide 9 in the PDF

    The reason for penalising is the decomposition of the expected test error, derived in Chapter 7 of Hastie, Tibshirani & Friedman (2009). Let \(y = f(\mathbf{x}) + \varepsilon\) with \(\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon] = 0\) and \(\mathrm{Var}(\varepsilon) = \sigma^2\). At a point \(\mathbf{x}_0\),

    \[\mathbb{E}\big[(y_0 - \hat f(\mathbf{x}_0))^2\big] = \sigma^2 + \big(\mathbb{E}[\hat f(\mathbf{x}_0)] - f(\mathbf{x}_0)\big)^2 + \mathrm{Var}\big(\hat f(\mathbf{x}_0)\big),\]
    (13.5)

    where the expectation is over possible training samples and over the noise of the new observation \(y_0 = f(\mathbf{x}_0) + \varepsilon_0\). The three terms are the noise, the squared bias and the variance. The bias is the systematic error of a family that is too simple. The variance is the sensitivity of \(\hat f\) to the training sample, and it grows with the flexibility of the model. The noise \(\sigma^2\) is the variance of the unpredictable part of \(y\), which no model can reduce.

    The derivation adds and subtracts \(\mathbb{E}[\hat f(\mathbf{x}_0)]\) in \(y_0 - \hat f(\mathbf{x}_0)\) and expands the square. The cross terms have mean zero because \(\varepsilon_0\) is independent of the training sample.

    In finance, return forecasts reduce the error very little. An out-of-sample \(R^2\) below 1% means that the model removes less than 1% of the squared loss of the benchmark. This does not identify \(\sigma^2\), because the remaining error also contains misspecification, omitted variables and estimation error. It does explain the design of the models that follow: penalties and the averaging of trees reduce variance at the price of a small bias.

      \[ \E\big[(y_0 - \hat f(\mathbf{x}_0))^2\big] = \underbrace{\sigma^2}_{\text{noise}} + \underbrace{\big(\E[\hat f(\mathbf{x}_0)] - f(\mathbf{x}_0)\big)^2}_{\text{bias}^2} + \underbrace{\Var\big(\hat f(\mathbf{x}_0)\big)}_{\text{variance}} \]

    Loss, Penalty and Five Model Families (3/5)

    Slide 10 in the PDF

    Penalised regressions apply (13.3) to a linear model with \(p\) standardised features and coefficients \(\boldsymbol\beta = (\beta_1, \dots, \beta_p)\):

    \[\hat{\boldsymbol\beta} = \arg\min_{\boldsymbol\beta}\ \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^{n} \big(y_i - \mathbf{x}_i^\top\boldsymbol\beta\big)^2 + \lambda\Big[\alpha\|\boldsymbol\beta\|_1 + \tfrac{1-\alpha}{2}\|\boldsymbol\beta\|_2^2\Big],\]
    (13.6)

    with \(\|\boldsymbol\beta\|_1 = \sum_j |\beta_j|\), \(\|\boldsymbol\beta\|_2^2 = \sum_j \beta_j^2\) and \(\alpha \in [0,1]\) the share of the absolute-value penalty. Ridge regression, \(\alpha = 0\) (Hoerl & Kennard, 1970), has the closed form \(\hat{\boldsymbol\beta} = (\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X} + \tfrac{n\lambda}{2}\mathbf{I})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{y}\). It shrinks all coefficients, none exactly to zero, and stabilises the estimate when predictors are correlated.

    The LASSO, \(\alpha = 1\) (Tibshirani, 1996), uses the penalty \(|\beta_j|\), which has a kink at zero. As a result some coefficients become exactly zero, and the LASSO selects features. The elastic net, \(0 < \alpha < 1\) (Zou & Hastie, 2005), keeps groups of correlated predictors together, whereas the LASSO arbitrarily keeps one of them.

    The chapter's linear benchmark is the penalised logit. It models the probability \(p = P(y = 1 \mid \mathbf{x}) = 1/(1+e^{-\mathbf{x}^\top\boldsymbol\beta})\) and replaces the squared loss in (13.6) by the log-loss.

    A classification tree splits the feature space recursively. At each node \(v\) it chooses the feature \(j\) and the threshold \(s\) that reduce the Gini impurity \(G = 1 - \sum_c \hat\pi_c^2\) the most:

    \[(j^*, s^*) = \arg\min_{j,\,s}\ \Big[\frac{n_L}{n_v}\,G(L) + \frac{n_R}{n_v}\,G(R)\Big], \qquad L = \{x_j \le s\},\ R = \{x_j > s\}.\]
    (13.7)

    Here \(\hat\pi_c\) is the share of class \(c\) in the node, and \(n_v\), \(n_L\), \(n_R\) are the numbers of observations in the node and in its two children. A pure node has \(G = 0\). With two classes \(G = 2\hat\pi(1-\hat\pi)\), which reaches its maximum of 0.5 at a 50/50 split. A leaf predicts the share of class 1 among its observations.

    A random forest (RF) (Breiman, 2001) averages \(B\) trees, \(\hat f_{RF}(\mathbf{x}) = \frac1B\sum_{b=1}^{B} T_b(\mathbf{x})\). Each tree \(T_b\) is grown on a bootstrap sample, with a random subset of features considered at each split. If one tree has variance \(\sigma_T^2\) and two trees have correlation \(\rho\), Chapter 15 of Hastie, Tibshirani & Friedman (2009) gives

    \[\mathrm{Var}\big(\hat f_{RF}(\mathbf{x})\big) = \rho\,\sigma_T^2 + \frac{1-\rho}{B}\,\sigma_T^2.\]
    (13.8)

    More trees remove the second term of (13.8) but not the first. The random feature subsets lower \(\rho\), the only term that more trees cannot remove. The out-of-bag error of the forest, computed on the observations left out of each bootstrap sample, is optimistic when labels overlap.

    The gradient boosting machine (GBM) (Friedman, 2001) builds the model step by step, adding at each step a small tree that corrects the errors of the current model:

    \[r_{im} = -\frac{\partial L(y_i, F)}{\partial F}\bigg|_{F = F_{m-1}(\mathbf{x}_i)}, \qquad F_m(\mathbf{x}) = F_{m-1}(\mathbf{x}) + \eta\, h_m(\mathbf{x}).\]
    (13.9)

    \(F_m\) is the model after \(m\) steps, \(h_m\) the tree fitted to the pseudo-residuals \(r_{im}\) and \(\eta \in (0, 1]\) the learning rate. For the log-loss on the logit score, the pseudo-residual is \(r_{im} = y_i - \hat p_{m-1}(\mathbf{x}_i)\), the difference between the outcome and the current predicted probability. A small \(\eta\) learns slowly and overfits less.

    The multilayer perceptron (MLP) stacks hidden layers \(\mathbf{h}^{(\ell)} = g(\mathbf{W}^{(\ell)}\mathbf{h}^{(\ell-1)} + \mathbf{b}^{(\ell)})\), starting from \(\mathbf{h}^{(0)} = \mathbf{x}\), and ends with the output \(\hat p = 1/(1 + e^{-(\mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{h}^{(D)} + b)})\). \(\mathbf{W}^{(\ell)}\) and \(\mathbf{b}^{(\ell)}\) are the weights and biases of layer \(\ell\), \(D\) is the number of hidden layers, and \(g(z) = \max(0, z)\) is the rectified linear unit (ReLU), applied element by element. The weights minimise the log-loss by gradient descent.

    The MLP is regularised by early stopping. A chronological validation set, the last 10% of the training data, purged, is held out, and training stops when the validation log-loss stops falling. Richer architectures are treated in Chapter 14.

      Features: Fractional Differencing and Its Limitations

      A feature should be stationary, so that a relation learnt on the past can hold in the future, yet differencing a price into returns erases the memory of its level. Fractional differencing, proposed for this purpose by López de Prado (2018), differences by less than one. This section defines the fixed-width window version, applies the proposed rule for choosing the order \(d\) to the S&P 500 and shows why the result is still a non-stationary series.

      Stationarity, Memory and the Fractional Difference (1/2)

      Slide 15 in the PDF

      Log prices \(X_t = \log P_t\), with \(P_t\) the closing price on day \(t\), behave as \(I(1)\) series. Returns \(\Delta\log P_t = \log P_t - \log P_{t-1}\) are \(I(0)\), but they erase the memory of the price level. The question is how to turn a price into a stationary feature without erasing that memory.

      The integration order is defined with the backshift operator \(B\), \(BX_t = X_{t-1}\). A series \(X_t\) is \(I(\delta)\) if \((1-B)^{\delta}X_t\) is \(I(0)\), that is, stationary with short memory. An \(I(1)\) series is of the random-walk type and becomes stationary after one difference. The order \(\delta\) may be fractional: the series is then stationary only for \(\delta < \tfrac12\), with long memory when \(0 < \delta < \tfrac12\) (Hosking, 1981).

      Fractional differencing applies \((1-B)^d\) with a non-integer \(d\), differencing by less than 1. The aim is to remove the unit root while keeping part of the memory of the level, which ordinary returns, \(d = 1\), discard.

      Stationarity, Memory and the Fractional Difference (2/2)

      Slide 16 in the PDF

      The fractional difference of order \(d\) expands \((1-B)^d\) as a binomial series:

      \[\tilde X_t = (1-B)^d X_t = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} w_k X_{t-k}, \qquad w_0 = 1,\quad w_k = -\,w_{k-1}\,\frac{d-k+1}{k}.\]
      (13.10)

      Here \(d\) is the differencing order chosen by the analyst, \(k\) the lag in days and \(w_k\) the weight of the value \(k\) days back. For \(d = 1\) the weights are \((1, -1, 0, \dots)\), ordinary returns. For \(0 < d < 1\) the weights decay hyperbolically, so distant values keep a non-zero weight and memory is kept.

      An \(I(1)\) log price differenced with order \(d\) is \(I(1-d)\). It is therefore still non-stationary for \(d \le \tfrac12\), and stationary with long memory for \(\tfrac12 < d < 1\). The infinite weights in (13.10) sum to \((1-1)^d = 0\), so the level cancels, but only in the limit; the asymptotics of the weights are in the Appendix.

      López de Prado (2018) proposes the minimum \(d\) at which an Augmented Dickey–Fuller (ADF) unit-root test rejects. The test equation and its critical value come two slides later, and the rest of the section shows why this rule fails.

      The Fixed-Width Window (FFD)

      Slide 17 in the PDF

      The infinite sum in (13.10) cannot be computed on a finite sample. Fixed-width window fractional differentiation (FFD) truncates the weights (López de Prado, 2018): it keeps \(w_0, \dots, w_K\) with \(|w_k| \ge \tau\), for example \(\tau = 10^{-4}\), and drops the rest, so that

      \[\tilde X_t = \sum_{k=0}^{K} w_k X_{t-k}, \qquad t > K.\]
      (13.11)

      \(\tau\) is the smallest weight kept and \(K\) the last lag kept, that is, the window length. The first \(K\) days have too little history and are dropped.

      Every \(\tilde X_t\) uses the same number of lags, and only past values enter, so the feature has no look-ahead. The price of the truncation is the window length: the smaller \(d\), the more lags are needed. At \(d = 0.25\) and \(\tau = 10^{-4}\), the window has \(K = 444\) lags.

      The truncated weights no longer sum to zero. Their sum is 0.178 at \(d = 0.25\) and 0.040 at \(d = 0.5\). This number decides the integration order of the FFD series, as the last slide of the section shows.

      \[\Delta\tilde X_t = c + \gamma\,\tilde X_{t-1} + \sum_{i=1}^{p}\phi_i\,\Delta\tilde X_{t-i} + e_t.\]

        Truncated FFD of an \(I(1)\) Price Is Still \(I(1)\)

        Slide 21 in the PDF

        The integration order of the FFD series follows from a decomposition. Adding and subtracting \(w_k X_t\) in each term of (13.11) gives

        \[\tilde X_t = \sum_{k=0}^{K} w_k X_{t-k} = \Big(\sum_{k=0}^{K} w_k\Big) X_t - \sum_{k=1}^{K} w_k\,(X_t - X_{t-k}).\]
        (13.13)

        The first term is today's level times the sum of the truncated weights. The second is a weighted sum of the \(k\)-day changes \(X_t - X_{t-k}\). If \(X_t\) is \(I(1)\), each change is \(I(0)\), and so is their finite weighted sum. The first term is \(0.178\,X_t\) at \(d = 0.25\): a scaled random walk. The FFD series is therefore \(I(1)\).

        The unit-root component has small variance next to the stationary part, so an ADF test with few lags confuses the two. A Monte Carlo experiment measures the size of the problem. For a pure random walk of \(T = 6{,}274\) days, filtered by FFD with \(d = 0.25\), the ADF test with one lag rejects the unit root in 97.8% of 500 samples, at a nominal level of 5%.

        The general statement is that a finite filter whose weights do not sum to zero keeps a level component, whatever the ADF \(p\)-value. The FFD log price in the case study is therefore a scaled price level, not a stationary feature. The memory of the series is measured directly, with the local Whittle estimator, in Further Topics.

        Labels: Definition of the Target Variable

        A supervised model learns the label it is given, so the definition of the label fixes the question the model answers. The plain sign of a future return ignores volatility and the path of the price before the horizon. The triple-barrier label of López de Prado (2018) reproduces a trader's real decision, with a profit-taking level, a stop-loss and a maximum holding period. Meta-labelling then separates the decision on the side of a trade from the decision on its size.

        From Fixed-Horizon to Triple-Barrier Labels (1/2)

        Slide 22 in the PDF

        The fixed-horizon label is the sign of the \(h\)-day log return, with a dead zone around zero:

        \[r_{t,t+h} = \log P_{t+h} - \log P_t, \qquad y_t = \begin{cases} +1 & \text{if } r_{t,t+h} > \tau \\ -1 & \text{if } r_{t,t+h} < -\tau \\ \phantom{+}0 & \text{otherwise.} \end{cases}\]
        (13.14)

        \(P_t\) is the closing price on day \(t\), \(h\) the horizon in days and \(\tau \ge 0\) a threshold; moves smaller than \(\tau\) in absolute value get the label 0.

        The label in (13.14) has three problems. A constant \(\tau\) ignores time-varying volatility, so the same threshold is a large move in a calm year and a small one in a crisis. The path is ignored: a stop-loss could have been hit before \(t + h\), and the label would still record only the return at the horizon. The classes are unbalanced: for the S&P 500 with \(h = 10\) and \(\tau = 0\), 60.5% of the labels are up and 39.5% down.

        From Fixed-Horizon to Triple-Barrier Labels (2/2)

        Slide 23 in the PDF

        The triple-barrier label (López de Prado, 2018) starts at an event time \(t_0\), with daily volatility \(\sigma_{t_0}\) and horizon \(h\). The upper barrier, for profit-taking, is reached when \(\log P_t - \log P_{t_0} \ge \pi_u \sigma_{t_0}\sqrt{h}\); the lower barrier, the stop-loss, when the same difference is \(\le -\pi_l \sigma_{t_0}\sqrt{h}\); and the vertical barrier is the date \(t = t_0 + h\). The label is set by the first barrier touched, at time \(t_1\): \(+1\) for the upper barrier, \(-1\) for the lower one, and at the vertical barrier the sign of \(r_{t_0,t_1}\), or 0 under an alternative convention.

        The quantity \(\sigma_{t_0}\sqrt{h}\) is the typical \(h\)-day move, and \(\pi_u, \pi_l > 0\) are the barrier widths in units of it. Each label has a span \([t_0, t_1]\), which records the days whose returns it uses; the span is the input of purging in validation.

        The daily volatility \(\sigma_{t_0}\) is the square root of an exponentially weighted moving average (EWMA) of squared deviations of daily log returns, with span 50:

        \[\sigma_t^2 \approx \alpha\sum_{k \ge 0}(1-\alpha)^k\,\big(r_{t-k} - \bar r_t\big)^2, \qquad \alpha = \frac{2}{\text{span} + 1} = \frac{2}{51} \approx 0.039,\]

        where \(\bar r_t\) is the exponentially weighted mean return. A span of 50 gives an effective memory of about two trading months. Volatility-scaled barriers are needed because a fixed threshold would be hit almost daily in 2008 or March 2020 and rarely in calm years.

          Meta-Labelling: Deciding Whether to Act (1/2)

          Slide 26 in the PDF

          Meta-labelling splits a trading decision into two models. A primary model, which may be a simple rule or an economic view chosen for high recall, uses the features \(\mathbf{x}_t\) to set the side of the trade, long or short. A secondary, or meta, model is an ML classifier that receives the same features and the primary signal and answers a narrower question: is the signal right? Its output sets the size of the bet, proportional to its predicted probability, with zero meaning that the signal is skipped.

          The meta-label is \(y^{meta}_t = 1\) if the primary signal was profitable, that is, if the barrier was touched in its direction, and \(0\) otherwise. The secondary model estimates \(\hat p = P(y^{meta}_t = 1 \mid \mathbf{x}_t)\), the probability that the signal of day \(t\) is right given the features.

          The division of labour is the point of the method. ML learns when to trust an interpretable primary rule, not the direction of the market itself, which the previous sections showed to be nearly unpredictable.

          Meta-Labelling: Deciding Whether to Act (2/2)

          Slide 27 in the PDF

          A meta model acts as a filter that decides which signals to act on, and three scores describe a filter:

          \[\text{precision} = \frac{TP}{TP+FP}, \qquad \text{recall} = \frac{TP}{TP+FN}, \qquad F_1 = \frac{2\cdot\text{precision}\cdot\text{recall}}{\text{precision}+\text{recall}}.\]
          (13.15)

          TP (true positives) counts the profitable signals taken, FP (false positives) the unprofitable signals taken, and FN (false negatives) the profitable signals skipped. Acting on every signal gives recall 1. \(F_1\) is the harmonic mean of precision and recall and lies between 0 and 1.

          The example comes from Seminar 13, exercise B11: a crossover of the 20- and 50-day moving averages (MA20/MA50) on Bitcoin, with 1,660 test trades. With the meta filter, precision rises from 0.475 to 0.517, recall falls from 1 to 0.541, and \(F_1\) falls from 0.644 to 0.529. The mean gross log return per trade, before costs, moves from −0.27% to +0.19%.

          Meta-labelling raises precision at the cost of recall. With a cost per trade, precision and the return per trade matter, not \(F_1\), which penalises the trades skipped as heavily as the losing trades taken.

          Validation without Information Leakage

          Cross-validation estimates how a model performs on data it has not seen, but only if the training and test sets do not share information. In financial data they usually do: features are serially correlated and labels overlap in time. This section defines leakage, presents purged K-fold CV with an embargo (López de Prado, 2018) and measures, on a target with nothing to predict, how much ordinary shuffled K-fold overstates skill.

          Leakage: What Crosses the Train/Test Boundary

          Slide 28 in the PDF

          Leakage occurs when the training set contains information that is also present in the test set, so that the test score no longer measures performance on genuinely new data. The question of this section is why ordinary \(K\)-fold overstates a model's skill on financial series.

          Two properties of financial data create leakage. Features are serially correlated, \(\mathbf{x}_t \approx \mathbf{x}_{t+1}\), because they are built from rolling windows, and labels overlap, \(y_t \approx y_{t+1}\), because consecutive multi-day returns share most of their daily returns. A shuffled \(K\)-fold therefore puts near-identical days on both sides of the boundary. A flexible model, such as RF or GBM, memorises the neighbour and reaches an excellent "out-of-sample" score; tuning on such a leaky CV selects the most overfitted model.

          Other sources of leakage are transformations fitted on the full sample, such as scaling, principal component analysis or the choice of \(d\) for FFD. Survivorship bias, data revisions and wrong timestamps add information that was not available at the decision date.

          Purged K-Fold with Embargo (1/2)

          Slide 29 in the PDF

          Purged \(K\)-fold uses contiguous blocks in time as folds (López de Prado, 2018). Test fold \(k\) holds the observations \(t^{a}_k, \dots, t^{b}_k\), whose labels end at the latest at \(T_k = \max_{i \in \text{test}_k} t_{1,i}\). The training set keeps only the observations that do not touch the test fold:

          \[\mathcal{A}_k = \Big\{ i \notin \text{test}_k:\ [t_{0,i}, t_{1,i}] \cap [t^{a}_k, T_k] = \emptyset \ \text{ and } \ t_{0,i} \notin (T_k,\, T_k + e] \Big\}.\]
          (13.16)

          The training set (13.16) rests on two conditions:

          1. Purging: it removes the training labels whose span overlaps the span of the test labels.
          2. Embargo: it removes the \(e\) observations that start right after \(T_k\), the end of the last test label, for example 1% of the sample. The embargo is needed because features look back in time, so the observations just after the test block still carry information from it.

          The purged CV score is the mean of \(K\) scores, each from a model fitted on \(\mathcal{A}_k\) and evaluated on fold \(k\). With labels of length \(h\), a boundary removes \(h\) observations before the test block and \(h + e\) after it, so an interior fold loses \(2h + e\) observations from training. A 10-observation example is worked out in the Appendix.

              Model Selection versus Deployment

              Slide 32 in the PDF

              Ordinary \(K\)-fold is not always invalid for time series. Bergmeir, Hyndman & Koo (2018) show that it is valid for a purely autoregressive model with serially uncorrelated errors. The conditions under which it fails, and how to check them, are in the Appendix.

              Two tasks require different schemes. Model selection, the choice of features, family and tuning, uses purged \(K\)-fold or combinatorial purged CV (CPCV). CPCV tests on many combinations of groups and returns a distribution of Sharpe ratios instead of one number.

              Deployment is evaluated by walk-forward (WF): the model is trained on \([0, T_k]\), tested on \((T_k, T_{k+1}]\) and rolled forward, where \(T_k\) is the end of the \(k\)-th training window. Walk-forward is realistic, but it produces a single path with high variance, so it serves the final evaluation and not the choice of the model. Both schemes are compared in the Appendix.

                Are the Differences Significant? Pesaran–Timmermann and AUC Intervals (1/2)

                Slide 39 in the PDF

                The Pesaran–Timmermann (PT) test (Pesaran & Timmermann, 1992) asks whether predicted and realised directions are related:

                \[PT = \frac{\hat P - \hat P^*}{\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}} \xrightarrow{d} N(0,1), \qquad \hat P^* = \hat p_y \hat p_x + (1-\hat p_y)(1-\hat p_x).\]
                (13.18)

                \(\hat P\) is the share of days with the correct sign, \(\hat p_y\) and \(\hat p_x\) are the shares of realised and of predicted "up", and \(\hat P^*\) is the share correct expected if the predictions were independent of the outcomes. Under \(H_0\) of independence, \(\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}\) is the standard error of \(\hat P - \hat P^*\), given in the Appendix. A value \(PT > 1.645\) indicates skill at 5% (one-sided), and \(PT < 0\) means fewer hits than chance.

                PT assumes independent days, which overlapping 5-day labels violate. The case study therefore also uses the \(t\)-statistic of the slope in \(y_t = a + b\,x_t + u_t\), with \(x_t\) the predicted and \(y_t\) the realised direction, computed with Newey–West standard errors with \(q = 4\) lags (Further Topics); \(b > 0\) means skill.

                For the AUC, the DeLong standard error assumes independent days (Appendix). The intervals are therefore computed by a circular block bootstrap (Künsch, 1989) on blocks of \(L = 20\) consecutive days, four times the label length, which keeps the serial dependence within each block.

                Walk-Forward Backtest: Strategy and Design

                Slide 41 in the PDF

                The backtest asks whether a trader following the model would have earned more, after costs, than by simply holding the index. The position is \(w_t \in \{0,1\}\): long the S&P 500 if the model's probability of "up" is \(\hat p_t > 0.5\), otherwise cash, decided with information up to \(t\). The decision at the close of day \(t\) is traded at the close of day \(t+1\), so the strategy return is

                \[r^{s}_{t+2} = w_t\, r_{t+2} - c\,|w_t - w_{t-1}|,\]
                (13.19)

                where \(r_{t+2}\) is the log return of the index from the close of \(t+1\) to that of \(t+2\). The turnover \(|w_t - w_{t-1}|\) is 1 on the days the position changes. The cost is \(c = 5\) basis points (bp), that is, 0.05% per unit traded, a prudent cost for an exchange-traded fund on the S&P 500; \(r - c\) approximates \(\log((1-c)\,e^{r}) = r + \log(1-c)\), the log return after paying the cost.

                The exposure \(\bar w = \frac{1}{T}\sum_t w_t\) is the share of the \(T\) days spent invested. Cash earns 0, so interest on cash days is ignored, and both Sharpe ratios use daily log returns with a risk-free rate of 0, annualised with \(\sqrt{252}\).

                The model is the GBM, refitted every January over 2010–2026 on all past data, with purging of the labels that overlap the test year. Its FFD log price uses \(d = 0.15\), chosen on data up to 2009 only. The benchmark is buy-and-hold, \(w_t = 1\), with exposure 100% and no turnover.

                  Slide 44 in the PDF

The cross-sectional literature and the predictability of the market return had been studied separately.

                  The benchmark is hard to beat: Welch & Goyal (2008) show that most published predictors do not beat the historical mean. The first out-of-sample forecast has 180 months for 100 predictors, so the regression on all predictors learns noise.                     Selection Bias and the False Strategy Theorem

                    Slide 48 in the PDF

                    The question of the section is how large the best Sharpe ratio among \(N\) trials is when none of them has skill. A researcher who tries \(N\) strategies and reports the best commits selection bias: even if all of them have a true Sharpe ratio of zero, the maximum \(\max_n \widehat{SR}_n\) of the estimated ratios grows with \(N\). In the cross-section of returns, Harvey, Liu & Zhu (2016) conclude that, given hundreds of published factors, a new factor needs \(t > 3\), not 2.

                    The False Strategy Theorem (Bailey & López de Prado, 2014) gives the size of the maximum. If the estimates \(\widehat{SR}_n\), \(n=1,\dots,N\), are independent and approximately \(N(0, \mathrm{Var}[\widehat{SR}])\), the expected maximum Sharpe ratio is

                    \[SR_0 \equiv \mathbb{E}\Big[\max_n \widehat{SR}_n\Big] \approx \sqrt{\mathrm{Var}[\widehat{SR}]}\,\Big[(1-\gamma_E)\,\Phi^{-1}\Big(1-\tfrac{1}{N}\Big) + \gamma_E\,\Phi^{-1}\Big(1-\tfrac{1}{N e}\Big)\Big].\]
                    (13.22)

                    Here \(\widehat{SR}_n\) is the estimated Sharpe ratio of strategy \(n\), \(\Phi\) the standard Normal distribution function and \(\Phi^{-1}\) its quantile function. \(\mathrm{Var}[\widehat{SR}]\) is the sampling variance of one estimated Sharpe ratio, about \(1/T\) per period for i.i.d. returns, with \(T\) the number of return periods, and \(\gamma_E \approx 0.5772\) is the Euler–Mascheroni constant.

                    \(SR_0\) is the Sharpe ratio that the luckiest of \(N\) worthless strategies is expected to show. By (13.22) it rises with \(N\), through the Normal quantiles, and with the sampling noise \(\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}[\widehat{SR}]}\), which falls with the length of the sample.

                        Sharpe Ratio Inference: Standard Error and PSR (1/2)

                        Slide 52 in the PDF

                        Two questions precede any correction for selection: how precise an estimated Sharpe ratio is, and how it is compared with a benchmark. The per-period Sharpe ratio of daily log returns, with a risk-free rate of 0, is \(\widehat{SR} = \bar r/\hat\sigma\). The shape of the return distribution enters through the skewness \(\gamma_3 = \mathbb{E}[(r-\mu)^3]/\sigma^3\) and the ordinary kurtosis \(\gamma_4 = \mathbb{E}[(r-\mu)^4]/\sigma^4\), which equals 3 for the Normal distribution, where \(\mu\) and \(\sigma\) are the mean and SD of \(r\).

                        For i.i.d. returns, the delta method gives the sampling variance (Lo, 2002; Opdyke, 2007)

                        \[\mathrm{Var}(\widehat{SR}) \approx \frac{1}{T}\Big(1 - \gamma_3\, SR + \frac{\gamma_4 - 1}{4}\, SR^2\Big),\]
                        (13.23)

                        where \(T\) is the number of returns and \(SR\) the true per-period Sharpe ratio. Negative skewness and fat tails widen the uncertainty. For the Normal distribution, \(\gamma_3 = 0\) and \(\gamma_4 = 3\), and (13.23) reduces to \((1 + \frac12 SR^2)/T\).

                        The formula requires a finite fourth moment, which Chapter 1 questions. Annualisation with \(\sqrt{A}\), where \(A\) is the number of return periods per year, also assumes zero autocorrelation; the correction is in Further Topics.

                        Sharpe Ratio Inference: Standard Error and PSR (2/2)

                        Slide 53 in the PDF

                        The probabilistic Sharpe ratio (PSR) of Bailey & López de Prado (2012) is called by its authors the probability that the true Sharpe ratio exceeds a benchmark \(SR^*\). Formally it is \(\Phi\) of a \(z\)-statistic whose denominator is the standard deviation from (13.23):

                        \[\widehat{PSR}(SR^*) = \Phi\left( \frac{(\widehat{SR} - SR^*)\sqrt{T-1}}{\sqrt{1 - \hat\gamma_3\,\widehat{SR} + \frac{\hat\gamma_4 - 1}{4}\,\widehat{SR}^2}} \right).\]
                        (13.24)

                        \(SR^*\) is the benchmark, for example 0 or the expected maximum \(SR_0\) of (13.22), and \(\hat\gamma_3\), \(\hat\gamma_4\) are the sample skewness and kurtosis.

                        The reading follows from the construction: \(\widehat{PSR} = 1 - p\), where \(p\) is the one-sided \(p\)-value of \(H_0: SR \le SR^*\). A value \(\widehat{PSR} > 0.95\) therefore means rejecting \(H_0\) at 5%, under i.i.d. returns and the delta-method approximation. It is not a 95% probability for the true Sharpe ratio, which is a fixed number in this frequentist setting.

                        Machine Learning for Inference

                        Prediction and inference have different targets. A model may predict well while giving a biased estimate of the effect of one variable, and a variable chosen by the LASSO is not thereby significant. This section presents two methods that use ML for the nuisance part of a model and keep valid inference on a parameter of interest: post-double selection (Belloni, Chernozhukov & Hansen, 2014) and double/debiased machine learning (Chernozhukov et al., 2018). A worked example asks whether the VIX predicts S&P 500 returns.

                        Selection Is Not Inference: Post-Double Selection (1/2)

                        Slide 55 in the PDF

                        The question of the section is how to obtain a valid confidence interval for the effect of one variable after ML has chosen the controls. The LASSO path answers "which feature enters first", not "which feature matters", and OLS \(t\)-statistics computed after a LASSO ignore the selection step. A control that is correlated with the regressor of interest \(d_t\) but only weakly with the outcome \(y_t\) gets dropped, and its effect moves into the coefficient of \(d_t\): the omitted-variable bias.

                        The framework is the partially linear model, in which the effect of \(d_t\) is linear and the controls enter through unknown functions:

                        \[y_t = \theta\, d_t + g(\mathbf{x}_t) + \varepsilon_t, \qquad d_t = m(\mathbf{x}_t) + v_t.\]
                        (13.25)

                        \(y_t\) is the outcome, \(d_t\) the regressor of interest and \(\theta\) its effect, the parameter to estimate. \(\mathbf{x}_t\) holds the \(p\) controls, \(g\) is their effect on \(y_t\), \(m\) describes how they predict \(d_t\), and \(\varepsilon_t\), \(v_t\) are errors. The model is approximately sparse when a few controls explain most of \(g\) and \(m\).

                        Selection Is Not Inference: Post-Double Selection (2/2)

                        Slide 56 in the PDF

                        Post-double selection (Belloni, Chernozhukov & Hansen, 2014) runs three regressions:

                        1. A LASSO of \(y_t\) on \(\mathbf{x}_t\), which selects the set \(\hat S_y\).
                        2. A LASSO of \(d_t\) on \(\mathbf{x}_t\), which selects \(\hat S_d\).
                        3. An OLS regression of \(y_t\) on \(d_t\) and on the controls kept by either LASSO, \(\mathbf{x}_{\hat S_y \cup \hat S_d}\), with a robust standard error.

                        The second step is what makes inference valid. A control is missed only if it matters for neither equation of (13.25): if it is weakly related to \(y_t\) but strongly to \(d_t\), the LASSO of \(d_t\) keeps it. The bias from the controls that are still omitted is then of second order.

                        The method is valid under independent sampling and sparsity. With HAC standard errors it is an approximation for time series. The penalty level and the formal conditions are in the Appendix.

                        Double/Debiased Machine Learning (DML): Orthogonal Scores and Cross-Fitting

                        Slide 57 in the PDF

                        Double/debiased machine learning (DML) (Chernozhukov et al., 2018) residualises both variables with any learner, the LASSO, a random forest or boosting, and then regresses residual on residual:

                        \[\tilde y_t = y_t - \hat\ell(\mathbf{x}_t), \quad \tilde v_t = d_t - \hat m(\mathbf{x}_t), \qquad \hat\theta = \frac{\sum_t \tilde v_t\, \tilde y_t}{\sum_t \tilde v_t^2}.\]
                        (13.26)

                        \(\hat\ell(\mathbf{x}) = \hat{\mathbb{E}}[y \mid \mathbf{x}]\) is the ML prediction of \(y_t\) from the controls and \(\hat m(\mathbf{x})\) the ML prediction of \(d_t\).                         The estimator solves the sample version of the Neyman-orthogonal score \(\psi = (\tilde y - \theta \tilde v)\,\tilde v\), whose mean is zero at the true \(\theta\). Orthogonality means that first-order errors in \(\hat\ell\) and \(\hat m\) do not bias \(\hat\theta\); the rate conditions are in the Appendix. Cross-fitting fits \(\hat\ell\) and \(\hat m\) on \(K-1\) folds, residualises the held-out fold and repeats over the folds, which removes the bias from overfitting each observation's own value.

                        The published theory assumes independent sampling. With overlapping 5-day labels, the chapter uses contiguous folds purged by 5 days around the test fold, and HAC standard errors. This is an adaptation to serial dependence outside the published theory.

                          Current Research: Structural and Flexible Models

                          The frontier of ML in asset pricing divides on the role of model complexity. One line of work argues that heavily over-parameterised models can still forecast well; critical readings trace the gains to simple strategies or to hard-to-arbitrage stocks. The case study of Chen, Pelger & Zhu (2024) takes a third route: it imposes economic structure, the no-arbitrage condition, on a deep network.

                          The Virtue of Complexity, and a Critical Reading [deeper]

                          Slide 61 in the PDF

                          Kelly, Malamud & Zhou (2024) fit ridge regressions on \(P\) random Fourier features of a few predictors, in rolling windows as short as \(T = 12\) months, with \(P\) in the thousands. Random Fourier features are sines and cosines of random linear combinations of the predictors. Their theory shows that with \(P > T\) the minimum-norm solution, the exact fit with the smallest \(\|\boldsymbol\beta\|_2\), shrinks implicitly, the phenomenon called "double descent". The \(R^2_{OOS}\) may be negative while the Sharpe ratio of market timing rises with complexity, so complexity is not automatically overfitting.

                          Nagel (2025) gives a critical reading. For \(P \gg T\) the forecast is a similarity-weighted average of the \(T\) training returns. In short windows similarity is mostly recency, so the method becomes a volatility-timed momentum strategy, which loses on artificial data with reversals.

                          Avramov, Cheng & Metzker (2023) find that the profits of deep-learning signals come from hard-to-arbitrage stocks: excluding microcaps, distressed firms or high-volatility episodes attenuates them, and costs erode them further. Chen, Pelger & Zhu (2024) impose economic structure instead, using the no-arbitrage moment conditions as the loss of a deep network. A survey of the field is Kelly & Xiu (2023), and a textbook is Dixon, Halperin & Bilokon (2020).

                          Slide 62 in the PDF

                          Chen, Pelger & Zhu (2024) estimate the stochastic discount factor (SDF) \(M_{t+1}\) that prices all individual US stocks, conditioning on 46 firm characteristics and on the history of 178 macroeconomic series. Under no arbitrage, the SDF prices every excess return (Section I.A, eq. 1 of the paper):

                          \[E_t[M_{t+1}R^e_{t+1,i}] = 0, \qquad M_{t+1} = 1 - \omega_t^\top R^e_{t+1}.\]
                          (13.27)

                          \(R^e_{t+1,i}\) is the excess return of stock \(i\) over the Treasury bill from month \(t\) to \(t+1\), and \(R^e_{t+1}\) the vector of all stocks. \(E_t\) is the expectation given the information at \(t\), and \(\omega_t\) are portfolio weights, a function of the characteristics and of the macroeconomic states.

                          The portfolio \(F_{t+1} = \omega_t^\top R^e_{t+1}\), the SDF portfolio, is the conditional tangency portfolio, the portfolio with the highest conditional Sharpe ratio. Condition (13.27) implies the beta representation

                          \[E_t[R^e_{t+1,i}] = \beta_{t,i}\,E_t[F_{t+1}],\]
                          (13.28)

                          where \(\beta_{t,i}\) is the loading of stock \(i\) on \(F\). A stock's expected excess return is its loading times the premium of \(F\); the derivation is in Further Topics.

                            A Checklist Before Any Claim

                            Slide 70 in the PDF

                            The checklist collects the conditions under which a predictive claim can be taken seriously.                             Where the Campbell–Thompson Formula Comes From

                            Slide 75 in the PDF

                            The derivation has four steps. Since \(\varepsilon_{t+1}\) is independent of \(x_t\), \(\sigma_\varepsilon^2 = (1 - R^2)\,\sigma_r^2\), the risk the informed investor still bears. Conditionally on \(x_t\) the investor faces mean \(\mu + x_t\) and variance \(\sigma_\varepsilon^2\); averaging \((\mu + x_t)^2\) over \(t\) gives \(\mu^2 + R^2\sigma_r^2\), and dividing by \(\sigma_\varepsilon^2\) with \(SR_{BH} = \mu/\sigma_r\) gives (13.29). With \(R^2 = 0.005\), \(\mathbb{E}[SR_t^2] = (0.128^2 + 0.005)/0.995 = 0.02149\), so \(\sqrt{\mathbb{E}[SR_t^2]} = 0.1466\) a month, 0.51 a year and 1.15 times \(SR_{BH}\).

                            In brief

                            • Same model: \(r_{t+1} = \mu + x_t + \varepsilon_{t+1}\)
                              • \(\E[x_t] = 0\), \(\varepsilon_{t+1}\) independent of \(x_t\)
                              • write \(\sigma_r^2 = \Var(r_{t+1})\)
                            • Step 1, the risk the informed investor still bears
                              • \(\Var(r_{t+1}) = \Var(x_t) + \sigma_\varepsilon^2\) and \(R^2 = \Var(x_t)/\sigma_r^2\), so
                              \[ \sigma_\varepsilon^2 = (1 - R^2)\,\sigma_r^2 \]
                            • Step 2, the investor's conditional Sharpe ratio at \(t\)
                              • mean \(\mu + x_t\), variance \(\sigma_\varepsilon^2\), squared SR \((\mu + x_t)^2/\sigma_\varepsilon^2\)
                            • Step 3, average over \(t\): \(\E[(\mu + x_t)^2] = \mu^2 + \Var(x_t) = \mu^2 + R^2\sigma_r^2\)
                            • Step 4, divide and use \(SR_{BH} = \mu/\sigma_r\):
                              \[ \E[SR_t^2] = \frac{\mu^2 + R^2\sigma_r^2}{(1 - R^2)\,\sigma_r^2} = \frac{SR_{BH}^2 + R^2}{1 - R^2} \]
                            • So the formula gives the average squared conditional SR, not the unconditional SR of the strategy
                              • for an investor who re-optimises each month, in monthly units
                            • Numbers, \(R^2 = 0.005\)
                              • \(\E[SR_t^2] = (0.128^2 + 0.005)/0.995 = 0.02149\), \(\sqrt{\E[SR_t^2]} = 0.1466\) a month
                              • \(\times\sqrt{12} = 0.51\) a year; \(0.1466/0.128 = 1.15\)

                            Memory Estimated Directly: Local Whittle (1/3)

                            Slide 78 in the PDF

                            A long-memory series has most of its variance in slow cycles, and the slope of its spectral density near frequency 0 reveals \(\delta\): \(f(\lambda) \sim G\,\lambda^{-2\delta}\) as \(\lambda \to 0\), with \(G > 0\). The local Whittle (LW) estimator (Robinson, 1995) fits this law on the first \(m = n^{a}\) Fourier frequencies \(\lambda_j = 2\pi j/n\), where \(n\) is the sample size and \(a \in (0,1)\) the bandwidth exponent; a larger \(a\) means less variance and more bias from short-run dynamics. The exact local Whittle (ELW) estimator (Shimotsu & Phillips, 2005) is also valid for non-stationary \(\delta \ge \tfrac12\), and the 95% confidence interval is \(\hat\delta \pm 1.96/(2\sqrt m)\).

                            In brief

                            • Idea: a long-memory series has most of its variance in slow cycles
                              • the slope of its spectrum near frequency 0 reveals \(\delta\)
                              \[ f(\lambda) \sim G\,\lambda^{-2\delta} \quad \text{as } \lambda \to 0 \]
                              • \(f(\lambda)\): spectral density, the variance contributed by cycles of frequency \(\lambda\) (period \(2\pi/\lambda\) days)
                              • \(G > 0\): scale constant; \(\sim\): the ratio of the two sides tends to 1
                              • \(\delta > 0\): \(f\) explodes near 0 (long memory); \(\delta = 0\): flat (short memory); \(\delta < 0\): \(f\) vanishes near 0
                            • Local Whittle (LW, Robinson (1995)): fits this law only on the first \(m = n^{a}\) Fourier frequencies
                              • \(\lambda_j = 2\pi j/n\), \(j = 1,\dots,m\)
                              • \(n\): sample size; \(a \in (0,1)\): bandwidth exponent; a larger \(a\) uses more frequencies: less variance, more bias from short-run dynamics
                            • Exact local Whittle (ELW, Shimotsu & Phillips (2005)): also valid for non-stationary \(\delta \ge \frac12\)
                              • both estimators in the Appendix
                            • 95% confidence interval (CI): \(\hat\delta \pm 1.96/(2\sqrt m)\)
                              • the standard error \(1/(2\sqrt m)\) depends only on \(m\)

                            Overlapping Labels and the Newey–West Long-Run Variance

                            Slide 87 in the PDF

                            The significance tests of the case study average a daily series \(z_t\), such as a hit, a loss differential or a regression score. With 5-day labels neighbouring \(z_t\) share returns, so \(\mathrm{Var}(\bar z) \ne \mathrm{Var}(z_t)/n\), and the long-run variance is estimated by the heteroskedasticity and autocorrelation consistent (HAC) estimator of Newey & West (1987) with Bartlett weights:

                            \[\hat\Omega_{NW} = \hat\Gamma_0 + 2\sum_{j=1}^{q}\Big(1 - \frac{j}{q+1}\Big)\hat\Gamma_j, \qquad \hat\Gamma_j = \frac1n\sum_{t=j+1}^{n} (z_t - \bar z)(z_{t-j} - \bar z),\]
                            (13.30)

                            with \(\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}(\bar z) = \sqrt{\hat\Omega_{NW}/n}\). Since \(h\)-day labels share returns up to \(h - 1\) days apart, \(q \ge h - 1 = 4\) is the minimum here. In a toy example with \(z_t\) the sum of the next 5 i.i.d. daily returns of variance \(\sigma^2\), the true long-run variance is \(25\sigma^2\) against \(\Gamma_0 = 5\sigma^2\), so an i.i.d. \(t\) is \(\sqrt5 \approx 2.2\) times too large; Bartlett weights with \(q = 4\) give \(17\sigma^2\), so \(q = h - 1\) is a floor, not a generous choice.

                            In brief

                            • The significance tests of the case study (Pesaran–Timmermann) average a daily series \(z_t\)
                              • \(z_t\): a hit, a loss differential \(d_t\), a regression score
                              • with 5-day labels, neighbouring \(z_t\) share returns, so \(\Var(\bar z) \ne \Var(z_t)/n\)
                            • Newey–West (Newey & West (1987)) heteroskedasticity and autocorrelation consistent (HAC) long-run variance
                              • with Bartlett weights:
                              \[ \hat\Omega_{NW} = \hat\Gamma_0 + 2\sum_{j=1}^{q}\Big(1 - \frac{j}{q+1}\Big)\hat\Gamma_j, \qquad \hat\Gamma_j = \frac1n\sum_{t=j+1}^{n} (z_t - \bar z)(z_{t-j} - \bar z) \]
                              • \(\hat\Gamma_j\): sample autocovariance of \(z_t\) at lag \(j\); \(q\): number of lags (“lag 4” in the tables); \(\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}(\bar z) = \sqrt{\hat\Omega_{NW}/n}\)
                              • lag rule: \(h\)-day labels share returns up to \(h - 1\) days apart, so \(q \ge h - 1 = 4\) is the minimum here
                            • Toy example: \(z_t\) = sum of the next 5 i.i.d. daily returns of variance \(\sigma^2\)

                            Correlated Trials in the DSR: Alternative Approximations

                            Slide 96 in the PDF

                            The two rows of the Bitcoin DSR table rest on different assumptions:

                            • (a) the raw \(N\) with \(\mathrm{Var}[\widehat{SR}_n]\) from the dispersion of the trial Sharpe ratios;
                            • (b) \(\mathrm{Var} = 1/T\) with an effective number of independent trials \(\hat N < N\) (Seminar 13, exercise B6).

                            Combining the two can count the same dependence twice. In a toy model of \(N\) zero-skill trials with pairwise correlation \(\rho\), the common shock creates no selection, so the expected maximum is \(\sqrt{1-\rho}\) times the independent value, and the dispersion across trials is \((1-\rho)\sigma^2\), which (a) captures. The SD ratio of the two rows, \(0.0109/0.0220 = 0.50\), reads in this model as \(\rho \approx 0.75\). Since the dispersion also contains true differences between rules, the recommendation is to report the conservative row and either (a) or (b), with \(N\), and to call a rule a discovery only if it also passes the conservative row.

                            In brief

                            Two approximations with different assumptions
                            • (a) raw \(N\), with \(\Var[\widehat{SR}_n]\) = dispersion of the trial SRs: row 1 of the Bitcoin DSR table
                            • (b) \(\Var[\widehat{SR}_n] = 1/T\), with an effective number of independent trials \(\hat N < N\)
                              • Seminar 13, exercise B6
                            • Used together, dispersion and \(\hat N\) can count the same dependence effect twice (toy model below)
                            • Why (a) works, toy model: \(N\) zero-skill trials \(\widehat{SR}_n = \sigma(\sqrt\rho\,Z + \sqrt{1-\rho}\,e_n)\)
                              • each of variance \(\sigma^2 = 1/T\), independent standard Normal \(Z,e_1,\ldots,e_N\), pairwise correlation \(\rho\)
                              • the common shock moves all trials together, so only \(\sqrt{1-\rho}\,\sigma\, e_n\) creates selection: \(\E[\max_n \widehat{SR}_n] = \sqrt{1-\rho}\;\times\) the independent-trial value
                              • the dispersion across trials is \((1-\rho)\sigma^2\), so (a) captures this scaling
                              • the two rows have SD ratio \(0.0109/0.0220 = 0.50\), which this toy model reads as \(\rho \approx 1 - 0.50^2 \approx 0.75\)
                            • The dispersion also contains true differences between rules
                              • with real rules (a) is an approximation
                            • Recommendation: report the conservative row and either (a) or (b), with \(N\)
                              • call a rule a discovery only if it also passes the conservative row

                            Probability of Backtest Overfitting (PBO)

                            Slide 101 in the PDF

                            Combinatorially symmetric cross-validation (CSCV) (Bailey, Borwein, López de Prado & Zhu, 2017) splits a \(T \times N\) matrix of returns into \(S\) contiguous blocks and, for each of the \(\binom{S}{S/2}\) choices, uses half of the blocks in sample and the rest out of sample. For each split it records the relative OOS rank \(\bar\omega\) of the best IS configuration and its logit \(\lambda = \log(\bar\omega/(1-\bar\omega))\); the PBO is \(\Pr[\lambda \le 0]\), the share of splits in which the IS winner ranks at or below the OOS median. A PBO near 0 means that the IS winner reliably stays above the median, and a PBO of 0.5 or more that selection is no better than chance. The PBO evaluates the research procedure, not one strategy, and like the DSR it needs every trial logged.

                            In brief

                            Combinatorially symmetric cross-validation (CSCV): Bailey, Borwein, López de Prado & Zhu (2017)
                            1. Matrix of returns: \(T\) periods \(\times\) \(N\) configurations; split the rows into \(S\) contiguous blocks
                            2. For each of the \(\binom{S}{S/2}\) choices, half of the blocks are in sample (IS), the rest out of sample (OOS)
                            3. Pick the best IS configuration \(n^*\)
                              • rank all \(N\) configurations OOS in ascending order
                              • set \(\bar\omega = \text{rank}(n^*)/(N+1) \in (0,1)\)
                            4. Logit \(\lambda = \log\frac{\bar\omega}{1-\bar\omega}\); \(PBO = \Pr[\lambda \le 0]\)
                              • the share of splits in which the IS winner ranks at or below the OOS median
                            • \(\bar\omega\): relative OOS rank of the IS winner, near 1 if it stays the best
                              • \(\lambda > 0\): above the OOS median
                            • \(PBO \approx 0\): the IS winner reliably stays above the OOS median
                              • \(PBO \approx 0.5\) or more: selecting the IS winner is no better than picking at random
                            • PBO evaluates the research procedure, not one strategy
                              • it is not the probability of losing money
                              • like DSR it needs every trial logged (Seminar 13, exercise B12)

                            From the Pricing Condition to the Beta Representation

                            Slide 102 in the PDF

                            With \(M = 1 - F\), the condition \(E[(1 - F)R^e_i] = 0\) gives \(E[R^e_i] = E[R^e_i F]\) for every stock; applied to \(R^e_i = F\) it gives \(E[F] = E[F^2]\); and with \(\beta_i = E[R^e_i F]/E[F^2]\) it follows that \(E[R^e_i] = \beta_i E[F]\), which is (13.28) written unconditionally. When the pricing condition holds, this second-moment beta equals the regression beta; on data with pricing errors the two can differ, so the paper's loading network computes the second-moment form. In the example \(E[F] = E[F^2] = 0.01\) and \(E[R^e_i F] = 0.005\), both forms give \(\beta_i = 0.5\) and \(E[R^e_i] = 0.5\%\) a month.

                            In brief

                            • Three steps (written unconditionally; conditionally, replace \(E\) by \(E_t\))
                              1. With \(M = 1 - F\): \(E[(1 - F)R^e_i] = 0\), so \(E[R^e_i] = E[R^e_i F]\) for every stock
                              2. \(F\) is itself an excess return; apply step 1 to \(R^e_i = F\): \(E[F] = E[F^2]\)
                              3. Define \(\beta_i = E[R^e_i F]/E[F^2]\); then \(E[R^e_i] = E[R^e_i F] = \beta_i E[F^2] = \beta_i E[F]\)
                            • This \(\beta_i\) is defined with second moments
                              • when step 1 holds it equals the regression beta, since \(\operatorname{Cov}(R^e_i, F) = \beta_i E[F^2] - \beta_i E[F]^2 = \beta_i \Var(F)\)

                            Fractional Weights and Their Asymptotics

                            Slide 114 in the PDF

                            The weights of \((1-B)^d = \sum_k \binom{d}{k}(-B)^k\) are, for \(d \notin \{0, 1, 2, \dots\}\), \(w_k = \Gamma(k-d)/(\Gamma(-d)\,\Gamma(k+1))\), with \(\Gamma\) the gamma function, and they decay like \(k^{-(1+d)}/\Gamma(-d)\), more slowly for smaller \(d\).                             • Weights: \(w_0 = 1\), \(w_k = -\,w_{k-1}\,\frac{d-k+1}{k}\)

                              • \(d = 0\): \(w_0 = 1\) and the rest zero, so the sum is 1; \(d = 1\): \(w = (1, -1)\), sum 0
                              • for \(d < 0\) the series of weights does not converge

                            Local Whittle and Exact Local Whittle (1/2)

                            Slide 115 in the PDF

                            The periodogram \(I(\lambda_j) = \frac{1}{2\pi n}|\sum_{t} X_t e^{-\mathrm{i}\lambda_j t}|^2\) estimates the spectral density at the Fourier frequency \(\lambda_j\).                             In brief

                            • Periodogram: the sample estimate of the spectral density
                              • at the Fourier frequency \(\lambda_j = 2\pi j/n\)

                              • on the first \(m\) frequencies

                              • \(\xrightarrow{d}\): convergence in distribution; hence the standard error \(1/(2\sqrt m)\)

                            When Is K-Fold Valid for Time Series?

                            Slide 118 in the PDF

                            Bergmeir, Hyndman & Koo (2018) show that standard \(K\)-fold CV is valid for a purely autoregressive model \(y_t = g(y_{t-1},\dots,y_{t-p}) + \varepsilon_t\) with serially uncorrelated errors, because the test errors are then uncorrelated with the training errors.                             Giacomini–White, Statistic and Rolling Window

                            Slide 122 in the PDF

                            The null of Giacomini & White (2006) concerns the forecasting method, the model with its estimation window and refitting rule: \(\mathbb{E}[d_{t+\tau} \mid \mathcal{F}_t] = 0\), tested through test functions \(\mathbf{h}_t\) known at \(t\), here 1 and the lagged loss differential, with \(GW = n\,\bar{\mathbf{Z}}^\top \hat\Omega^{-1} \bar{\mathbf{Z}} \to \chi^2_2\). The theory requires a bounded, rolling estimation window, so that estimation noise never vanishes; with a rolling 8-year window, the S&P 500 logit gives \(GW = 26.7\) (\(p < 0.001\)) and forecasts worse than the constant.

                            In brief

                            • Giacomini & White (2006): the null concerns the forecasting method, not the population model
                              • method: model + estimation window + refitting rule
                              \[ H_0: \E\big[d_{t+\tau} \mid \mathcal{F}_t\big] = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; \E\big[\mathbf{h}_t\, d_{t+\tau}\big] = \mathbf{0}, \qquad GW = n\,\bar{\mathbf{Z}}^\top \hat\Omega^{-1} \bar{\mathbf{Z}} \xrightarrow{d} \chi^2_q \]
                            • Notation
