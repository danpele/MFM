Harvey, Liu & Zhu (2016) show that, once hundreds of factors have been tried on the same data, the usual hurdle \(t > 2\) no longer separates discoveries from the luck of selection: a new factor needs \(t > 3\). Machine learning brings flexible, nonlinear functional forms and hundreds of candidate predictors to finance, and with them the same problem on a larger scale. Financial data have a very low signal-to-noise ratio and a single, serially dependent history. On daily S&P 500 returns over 2000–2026, the autocorrelations of returns are small, the lag-1 autocorrelation is \(-0.099\), while the autocorrelations of absolute returns are large and persistent: risk has predictable structure, direction hardly any.

The chapter's case study asks the question directly: can the 5-day direction of the S&P 500 be predicted? On 6,269 daily observations, four models (a penalised logit, a random forest, gradient boosting and a neural network) are validated on the same 5 folds, with purging and embargo. No model beats the "always up" benchmark, whose accuracy is 58.1%. The logit reaches 57.7% accuracy because it predicts "up" on 98.3% of days. In the 2010–2026 walk-forward backtest, the GBM strategy has a Sharpe ratio of 0.33 and buy-and-hold 0.66.

Most false results come from the process, not the algorithm. On a target with nothing to predict, K-fold with shuffled observations reports 69.4% accuracy, purged K-fold with embargo 49.6%. On a grid of 1,284 Bitcoin trading rules, the selected rule has a Sharpe ratio of 2.02 in-sample and 0.03 out-of-sample. The chapter therefore builds, step by step, the features, labels, validation and tests that separate a real prediction from the best of many trials.

    Evidence, and the Economic Value of a Small \(R^2_{OOS}\)

    Slide 7 in the PDF
    • Gu, Kelly & Xiu (2020): monthly returns of individual US stocks, 1957–2016
      • they compare linear models, penalised regressions, principal component regression (PCR), partial least squares (PLS)
      • random forests, gradient boosting and neural nets
    • Their fit measure: the out-of-sample (OOS) \(R^2\)
      • how much of the squared return a forecast removes, relative to forecasting zero
      \[ R^2_{OOS} = 1 - \frac{\sum_t (r_t - \hat r_t)^2}{\sum_t r_t^2} \]
      • \(r_t\): realised return in month \(t\) of the test period; \(\hat r_t\): forecast made with information up to \(t-1\)
      • the denominator is the error of the zero forecast \(\hat r_t = 0\): \(R^2_{OOS} > 0\) if the model beats it, \(R^2_{OOS} < 0\) if it does worse
    • Results (Table 1, monthly \(R^2_{OOS}\))
      • three-layer network \(0.40\%\), gradient boosting \(0.34\%\), random forest \(0.33\%\)
      • OLS with 3 predictors \(0.16\%\), OLS with all 900+ predictors \(-3.46\%\)
    • Trees and nets win through non-linear interactions
      • momentum, liquidity and volatility dominate
    • The benchmark matters
      • Welch & Goyal (2008) show that most published predictors lose to the historical mean
      • Gu, Kelly & Xiu (2020) and Chapter 14 use a zero forecast

    A Minimal Machine Learning Toolbox

    Loss, Penalty and Five Model Families (1/5)

    Slide 8 in the PDF
    • The question of this section
      • why, with a weak signal, does variance control matter more than flexibility?
    • Supervised learning picks, within a model family, the function that fits the training data best
      • after a penalty for complexity
      \[ \hat f = \arg\min_{f \in \mathcal{F}} \; \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} L\big(y_i, f(\mathbf{x}_i)\big) + \lambda\,\Omega(f) \]
      • \((y_i, \mathbf{x}_i)\), \(i = 1,\dots,n\): target and feature vector of observation \(i\); \(\mathcal{F}\): the model family (e.g. all trees of depth 3)
      • \(L\): loss, the squared error \((y - f)^2\) or, for a probability \(f\) of \(y = 1\), the log-loss \(-[y\log f + (1-y)\log(1-f)]\)
      • \(\Omega(f)\): complexity penalty with weight \(\lambda \ge 0\); \(\lambda = 0\): no penalty, large \(\lambda\): a simpler model
    • \(\lambda\) is chosen by \(K\)-fold cross-validation (CV) on folds \(\mathcal{P}_1, \dots, \mathcal{P}_K\):
      \[ CV(\lambda) = \frac1K\sum_{k=1}^{K} \frac{1}{n_k}\sum_{i \in \mathcal{P}_k} L\big(y_i, \hat f^{(-k)}_\lambda(\mathbf{x}_i)\big), \qquad \hat\lambda = \arg\min_\lambda CV(\lambda) \]
      • \(n_k\): number of observations in fold \(k\); \(\hat f^{(-k)}_\lambda\): the model fitted on the other \(K-1\) folds

    Loss, Penalty and Five Model Families (2/5)

    Slide 9 in the PDF
    • Why we penalise: the expected test error at a point \(\mathbf{x}_0\) (Hastie, Tibshirani & Friedman (2009), Ch. 7)
      • for \(y = f(\mathbf{x}) + \varepsilon\) with \(\E[\varepsilon] = 0\) and \(\Var(\varepsilon) = \sigma^2\):
      \[ \E\big[(y_0 - \hat f(\mathbf{x}_0))^2\big] = \underbrace{\sigma^2}_{\text{noise}} + \underbrace{\big(\E[\hat f(\mathbf{x}_0)] - f(\mathbf{x}_0)\big)^2}_{\text{bias}^2} + \underbrace{\Var\big(\hat f(\mathbf{x}_0)\big)}_{\text{variance}} \]
      • \(\E\): expectation over possible training samples and over the noise of the new observation \(y_0 = f(\mathbf{x}_0) + \varepsilon_0\)
      • bias: the systematic error of a too-simple family
      • variance: the sensitivity of \(\hat f\) to the training sample; it grows with model flexibility
      • \(\sigma^2\): variance of the unpredictable part of \(y\), which no model can reduce
    • Derivation: add and subtract \(\E[\hat f(\mathbf{x}_0)]\) in \(y_0 - \hat f(\mathbf{x}_0)\)
      • the cross terms have mean zero because \(\varepsilon_0\) is independent of the training sample
    • In finance, return forecasts reduce the error very little
      • an out-of-sample \(R^2\) below 1%: the model removes less than 1% of the benchmark's squared loss
      • this does not identify \(\sigma^2\): the remaining error also holds misspecification, omitted variables and estimation error
      • so penalties and tree averaging cut variance at the price of a small bias

    Loss, Penalty and Five Model Families (3/5)

    Slide 10 in the PDF
    • Penalised regressions, with \(p\) standardised features and coefficients \(\bm\beta = (\beta_1, \dots, \beta_p)\):
      \[ \hat{\bm\beta} = \arg\min_{\bm\beta}\ \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^{n} \big(y_i - \mathbf{x}_i^\top\bm\beta\big)^2 + \lambda\Big[\alpha\|\bm\beta\|_1 + \tfrac{1-\alpha}{2}\|\bm\beta\|_2^2\Big] \]
      • \(\|\bm\beta\|_1 = \sum_j |\beta_j|\), \(\|\bm\beta\|_2^2 = \sum_j \beta_j^2\); \(\alpha \in [0,1]\): share of the absolute-value penalty
    • Ridge, \(\alpha = 0\) (Hoerl & Kennard (1970)): \(\hat{\bm\beta} = \big(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X} + \tfrac{n\lambda}{2}\mathbf{I}\big)^{-1}\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{y}\)
      • shrinks all coefficients, none exactly to zero
      • stabilises the estimate when predictors are correlated
    • LASSO (least absolute shrinkage and selection operator), \(\alpha = 1\) (Tibshirani (1996))
      • the penalty \(|\beta_j|\) has a kink at zero, so some coefficients become exactly zero
      • LASSO selects features
    • Elastic net, \(0 < \alpha < 1\) (Zou & Hastie (2005)): keeps groups of correlated predictors together
      • LASSO, by contrast, arbitrarily keeps one of them
    • The chapter's linear benchmark is the penalised logit
      • \(p = P(y = 1 \mid \mathbf{x}) = 1/\big(1+e^{-\mathbf{x}^\top\bm\beta}\big)\), with the log-loss in place of the squared loss

    Loss, Penalty and Five Model Families (4/5)

    Slide 11 in the PDF
    • Trees: at each node \(v\) choose the feature \(j\) and threshold \(s\) that reduce the Gini impurity the most
      • Gini impurity: \(G = 1 - \sum_c \hat\pi_c^2\)
      \[ (j^*, s^*) = \arg\min_{j,\,s}\ \Big[\frac{n_L}{n_v}\,G(L) + \frac{n_R}{n_v}\,G(R)\Big], \qquad L = \{x_j \le s\},\ R = \{x_j > s\} \]
      • \(\hat\pi_c\): share of class \(c\) in the node; \(n_v\), \(n_L\), \(n_R\): observations in the node and in its two children
      • \(G = 0\) for a pure node; with two classes \(G = 2\hat\pi(1-\hat\pi)\), at most \(0.5\) at 50/50; a leaf predicts the share of class 1
    • Random forest (RF, Breiman (2001)): \(\hat f_{RF}(\mathbf{x}) = \frac1B\sum_{b=1}^{B} T_b(\mathbf{x})\)
      • each tree \(T_b\) grown on a bootstrap sample with a random subset of features at each split
      \[ \Var\big(\hat f_{RF}(\mathbf{x})\big) = \rho\,\sigma_T^2 + \frac{1-\rho}{B}\,\sigma_T^2 \]
      • \(\sigma_T^2\): variance of one tree; \(\rho\): correlation between two trees (Hastie, Tibshirani & Friedman (2009), Ch. 15)
      • random feature subsets lower \(\rho\), the only term that more trees cannot remove
      • the out-of-bag error is optimistic when labels overlap

    Loss, Penalty and Five Model Families (5/5)

    Slide 12 in the PDF
    • Gradient boosting machine (GBM, Friedman (2001))
      • add, step by step, a small tree that corrects the errors of the current model
      \[ r_{im} = -\frac{\partial L(y_i, F)}{\partial F}\bigg|_{F = F_{m-1}(\mathbf{x}_i)}, \qquad F_m(\mathbf{x}) = F_{m-1}(\mathbf{x}) + \eta\, h_m(\mathbf{x}) \]
      • \(F_m\): the model after \(m\) steps; \(h_m\): the tree fitted to the pseudo-residuals \(r_{im}\); for the log-loss on the logit score, \(r_{im} = y_i - \hat p_{m-1}(\mathbf{x}_i)\)
      • \(\eta \in (0, 1]\): learning rate; a small \(\eta\) learns slowly and overfits less
    • Multilayer perceptron (MLP): hidden layers \(\mathbf{h}^{(\ell)} = g\big(\mathbf{W}^{(\ell)}\mathbf{h}^{(\ell-1)} + \mathbf{b}^{(\ell)}\big)\), \(\mathbf{h}^{(0)} = \mathbf{x}\)
      • the output: \(\hat p = 1/\big(1 + e^{-(\mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{h}^{(D)} + b)}\big)\)
      • \(\mathbf{W}^{(\ell)}\), \(\mathbf{b}^{(\ell)}\): weights and biases of layer \(\ell\); \(D\): number of hidden layers; \(g(z) = \max(0, z)\): the ReLU (rectified linear unit), element by element
      • the weights minimise the log-loss by gradient descent
      • early stopping: a chronological validation set (the last 10% of the training data, purged); training stops when the validation log-loss stops falling
      • richer architectures are in Chapter 14

      Features: Fractional Differencing and Its Pitfall

      Stationarity, Memory and the Fractional Difference (1/2)

      Slide 15 in the PDF
      • The question of this section
        • how do we turn a price into a stationary feature without erasing its memory?
      • Log prices \(X_t = \log P_t\) behave as \(I(1)\)
        • \(P_t\): closing price on day \(t\)
      • Returns \(\Delta\log P_t = \log P_t - \log P_{t-1}\) are \(I(0)\) but erase the memory of the price level
      • Integration order: a series \(X_t\) is \(I(\delta)\) if \((1-B)^{\delta} X_t\) is \(I(0)\)
        • \(B\): backshift operator, \(B X_t = X_{t-1}\)
        • \(I(0)\): stationary with short memory; \(I(1)\): a random walk type series, stationary after one difference
        • \(\delta\) may be fractional: the series is stationary only for \(\delta < \tfrac12\), with long memory when \(0 < \delta < \tfrac12\) (Hosking (1981))
      • Idea of fractional differencing: difference by less than 1
        • removing the unit root while keeping part of the memory

      Stationarity, Memory and the Fractional Difference (2/2)

      Slide 16 in the PDF
      Definition (Fractional difference of order \(d\)).
      • \(\tilde X_t = (1-B)^d X_t = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} w_k X_{t-k}\), with \(w_0 = 1\) and \(w_k = -\,w_{k-1}\,\frac{d-k+1}{k}\)
      • Notation
        • \(d\): differencing order chosen by the analyst; \(k\): lag in days
        • \(w_k\): weight of the value \(k\) days back, from the binomial expansion of \((1-B)^d\)
      • \(d = 1\): \(w = (1, -1, 0, \dots)\), ordinary returns
      • \(0 < d < 1\): weights decay hyperbolically, so memory is kept
      • An \(I(1)\) log price differenced with \(d\) is \(I(1-d)\)
        • non-stationary for \(d \le \frac12\)
        • stationary with long memory for \(\frac12 < d < 1\)
      • The infinite weights sum to \((1-1)^d = 0\)
        • the level cancels only in the limit (asymptotics in the Appendix)
      • López de Prado (2018) proposes the minimum \(d\) at which an ADF (Augmented Dickey–Fuller) unit-root test rejects
        • test equation and critical value on the ADF slide; this section shows why that rule fails

      The Fixed-Width Window (FFD)

      Slide 17 in the PDF
      Definition (Fixed-width-window fractional differentiation (FFD)).
      • Truncate the weights: keep \(w_0,\dots,w_{K}\) with \(|w_k| \ge \tau\) (e.g. \(\tau = 10^{-4}\)), drop the rest (López de Prado (2018))
      • \(\tilde X_t = \sum_{k=0}^{K} w_k X_{t-k}\) for every \(t > K\)
      • \(\tau\): smallest weight kept; \(K\): the last lag kept, i.e. the window length
        • the first \(K\) days have too little history and are dropped
      • Every \(\tilde X_t\) uses the same number of lags
        • only past values enter \(\Rightarrow\) no look-ahead
      • The smaller \(d\), the more lags are needed
        • \(d = 0.25\), \(\tau = 10^{-4}\) \(\Rightarrow\) \(K = 444\) lags
      • The truncated weights no longer sum to zero
        • \(\sum_{k=0}^{K} w_k = 0.178\) at \(d = 0.25\) (0.040 at \(d = 0.5\))

      The ADF Statistic Along \(d\): S&P 500 (1/3)

      Slide 18 in the PDF
      • Augmented Dickey–Fuller (ADF) unit-root test: the \(t\)-statistic of \(\gamma\) in
        \[ \Delta\tilde X_t = c + \gamma\,\tilde X_{t-1} + \sum_{i=1}^{p}\phi_i\,\Delta\tilde X_{t-i} + e_t \]
        • \(H_0\): \(\gamma = 0\), a unit root; \(p\): number of lagged differences, which absorb the autocorrelation of \(e_t\)
        • with a constant, reject \(H_0\) at 5% below \(\approx -2.86\)
      • The rule of López de Prado (2018)
        • compute the FFD series on a grid of \(d\) between 0 and 1
        • pick the smallest \(d\) at which the ADF rejects
      • Next chart: \(d\) on the horizontal axis
        • the ADF statistic (blue) on the left axis
        • the correlation of the FFD series with the log price (green) on the right axis
        • red dashed line: the 5% critical value; vertical line: \(d^* = 0.25\)

      The ADF Statistic Along \(d\): S&P 500 (2/3)

      Slide 19 in the PDF
        Question for the room At d = 0.25, ADF with one lag rejects the unit root. Is this rejection sufficient to treat the FFD series as stationary?
        Answer

        ADF with one lag rejects: −2.96 < −2.86. With 34 AIC-selected lags, the statistic is −0.72 and does not reject. Neither decision is a proof. The structural argument is that, for an I(1) log price, a finite filter whose weights sum to 0.178 retains the I(1) component. The 0.98 correlation with the log price is a warning sign, not a stationarity test.

        Truncated FFD of an \(I(1)\) Price Is Still \(I(1)\)

        Slide 21 in the PDF
        • Decompose the FFD series:
          \[ \tilde X_t = \sum_{k=0}^{K} w_k X_{t-k} = \Big(\sum_{k=0}^{K} w_k\Big) X_t - \sum_{k=1}^{K} w_k\,(X_t - X_{t-k}) \]
          • first term: today's level times the sum of the truncated weights; second term: a weighted sum of the \(k\)-day changes \(X_t - X_{t-k}\)
        • If \(X_t\) is \(I(1)\), each \(X_t - X_{t-k}\) is \(I(0)\)
          • the first term is \(0.178\, X_t\) at \(d = 0.25\): a scaled random walk
        • The unit-root component has small variance next to the stationary part
          • so ADF with few lags confuses the two

        • Consequence: the FFD log-price in the case study is a scaled price level, not a stationary feature

        From Fixed-Horizon to Triple-Barrier Labels (1/2)

        Slide 22 in the PDF
        • The question of this section
          • which label reproduces a trader's real decision, with take-profit, stop-loss and a maximum holding period?
        • Fixed horizon: the label is the sign of the \(h\)-day log return
          • with a dead zone around zero
          \[ r_{t,t+h} = \log P_{t+h} - \log P_t, \qquad y_t = \begin{cases} +1 & \text{if } r_{t,t+h} > \tau \\ -1 & \text{if } r_{t,t+h} < -\tau \\ \phantom{+}0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases} \]
          • \(P_t\): closing price on day \(t\); \(h\): horizon in days; \(\tau \ge 0\): threshold, moves smaller than \(\tau\) get the label 0
        • Three problems
          • a constant \(\tau\) ignores time-varying volatility
          • the path is ignored: a stop-loss could have been hit before \(t + h\)
          • the classes are unbalanced: S&P 500, \(h = 10\), \(\tau = 0\): 60.5% up vs. 39.5% down

        From Fixed-Horizon to Triple-Barrier Labels (2/2)

        Slide 23 in the PDF
        Definition (Triple-barrier label, López de Prado (2018)).
        • At event time \(t_0\), with daily volatility \(\sigma_{t_0}\) and horizon \(h\)
          • upper barrier \(\log P_t - \log P_{t_0} \ge \pi_u \sigma_{t_0}\sqrt{h}\) (profit-taking)
          • lower barrier \(\le -\pi_l \sigma_{t_0}\sqrt{h}\) (stop-loss), vertical barrier \(t = t_0 + h\)
        • Label = first barrier touched at \(t_1\)
          • \(+1\) upper, \(-1\) lower; at the vertical barrier \(\operatorname{sign}(r_{t_0,t_1})\) (or \(0\))
        • \(\sigma_{t_0}\sqrt{h}\): the typical \(h\)-day move; \(\pi_u, \pi_l > 0\): barrier widths in units of it
        • Each label has a span \([t_0, t_1]\): the input of purging in validation
        • \(\sigma_{t_0}\): exponentially weighted moving average (EWMA) volatility, span 50
          • square root of the EWMA of squared deviations of daily log returns
          \[ \sigma_t^2 \approx \alpha\sum_{k \ge 0}(1-\alpha)^k\,\big(r_{t-k} - \bar r_t\big)^2, \qquad \alpha = \frac{2}{\text{span} + 1} = \frac{2}{51} \approx 0.039 \]
          • \(\bar r_t\): the exponentially weighted mean return; span 50: an effective memory of about two trading months
        • Why volatility-scaled barriers
          • a fixed threshold would be hit almost daily in 2008 or March 2020 and rarely in calm years

          Leakage: What Crosses the Train/Test Boundary

          Slide 28 in the PDF
          • The question of this section
            • why does ordinary \(K\)-fold overstate a model's skill on financial series?
          Definition (Leakage).
          • The training set contains information that is also present in the test set
            • so the test score no longer measures performance on genuinely new data
          • Serially correlated features (\(\mathbf{x}_t \approx \mathbf{x}_{t+1}\)) and overlapping labels (\(y_t \approx y_{t+1}\))
            • a shuffled \(K\)-fold puts near-identical days on both sides
          • A flexible model (RF, GBM) memorises the neighbour \(\Rightarrow\) excellent “out-of-sample” score
            • tuning on a leaky CV selects the most overfitted model
          • Other sources of leakage
            • transformations fitted on the full sample (scaling, principal component analysis, the choice of \(d\) for FFD)
            • survivorship bias, data revisions, wrong timestamps

          Purged K-Fold with Embargo (1/2)

          Slide 29 in the PDF
          • Folds are contiguous blocks in time (López de Prado (2018))
            • test fold \(k\) holds the observations \(t^{a}_k, \dots, t^{b}_k\)
            • their labels end at the latest at \(T_k = \max_{i \in \text{test}_k} t_{1,i}\)
          • The training set keeps only the observations that do not touch the test:
            \[ \mathcal{A}_k = \Big\{ i \notin \text{test}_k:\ [t_{0,i}, t_{1,i}] \cap [t^{a}_k, T_k] = \emptyset \ \text{ and } \ t_{0,i} \notin (T_k,\, T_k + e] \Big\} \]
            • the first condition is purging: it removes labels that overlap the span of the test labels
            • the second is the embargo: \(e\) observations after the end of the last test label, \(T_k\) (e.g. 1%), needed because features look back in time
          • The purged CV score is the mean of \(K\) scores
            • each from a model fitted on \(\mathcal{A}_k\) and evaluated on fold \(k\)
          • With labels of length \(h\), a boundary removes \(h\) observations before and \(h + e\) after the test block
            • an interior fold loses \(2h + e\) observations
            • a 10-observation example: Appendix
          • Next chart: five folds in rows, observation index on the horizontal axis
            • blue train, red test, purple purged, brown embargo

                Walk-Forward Backtest: Strategy and Design

                Slide 41 in the PDF
                • The question
                  • would a trader following the model have earned more, after costs, than by simply holding the index?

                  • decided with information up to \(t\)

                  • the decision at the close of day \(t\) is traded at the close of day \(t+1\)

                • Exposure \(\bar w = \frac{1}{T}\sum_t w_t\): share of the \(T\) days spent invested

                  • both Sharpe ratios use daily log returns with risk-free rate 0, annualised with \(\sqrt{252}\)

                    Selection Bias and the False Strategy Theorem

                    Slide 48 in the PDF
                    • The question of this section
                      • how large is the best Sharpe ratio among \(N\) trials when none has skill?
                    • A researcher tries \(N\) strategies and reports the best: selection bias
                      • even if all have zero true Sharpe ratio, \(\max_n \widehat{SR}_n\) grows with \(N\)
                    • Harvey, Liu & Zhu (2016): given hundreds of published factors, a new factor needs \(t > 3\), not 2
                    Theorem (False Strategy Theorem, Bailey & López de Prado (2014)).
                    • The expected maximum Sharpe ratio of \(N\) strategies with no skill
                      • if \(\widehat{SR}_n\), \(n=1,\dots,N\), are independent and approximately \(N\big(0, \Var[\widehat{SR}]\big)\), then
                      \[ SR_0 \equiv \E\Big[\max_n \widehat{SR}_n\Big] \approx \sqrt{\Var[\widehat{SR}]}\,\Big[(1-\gamma_E)\,\Phi^{-1}\Big(1-\tfrac{1}{N}\Big) + \gamma_E\,\Phi^{-1}\Big(1-\tfrac{1}{N e}\Big)\Big] \]
                    • Notation
                      • \(\widehat{SR}_n\): estimated Sharpe ratio of strategy \(n\); \(\Phi\): standard Normal distribution function; \(\Phi^{-1}\): its quantile function
                      • \(\Var[\widehat{SR}]\): sampling variance of one estimated SR, \(\approx 1/T\) per period for i.i.d. returns, \(T\) the number of return periods
                      • \(\gamma_E \approx 0.5772\): Euler–Mascheroni constant
                    • \(SR_0\) is the Sharpe ratio the luckiest of \(N\) worthless strategies is expected to show
                      • it rises with \(N\) and with the sampling noise \(\sqrt{\Var[\widehat{SR}]}\)

                        Sharpe Ratio Inference: Standard Error and PSR (1/2)

                        Slide 52 in the PDF
                        • The question
                          • how precise is an estimated Sharpe ratio?
                          • how do we compare it with a benchmark?
                        • Per-period Sharpe ratio of daily log returns, risk-free rate 0: \(\widehat{SR} = \bar r/\hat\sigma\)
                        • Shape of the distribution, with \(\mu\), \(\sigma\) the mean and SD of \(r\)
                          • skewness \(\gamma_3 = \E[(r-\mu)^3]/\sigma^3\)
                          • ordinary kurtosis \(\gamma_4 = \E[(r-\mu)^4]/\sigma^4\) (\(3\) for the Normal distribution)
                        • Sampling variance of \(\widehat{SR}\), i.i.d. returns, delta method (Lo (2002); Opdyke (2007)):
                          \[ \Var(\widehat{SR}) \approx \frac{1}{T}\Big(1 - \gamma_3\, SR + \frac{\gamma_4 - 1}{4}\, SR^2\Big) \]
                          • \(T\): number of returns; \(SR\): true per-period Sharpe ratio
                          • negative skewness and fat tails widen the uncertainty; Normal distribution (\(\gamma_3 = 0\), \(\gamma_4 = 3\)): \((1 + \frac12 SR^2)/T\)
                          • the formula requires a finite fourth moment, which Chapter 1 questions

                        Sharpe Ratio Inference: Standard Error and PSR (2/2)

                        Slide 53 in the PDF
                        Definition (Probabilistic Sharpe Ratio (PSR), Bailey & López de Prado (2012)).
                        • Called by its authors the probability that the true SR exceeds a benchmark \(SR^*\)
                          • formally \(\Phi\) of a \(z\)-statistic, with the variance above in the denominator:
                          \[ \widehat{PSR}(SR^*) = \Phi\left( \frac{(\widehat{SR} - SR^*)\sqrt{T-1}}{\sqrt{1 - \hat\gamma_3\,\widehat{SR} + \frac{\hat\gamma_4 - 1}{4}\,\widehat{SR}^2}} \right) \]
                        • \(SR^*\): the benchmark, e.g. 0 or the expected maximum \(SR_0\)
                          • \(\hat\gamma_3\), \(\hat\gamma_4\): sample skewness and kurtosis
                        • Reading: \(\widehat{PSR} = 1 - p\), with \(p\) the one-sided p-value of \(H_0: SR \le SR^*\)
                        • \(\widehat{PSR} > 0.95\) means rejecting \(H_0\) at 5%, not a 95% probability for the true SR
                          • under i.i.d. returns and the delta-method approximation

                        Machine Learning for Inference

                        Selection Is Not Inference: Post-Double Selection (1/2)

                        Slide 55 in the PDF
                        • The question of this section
                          • how do we get a valid confidence interval for the effect of one variable after ML has chosen the controls?
                        • The LASSO path answers “which feature enters first”, not “which feature matters”
                          • OLS \(t\)-statistics after LASSO ignore the selection step
                        • A control correlated with the regressor of interest \(d_t\) but only weakly with \(y_t\) gets dropped
                          • its effect moves into the coefficient of \(d_t\) (omitted-variable bias)
                        • Partially linear model: the effect of \(d_t\) is linear
                          • the controls enter through unknown functions
                          \[ y_t = \theta\, d_t + g(\mathbf{x}_t) + \varepsilon_t, \qquad d_t = m(\mathbf{x}_t) + v_t \]
                          • \(y_t\): outcome; \(d_t\): regressor of interest; \(\theta\): its effect, the parameter to estimate
                          • \(\mathbf{x}_t\): the \(p\) controls; \(g\): their effect on \(y_t\); \(m\): how they predict \(d_t\); \(\varepsilon_t\), \(v_t\): errors
                          • approximately sparse: a few controls explain most of \(g\) and \(m\)

                        Selection Is Not Inference: Post-Double Selection (2/2)

                        Slide 56 in the PDF
                        • Post-double selection (Belloni, Chernozhukov & Hansen (2014)):
                          1. LASSO of \(y_t\) on \(\mathbf{x}_t\) \(\Rightarrow\) selected set \(\hat S_y\)
                          2. LASSO of \(d_t\) on \(\mathbf{x}_t\) \(\Rightarrow\) selected set \(\hat S_d\)
                          3. OLS of \(y_t\) on \(d_t\) and \(\mathbf{x}_{\hat S_y \cup \hat S_d}\), robust standard error
                        • \(\hat S_y\), \(\hat S_d\): indices of the controls kept by each LASSO
                          • \(\mathbf{x}_{\hat S_y \cup \hat S_d}\): the controls kept by either
                        • A control is missed only if it matters for neither equation
                          • so the omitted bias is second order
                        • Valid under independent sampling and sparsity
                          • with HAC errors an approximation for time series
                          • penalty and conditions in the Appendix

                        Double/Debiased Machine Learning (DML): Orthogonal Scores and Cross-Fitting

                        Slide 57 in the PDF
                        • Chernozhukov et al. (2018): residualise both variables with any learner (LASSO, RF, boosting)
                          • then regress residual on residual
                          \[ \tilde y_t = y_t - \hat\ell(\mathbf{x}_t), \quad \tilde v_t = d_t - \hat m(\mathbf{x}_t), \qquad \hat\theta = \frac{\sum_t \tilde v_t\, \tilde y_t}{\sum_t \tilde v_t^2} \]
                          • \(\hat\ell(\mathbf{x}) = \hat\E[y \mid \mathbf{x}]\): ML prediction of \(y_t\) from the controls; \(\hat m(\mathbf{x})\): ML prediction of \(d_t\)
                          • \(\tilde y_t\), \(\tilde v_t\): the parts of \(y_t\) and \(d_t\) the controls do not explain; \(\hat\theta\): OLS slope of \(\tilde y_t\) on \(\tilde v_t\)
                        • Neyman-orthogonal score \(\psi = (\tilde y - \theta \tilde v)\,\tilde v\)
                          • its mean is zero at the true \(\theta\)
                          • first-order errors in \(\hat\ell\) and \(\hat m\) do not bias \(\hat\theta\) (rate conditions in the Appendix)
                        • Cross-fitting: fit \(\hat\ell\), \(\hat m\) on \(K-1\) folds, residualise the held-out fold, repeat
                          • removes the own-observation overfitting bias
                        • The published theory assumes independent sampling
                          • with overlapping 5-day labels we use contiguous folds purged by 5 days around the test fold and HAC standard errors
                          • an adaptation to serial dependence outside the published theory

                            Evidence, and the Economic Value of a Small \(R^2_{OOS}\) (1/2)

                            Slide 73 in the PDF
                            • Campbell & Thompson (2008): what a small \(R^2\) is worth to an investor
                              • returns have a predictable and an unpredictable part
                              \[ r_{t+1} = \mu + x_t + \varepsilon_{t+1}, \qquad R^2 = \frac{\Var(x_t)}{\Var(r_{t+1})} \]
                              • \(\mu\): mean return; \(x_t\): predictable component, known at \(t\), mean zero; \(\varepsilon_{t+1}\): unpredictable shock
                              • \(R^2\): share of the return variance explained by the predictor
                            • An unconstrained mean-variance investor who knows \(x_t\) and re-optimises every period
                              • no costs, no estimation error; \(SR_t\): the conditional Sharpe ratio at \(t\)
                              \[ \E[SR_t^{2}] = \frac{SR_{BH}^2 + R^2}{1 - R^2} \]
                              • \(\E[SR_t^2]\) is not, in general, the square of the unconditional Sharpe ratio of the dynamic strategy
                              • the optimal mean-variance utility at \(t\) is \(SR_t^2/(2\gamma)\), \(\gamma\) the risk aversion, so the gain reads as mean utility
                              • \(SR_{BH}\): SR of buy-and-hold, which ignores \(x_t\); \(R^2 = 0\) gives \(\E[SR_t^2] = SR_{BH}^2\)

                            Where the Campbell–Thompson Formula Comes From

                            Slide 75 in the PDF
                            • Same model: \(r_{t+1} = \mu + x_t + \varepsilon_{t+1}\)
                              • \(\E[x_t] = 0\), \(\varepsilon_{t+1}\) independent of \(x_t\)
                              • write \(\sigma_r^2 = \Var(r_{t+1})\)
                            • Step 1, the risk the informed investor still bears
                              • \(\Var(r_{t+1}) = \Var(x_t) + \sigma_\varepsilon^2\) and \(R^2 = \Var(x_t)/\sigma_r^2\), so
                              \[ \sigma_\varepsilon^2 = (1 - R^2)\,\sigma_r^2 \]
                            • Step 2, the investor's conditional Sharpe ratio at \(t\)
                              • mean \(\mu + x_t\), variance \(\sigma_\varepsilon^2\), squared SR \((\mu + x_t)^2/\sigma_\varepsilon^2\)
                            • Step 3, average over \(t\): \(\E[(\mu + x_t)^2] = \mu^2 + \Var(x_t) = \mu^2 + R^2\sigma_r^2\)
                            • Step 4, divide and use \(SR_{BH} = \mu/\sigma_r\):
                              \[ \E[SR_t^2] = \frac{\mu^2 + R^2\sigma_r^2}{(1 - R^2)\,\sigma_r^2} = \frac{SR_{BH}^2 + R^2}{1 - R^2} \]
                            • So the formula gives the average squared conditional SR, not the unconditional SR of the strategy
                              • for an investor who re-optimises each month, in monthly units
                            • Numbers, \(R^2 = 0.005\)
                              • \(\E[SR_t^2] = (0.128^2 + 0.005)/0.995 = 0.02149\), \(\sqrt{\E[SR_t^2]} = 0.1466\) a month
                              • \(\times\sqrt{12} = 0.51\) a year; \(0.1466/0.128 = 1.15\)

                            Memory Estimated Directly: Local Whittle (1/3)

                            Slide 78 in the PDF
                            • Idea: a long-memory series has most of its variance in slow cycles
                              • the slope of its spectrum near frequency 0 reveals \(\delta\)
                              \[ f(\lambda) \sim G\,\lambda^{-2\delta} \quad \text{as } \lambda \to 0 \]
                              • \(f(\lambda)\): spectral density, the variance contributed by cycles of frequency \(\lambda\) (period \(2\pi/\lambda\) days)
                              • \(G > 0\): scale constant; \(\sim\): the ratio of the two sides tends to 1
                              • \(\delta > 0\): \(f\) explodes near 0 (long memory); \(\delta = 0\): flat (short memory); \(\delta < 0\): \(f\) vanishes near 0
                            • Local Whittle (LW, Robinson (1995)): fits this law only on the first \(m = n^{a}\) Fourier frequencies
                              • \(\lambda_j = 2\pi j/n\), \(j = 1,\dots,m\)
                              • \(n\): sample size; \(a \in (0,1)\): bandwidth exponent; a larger \(a\) uses more frequencies: less variance, more bias from short-run dynamics
                            • Exact local Whittle (ELW, Shimotsu & Phillips (2005)): also valid for non-stationary \(\delta \ge \frac12\)
                              • both estimators in the Appendix
                            • 95% confidence interval (CI): \(\hat\delta \pm 1.96/(2\sqrt m)\)
                              • the standard error \(1/(2\sqrt m)\) depends only on \(m\)

                            Overlapping Labels and Sample Uniqueness (1/2)

                            Slide 84 in the PDF
                            • Label \(i\) spans \([t_{0,i}, t_{1,i}]\)
                              • consecutive labels share returns \(\Rightarrow\) samples are not i.i.d.
                            • Concurrency: the number of labels whose span contains day \(t\)
                              \[ c_t = \sum_i \mathbf{1}\{t_{0,i} \le t \le t_{1,i}\} \]

                              • \(\bar u_i = 1\): no shared return; \(\bar u_i = 1/5\): the harmonic mean of \(c_t\) over the span of label \(i\) is five

                            Overlapping Labels and the Newey–West Long-Run Variance

                            Slide 87 in the PDF
                            • The significance tests of the case study (Pesaran–Timmermann) average a daily series \(z_t\)
                              • \(z_t\): a hit, a loss differential \(d_t\), a regression score
                              • with 5-day labels, neighbouring \(z_t\) share returns, so \(\Var(\bar z) \ne \Var(z_t)/n\)
                            • Newey–West (Newey & West (1987)) heteroskedasticity and autocorrelation consistent (HAC) long-run variance
                              • with Bartlett weights:
                              \[ \hat\Omega_{NW} = \hat\Gamma_0 + 2\sum_{j=1}^{q}\Big(1 - \frac{j}{q+1}\Big)\hat\Gamma_j, \qquad \hat\Gamma_j = \frac1n\sum_{t=j+1}^{n} (z_t - \bar z)(z_{t-j} - \bar z) \]
                              • \(\hat\Gamma_j\): sample autocovariance of \(z_t\) at lag \(j\); \(q\): number of lags (“lag 4” in the tables); \(\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}(\bar z) = \sqrt{\hat\Omega_{NW}/n}\)
                              • lag rule: \(h\)-day labels share returns up to \(h - 1\) days apart, so \(q \ge h - 1 = 4\) is the minimum here
                            • Toy example: \(z_t\) = sum of the next 5 i.i.d. daily returns of variance \(\sigma^2\)
                              • \(\Gamma_j = (5 - j)\sigma^2\) for \(j \le 4\), \(0\) beyond
                              • i.i.d. formula: \(\Gamma_0 = 5\sigma^2\); true long-run variance \(\Gamma_0 + 2(4 + 3 + 2 + 1)\sigma^2 = 25\sigma^2\): an i.i.d. \(t\) is \(\sqrt5 \approx 2.2\) times too large
                              • Bartlett with \(q = 4\): \(5 + 2(0.8\cdot4 + 0.6\cdot3 + 0.4\cdot2 + 0.2\cdot1) = 17\sigma^2\); the weights damp far lags, so \(q = h-1\) is a floor, not a generous choice

                            Comparing Forecasts: Diebold–Mariano, Clark–West, Giacomini–White (1/2)

                            Slide 88 in the PDF

                              • model 1 gives \(\hat p_{1,t}\)

                            Feature Importance: MDI and MDA

                            Slide 90 in the PDF
                            • The question
                              • which features does the model rely on?
                              • which of them help prediction on new data?
                            • Mean decrease impurity (MDI), trees only, computed in sample:
                              \[ \mathrm{MDI}_j = \frac1B\sum_{b=1}^{B}\ \sum_{v \in T_b:\ j(v) = j} \frac{n_v}{n}\Big[G_v - \frac{n_{L}}{n_v}\,G_{L} - \frac{n_{R}}{n_v}\,G_{R}\Big] \]
                              • the sum of the Gini reductions of the nodes \(v\) that split on feature \(j\), weighted by the share \(n_v/n\) of observations reaching the node
                              • favours continuous features with many possible thresholds
                            • Mean decrease accuracy (MDA), any model, on test data:
                              \[ \mathrm{MDA}_j = S\big(\hat f;\, \mathcal{D}_{test}\big) - \frac1R\sum_{r=1}^{R} S\big(\hat f;\, \mathcal{D}_{test}^{\pi_r(j)}\big) \]
                              • \(S\): the score (here AUC); \(\mathcal{D}_{test}^{\pi_r(j)}\): the test data with column \(j\) randomly permuted, repetition \(r\)
                              • \(\mathrm{MDA}_j < 0\): permuting raised the score; read it with the spread over the \(R\) permutations and the estimation uncertainty
                              • a small negative MDA alone does not show that a feature hurts

                            Correlated Trials in the DSR: Alternative Approximations

                            Slide 96 in the PDF
                            Two approximations with different assumptions
                            • (a) raw \(N\), with \(\Var[\widehat{SR}_n]\) = dispersion of the trial SRs: row 1 of the Bitcoin DSR table
                            • (b) \(\Var[\widehat{SR}_n] = 1/T\), with an effective number of independent trials \(\hat N < N\)
                              • Seminar 13, exercise B6
                            • Used together, dispersion and \(\hat N\) can count the same dependence effect twice (toy model below)
                            • Why (a) works, toy model: \(N\) zero-skill trials \(\widehat{SR}_n = \sigma(\sqrt\rho\,Z + \sqrt{1-\rho}\,e_n)\)
                              • each of variance \(\sigma^2 = 1/T\), independent standard Normal \(Z,e_1,\ldots,e_N\), pairwise correlation \(\rho\)
                              • the common shock moves all trials together, so only \(\sqrt{1-\rho}\,\sigma\, e_n\) creates selection: \(\E[\max_n \widehat{SR}_n] = \sqrt{1-\rho}\;\times\) the independent-trial value
                              • the dispersion across trials is \((1-\rho)\sigma^2\), so (a) captures this scaling
                              • the two rows have SD ratio \(0.0109/0.0220 = 0.50\), which this toy model reads as \(\rho \approx 1 - 0.50^2 \approx 0.75\)
                            • The dispersion also contains true differences between rules
                              • with real rules (a) is an approximation
                            • Recommendation: report the conservative row and either (a) or (b), with \(N\)
                              • call a rule a discovery only if it also passes the conservative row

                            Probability of Backtest Overfitting (PBO)

                            Slide 101 in the PDF
                            Combinatorially symmetric cross-validation (CSCV): Bailey, Borwein, López de Prado & Zhu (2017)
                            1. Matrix of returns: \(T\) periods \(\times\) \(N\) configurations; split the rows into \(S\) contiguous blocks
                            2. For each of the \(\binom{S}{S/2}\) choices, half of the blocks are in sample (IS), the rest out of sample (OOS)
                            3. Pick the best IS configuration \(n^*\)
                              • rank all \(N\) configurations OOS in ascending order
                              • set \(\bar\omega = \text{rank}(n^*)/(N+1) \in (0,1)\)
                            4. Logit \(\lambda = \log\frac{\bar\omega}{1-\bar\omega}\); \(PBO = \Pr[\lambda \le 0]\)
                              • the share of splits in which the IS winner ranks at or below the OOS median
                            • \(\bar\omega\): relative OOS rank of the IS winner, near 1 if it stays the best
                              • \(\lambda > 0\): above the OOS median
                            • \(PBO \approx 0\): the IS winner reliably stays above the OOS median
                              • \(PBO \approx 0.5\) or more: selecting the IS winner is no better than picking at random
                            • PBO evaluates the research procedure, not one strategy
                              • it is not the probability of losing money
                              • like DSR it needs every trial logged (Seminar 13, exercise B12)

                            From the Pricing Condition to the Beta Representation

                            Slide 102 in the PDF
                            • Three steps (written unconditionally; conditionally, replace \(E\) by \(E_t\))
                              1. With \(M = 1 - F\): \(E[(1 - F)R^e_i] = 0\), so \(E[R^e_i] = E[R^e_i F]\) for every stock

                              • step 1 is a first-moment condition

                            Fractional Weights and Their Asymptotics

                            Slide 114 in the PDF
                            Definition (Fractional difference operator).
                            • For a real order \(d\):
                              \[ (1-B)^d = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \binom{d}{k}(-B)^k, \qquad \tilde X_t = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} w_k X_{t-k} \]
                            • Weights: \(w_0 = 1\), \(w_k = -\,w_{k-1}\,\frac{d-k+1}{k}\)
                              • for \(d \notin \{0, 1, 2, \dots\}\): \(w_k = \frac{\Gamma(k-d)}{\Gamma(-d)\,\Gamma(k+1)}\) and \(w_k \sim \frac{k^{-(1+d)}}{\Gamma(-d)}\) as \(k \to \infty\)
                            • \(\binom{d}{k} = d(d-1)\cdots(d-k+1)/k!\): the binomial coefficient, defined for any real \(d\)
                              • \(\Gamma\): the gamma function, \(\Gamma(k+1) = k!\)
                            • The weights decay like \(k^{-(1+d)}\), more slowly for smaller \(d\)
                            • For \(0 < d < 1\): \(\sum_{k=0}^{\infty} w_k = (1-1)^d = 0\)
                              • it follows from the binomial series at \(B = 1\), which converges for \(d > 0\)
                              • \(d = 0\): \(w_0 = 1\) and the rest zero, so the sum is 1; \(d = 1\): \(w = (1, -1)\), sum 0
                              • for \(d < 0\) the series of weights does not converge

                            Local Whittle and Exact Local Whittle (1/2)

                            Slide 115 in the PDF

                              • at the Fourier frequency \(\lambda_j = 2\pi j/n\)

                              • e.g. both errors \(o_P(n^{-1/4})\): each learner may converge more slowly than a parametric model, because only the product of the errors matters

                            Slide 133 in the PDF
                            • Metrics (II.F): the SR of \(F\) and two fit measures
                              • \(\hat\epsilon_{t+1,i}\): pricing error, the part of \(R^e_{t+1,i}\) not explained by \(\hat\beta_{t,i}F_{t+1}\)
                            • Explained variation (EV)
                              • one minus the average squared pricing error over the average squared excess return
                              \[ \text{EV} = 1 - \frac{\overline{\hat\epsilon^2}}{\overline{(R^e)^2}} \]
                              • overline: time average of the monthly cross-sectional means; EV \(= 1\): the factor explains all variation
                            • Cross-sectional \(R^2\): how well the model explains average returns
                              \[ \text{XS-}R^2 = 1 - \frac{\frac1N\sum_i \frac{T_i}{T}\,\big(\bar{\hat\epsilon}_i\big)^2}{\frac1N\sum_i \frac{T_i}{T}\,\big(\bar R^e_i\big)^2} \]
