The chapter asks two questions: what does trading cost, and how do prices incorporate information, when orders are matched by computers in microseconds? The most direct cost is the effective spread, measured from trades and quotes. Roll (1984) showed that in an efficient market the spread can be inferred from prices alone, from the autocovariance of price changes. The Corwin–Schultz, Abdi–Ranaldo and EDGE estimators build on the same idea of inferring the cost from prices alone.

For SPY, a tick of USD 0.01 is 0.21 bp. It is a floor for the quoted spread, not for the effective one. From daily bars, September 2024 to September 2026, the estimates lie two orders of magnitude above a tick: Roll 64.3 bp, Corwin–Schultz 26.5 bp, Abdi–Ranaldo 55.1 bp and EDGE 40.1 bp. From 5-minute bars, over 1,477 days in 2020–2026, EDGE gives 0.46 bp, with interval [0.41, 0.51] bp, the closest to one tick. The daily estimators are dominated by volatility, not by the cost of trading.

The same problem appears across markets. For 25 assets from the US, the Bucharest Stock Exchange (BVB) and crypto, the median Corwin–Schultz estimate is 53 bp in the US and 54 bp on the BVB, although the median daily traded value is USD 10,505 million in the US and USD 0.34 million on the BVB. The Amihud illiquidity ratio separates the two markets: for Banca Transilvania, the most liquid BVB stock, it is 28.0 bp per USD 1 million, about 17,600 times the value for SPY. The chapter therefore builds the liquidity measures from the limit order book to the Glosten–Milgrom and Kyle models, which explain the spread and price impact through information, and to the optimal execution of a large order.

The Limit Order Book

Liquidity and the Limit Order Book: Definitions

Slide 3 in the PDF
  • Question: what does a market order buy, and why does its cost depend on the shape of the book?
  • Liquidity: trading a quantity quickly at a price close to fair value
    • no single number captures it
    • Tightness (the spread), depth (size needed to move the price), resiliency (speed of recovery) Kyle (1985); immediacy Harris (2003)
  • Limit order book (LOB): all standing limit orders, by price and time of arrival Gould et al. (2013)
  • At time \(t\): best bid \(b_t\) < best ask \(a_t\)
    • mid-price \(m_t = (a_t + b_t)/2\)
    • relative spread \((a_t - b_t)/m_t\) in basis points (1 bp = 0.01%)
    • Depth: quantity waiting at a price level; tick size: smallest price step (USD 0.01 for most US stocks above USD 1)

Orders, Priority and Matching

Slide 4 in the PDF
  • Market order: immediate execution against the available liquidity, uncertain price
    • limit order: price bounded by the limit, uncertain execution
    • other order types: Appendix
  • Resting limit orders supply liquidity
    • market orders and marketable limit orders consume it
  • Price-time priority: the better price first, then the earlier order at the same price
    • orders at the same price form a queue
    • queue position is valuable (case study: the high-frequency trading arms race)
  • Call auctions at the open and close: orders are pooled
    • they are matched at the single price that maximises the executed quantity
  • A market buy of size \(Q\) walks up the ask levels
    • it pays the half-spread plus the cost of the deeper levels

      The Bid-Ask Spread

      Why Is There a Spread?

      Slide 8 in the PDF
      • Question: why is there a spread, and how do we measure it with and without quotes?
      • A liquidity provider buys at the bid and sells at the ask
      • The spread pays three costs Huang & Stoll (1997)
        • Order processing: fees, technology, clearing
        • Inventory: holding an unwanted position exposes the provider to price risk
        • Adverse selection: some counterparties know more Bagehot (1971)
      • The provider incurs losses when trading with informed traders
        • it must recover them from noise traders (section Information and Prices)

      Quoted, Effective and Realised Spread (1/2)

      Slide 9 in the PDF
      • Three measures of what a round trip costs, all relative to the mid-price \(m_t\) just before the trade
        • \(p_t\): trade price
        • \(q_t = +1\) if the buyer initiated the trade (it hit the ask), \(-1\) if the seller did
      • Quoted spread, from quotes only: \(S^Q_t = (a_t - b_t)/m_t \ge 0\)
      • Effective spread, what the trade paid: \(S^E_t = 2 q_t (p_t - m_t)/m_t\)
        • \(q_t\) makes the cost positive on both sides
        • the one-way cost is \(S^E_t/2\)
      • Realised spread: \(S^R_t = 2 q_t (p_t - m_{t+\Delta})/m_t\)
        • what the liquidity provider keeps before fees and inventory costs
        • \(m_{t+\Delta}\): the mid a time \(\Delta\) after the trade (e.g. 5 minutes)
        • \(S^R_t < 0\) when \(q_t(m_{t+\Delta} - p_t) > 0\): the later mid-price passes the trade price, in the trade's direction
        • a smaller continuation lowers \(S^R_t\) but can leave it positive

      Quoted, Effective and Realised Spread (2/2)

      Slide 10 in the PDF
      • Price impact: \(S^E_t - S^R_t = 2 q_t (m_{t+\Delta} - m_t)/m_t\), the lasting move of the mid
        • usually attributed to adverse selection
        • inventory and order-flow pressure also contribute
      • Effective and realised spreads need trades, the prevailing quotes and the trade direction
        • direction signed for example by the rule of Lee & Ready (1991)
        • above the mid a buy, below it a sell
        • at the mid the tick test (against the last different trade price)
      • Without such data the spread is estimated from prices only: the next slides

      The Roll Model

      Slide 11 in the PDF
      • Idea Roll (1984): the spread can be read from the way trade prices bounce between bid and ask
        • \(t = 1, \dots, n\): successive trades, prices in USD (or log prices)
      • Efficient price, a random walk that moves only on news: \(m_t = m_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t\)
        • \(\varepsilon_t\) i.i.d. (independent, identically distributed), mean 0, variance \(\sigma^2\)
      • Trade price: \(p_t = m_t + c\,q_t\)
        • \(c > 0\): the half-spread (\(s = 2c\))
        • \(q_t = +1\) (buy at the ask) or \(-1\) (sell at the bid), each with probability 1/2
        • signs i.i.d. and independent of \(\varepsilon\)
      • Price change: \(\Delta p_t = \varepsilon_t + c(q_t - q_{t-1})\), so \(\Var(\Delta p_t) = \sigma^2 + 2c^2\) and \(\Cov(\Delta p_t, \Delta p_{t-1}) = -c^2\)
        • Bid-ask bounce: the common term \(q_{t-1}\) enters \(\Delta p_{t-1}\) with \(+c\) and \(\Delta p_t\) with \(-c\), so a jump up to the ask tends to be followed by a fall to the bid, even when the efficient price is a random walk
      • Roll estimator: \(\hat s = 2\hat c = 2\sqrt{-\widehat{\Cov}(\Delta p_t, \Delta p_{t-1})}\)
        • \(\widehat{\Cov}\): the sample first-order autocovariance of the \(n - 1\) price changes
        • defined only if it is negative

        The Roll Estimator in Finite Samples

        Slide 13 in the PDF
        • On real data the sample covariance is often positive, so \(\hat s\) does not exist
          • on SPY 5-minute bars, on 41% of days
          • Sampling error: the model is true, but \(T\) is small and \(c^2\) is small relative to \(\sigma^2\)
          • Misspecification: trends after news, orders split into many trades, stale prices, bars instead of trades
        • Notation Harris (1990): \(\gamma_k = \Cov(\Delta p_t, \Delta p_{t-k})\)
          • \(\gamma_0 = \sigma^2 + 2c^2\) and \(\gamma_1 = -c^2\) in the Roll model
          • \(T\): the number of price changes (77 per SPY day)
          • \(\hat\gamma_1\): the sample value, from demeaned price changes
        • Bias: \(\E[\hat\gamma_1] \approx \gamma_1 - (\gamma_0 + 2\gamma_1)/T\), negative even with no spread (\(c = 0\))
          • Roll then finds a spread that is not there
        • Noise: \(T\,\Var(\hat\gamma_1) \to \gamma_0^2 + \gamma_1^2\) as \(T \to \infty\) (independent signs \(q_t = \pm 1\)), so the variance shrinks only as \(1/T\)
          • Bartlett's Gaussian approximation, \(\gamma_0^2 + 3\gamma_1^2\), omits the fourth cumulant of binary signs: \(-2c^4 = -2\gamma_1^2\)
          • the limit requires a finite fourth moment, which Chapter 1 questions
        • Wrong sign: \(\hat\gamma_1\) is approximately Normal, so \(P(\hat\gamma_1 > 0) \approx \Phi(\sqrt{T}\,\gamma_1/\gamma_0)\)
          • \(\Phi\): distribution function of the standard Normal distribution
          • close to 1/2 whenever \(c^2 \ll \sigma^2/\sqrt{T}\)

        Spreads from Daily Ranges: Corwin–Schultz

        Slide 14 in the PDF
        • Idea Corwin & Schultz (2012): the daily high is usually a buy at the ask, the low a sell at the bid
          • so the high-low range contains volatility plus the spread
        • The volatility part grows with the length of the interval, the spread part does not
          • compare one day with two days
        • \(H_t\), \(L_t\): high and low of day \(t\)
          • \(H_{t,t+1}\), \(L_{t,t+1}\): high and low over days \(t\) and \(t+1\)
          • \(\ln(H/L)\): the log range
        • \(\beta = \sum_{j=0}^{1}\left[\ln \frac{H_{t+j}}{L_{t+j}}\right]^2\), the sum of the two squared one-day ranges
          • \(\gamma = \left[\ln \frac{H_{t,t+1}}{L_{t,t+1}}\right]^2\), the squared two-day range
          • Without a spread, \(\gamma \approx \beta\) on average (variance adds up over two days); the spread inflates \(\beta\) twice, \(\gamma\) only once
        • Solving the two moment equations: \(\;\alpha = \dfrac{\sqrt{2\beta} - \sqrt{\beta}}{3 - 2\sqrt{2}} - \sqrt{\dfrac{\gamma}{3 - 2\sqrt{2}}}, \qquad \hat S = \dfrac{2(e^{\alpha} - 1)}{1 + e^{\alpha}}\)
          • \(\alpha\): the spread part of the log range; \(\hat S\): relative spread, a fraction of the price (\(\times 10^4\) for bp), with \(\hat S \approx \alpha\) for small \(\alpha\)

        Abdi–Ranaldo and EDGE (1/2)

        Slide 15 in the PDF
        • Abdi–Ranaldo Abdi & Ranaldo (2017): the close sits at the bid or the ask
          • the middle of the day's range proxies the mid-price
          • \(c_t\): log closing price of day \(t\); \(\eta_t = (\ln H_t + \ln L_t)/2\): log mid-range of day \(t\)
        • The product of the close's distances from today's and tomorrow's mid-range
          • is, on average, a quarter of the squared spread: \(\;\hat S^2 = 4\,\E\left[(c_t - \eta_t)(c_t - \eta_{t+1})\right]\)
          • News moves \(\eta_{t+1}\) independently of the bounce at \(c_t\), so the volatility terms average out
          • \(\E\) is replaced by the sample mean over days; a negative mean product gives \(\hat S = 0\)

        Abdi–Ranaldo and EDGE (2/2)

        Slide 16 in the PDF
        • EDGE (Efficient Discrete Generalized Estimator) Ardia, Guidotti & Kroencke (2024): uses open, high, low and close (OHLC) prices
          • Two moment conditions, each a product of log-returns between the open, the close and \(\eta_t\) (exact form: Appendix), give two unbiased estimates \(\hat e_1\), \(\hat e_2\) of \(S^2\), with sampling variances \(v_1\), \(v_2\)
        • Minimum-variance combination (diagonal weighting): \(\;\hat S^2 = \dfrac{v_2 \hat e_1 + v_1 \hat e_2}{v_1 + v_2}\)
          • Each estimate is weighted by the other's variance, so the noisier one gets the smaller weight
        • Common to both: only OHLC bars are needed
          • a negative mean moment gives \(\hat S = 0\)
          • \(\sqrt{\hat S^2}\) is not exactly unbiased for \(S\)
        • For crypto-assets, high-low estimators track the time variation of high-frequency liquidity
          • the Amihud ratio is better for levels Brauneis, Mestel, Riordan & Theissen (2021)

            Illiquidity and Intraday Patterns

            The Amihud Illiquidity Ratio

            Slide 21 in the PDF
            • Question: how do we measure market depth with only daily returns and volumes?
            • Daily price impact per unit of money traded Amihud (2002): \(\;\mathrm{ILLIQ}_i = \dfrac{K}{D_i}\displaystyle\sum_{d=1}^{D_i} \dfrac{|r_{i,d}|}{\mathrm{DVOL}_{i,d}}\)
              • \(i\): the asset; \(d = 1, \dots, D_i\): its trading days with nonzero volume; \(r_{i,d}\): daily return; \(\mathrm{DVOL}_{i,d}\): traded value (price \(\times\) volume)
              • \(K = 10^4 \times 10^6 = 10^{10}\), with \(r\) as a fraction and DVOL in USD: basis points of price move per USD 1 million traded
            • Larger means less liquid: the same traded value moves the price more
            • Needs only daily returns and volumes, available for every market and period
              • BVB stocks: lei at the BNR reference rate of the day
              • crypto: traded value in USD summed across exchanges
            • A low-frequency proxy of Kyle's lambda (section Information and Prices) Hasbrouck (2009)

            Reading the Amihud Ratio: Units and Kyle's Lambda

            Slide 22 in the PDF
            • A toy day: \(|r| = 50\) bp on USD 25 million traded gives \(50/25 = 2\) bp per USD 1 million
              • i.e. 200 bp per USD 100 million
              • \(\mathrm{ILLIQ}_i\) averages such daily ratios
            • The intraday SPY measure of this section: bp per USD 100 million
              • the mean of the day's 78 bar ratios
              • Its mean 1.83 is 0.0183 bp per USD 1 million, not comparable with the daily SPY ratio (0.0016): a bar carries about 1/78 of the day's value, but its \(|r|\) is not 78 times smaller
            • Link to Kyle's \(\lambda\), the price move per share of net order flow \(y\)
              • if all volume were net flow, \(\Delta p = \lambda y\) at price \(p\) gives \(|r|/\mathrm{DVOL} = (\lambda|y|/p)/(p|y|) = \lambda/p^2\)
              • Offsetting buys and sells add volume without moving the price, so the ratio is at most \(\lambda/p^2\) (seminar A6)

                    Information and Prices

                    The Glosten–Milgrom Model

                    Slide 31 in the PDF
                    • Question: how does private information enter prices, and why does it alone create a spread?
                    • Value \(V \in \{V_L, V_H\}\), \(P(V = V_H) = \theta\); each period one trader arrives Glosten & Milgrom (1985)
                      • With probability \(\mu\) informed (buys if \(V = V_H\), sells if \(V = V_L\)), otherwise a noise trader, uninformed and trading for liquidity needs (buys or sells with probability 1/2) Bagehot (1971)
                    • Competitive, risk-neutral market maker with zero expected profit on each trade
                      • \(a = \E[V \mid \text{buy}]\) (ask), \(b = \E[V \mid \text{sell}]\) (bid)
                      • A buy signals a high value and a sell a low one, so \(a > b\)
                    • Informed traders always buy when \(V = V_H\), noise traders half of the time
                      • \(P(\text{buy} \mid V_H) = \mu + \tfrac{1 - \mu}{2}\), \(P(\text{buy} \mid V_L) = \tfrac{1 - \mu}{2}\)
                    • After each trade, Bayes' rule updates \(\theta\): a buy raises it, a sell lowers it

                    Glosten–Milgrom: the Spread

                    Slide 32 in the PDF
                    • Bayes' rule after a buy, then the ask as the expected value
                      • \(P(V_H \mid \text{buy}) = \dfrac{\left(\mu + \frac{1 - \mu}{2}\right)\theta}{\left(\mu + \frac{1 - \mu}{2}\right)\theta + \frac{1 - \mu}{2}(1 - \theta)}, \qquad a = V_L + (V_H - V_L)\,P(V_H \mid \text{buy})\)
                      • Numerator: probability of “high value and a buy”; denominator: total probability of a buy; the bid follows symmetrically from a sell
                    • At \(\theta = 1/2\): \(a - b = \mu(V_H - V_L)\), linear in the share of informed traders
                      • \(V_L = 90\), \(V_H = 110\): spread 2 for \(\mu = 0.1\), 6 for \(\mu = 0.3\)
                    • No inventory, no fees: the spread exists purely because of information
                    • Trade prices form a martingale: the market is efficient in the semi-strong sense (Chapter 2)

                    The Kyle Model

                    Slide 33 in the PDF
                    • One insider knows \(v \sim N(p_0, \sigma_v^2)\), with \(p_0\) the price before trading, and submits a market order \(x\) Kyle (1985)
                      • Noise traders submit \(u \sim N(0, \sigma_u^2)\), independent of \(v\)
                      • The market maker sees only the total order flow \(y = x + u\) and sets \(p = \E[v \mid y]\)
                    • Linear equilibrium: \(x = \beta (v - p_0)\), \(p = p_0 + \lambda y\)
                      • \(\beta > 0\): the insider's trading intensity
                      • \(\lambda > 0\): Kyle's lambda, the price move per share of net order flow
                    • The insider takes the pricing rule as given: \(\E[(v - p)\,x \mid v] = (v - p_0)x - \lambda x^2\)
                      • the gain from information minus the cost of moving the price against himself
                      • First-order condition: \(x = (v - p_0)/(2\lambda)\), so \(\beta = 1/(2\lambda)\): he trades less because his order moves the price
                    • The market maker: \((v, y)\) is jointly Normal, so \(\lambda = \Cov(v, y)/\Var(y) = \beta\sigma_v^2/(\beta^2\sigma_v^2 + \sigma_u^2)\)
                      • the population OLS (ordinary least squares) slope of \(v\) on \(y\)

                    Kyle: the Equilibrium and What the Price Reveals

                    Slide 34 in the PDF
                    • Substituting \(\beta = 1/(2\lambda)\) into the slope gives \(\beta^2\sigma_v^2 = \sigma_u^2\), hence
                      • \(\beta = \sigma_u/\sigma_v, \quad \lambda = \sigma_v/(2\sigma_u), \quad \E[\text{insider profit}] = \sigma_v\sigma_u/2\)
                    • More noise trading (\(\sigma_u\) up): a smaller \(\lambda\) (deeper market), a larger \(\beta\) and a larger profit
                    • More valuable information (\(\sigma_v\) up): a larger \(\lambda\), the market maker protects himself
                    • \(\Var(x) = \beta^2\sigma_v^2 = \sigma_u^2\): the insider's orders are as variable as noise trading, behind which he hides
                    • \(\Var(v \mid y) = \sigma_v^2 - \lambda^2\Var(y) = \sigma_v^2/2\): the price reveals half of the private information

                      Signed Order Flow: Empirical Lambda and OFI

                      Slide 39 in the PDF
                      • Question: how much does order flow move the price over short intervals, measured directly from data?
                      • Empirical Kyle lambda: regress the mid-price change on the net signed flow Hasbrouck (2007)
                        • over intervals \(k\) (e.g. 5 minutes)
                        • \(\Delta m_k = \lambda \sum_{t \in k} q_t v_t + u_k\); \(v_t\): size of trade \(t\); \(q_t\): its sign (Lee–Ready rule)
                      • OFI (order-flow imbalance) Cont, Kukanov & Stoikov (2014): built from order-book events \(n\)
                        • each event changes the best prices \(P^b_n\), \(P^a_n\) and the depths \(d^b_n\), \(d^a_n\) at the best quotes
                        • \(\begin{aligned} e_n = {}& d^b_n\,\mathbf{1}\{P^b_n \ge P^b_{n-1}\} - d^b_{n-1}\,\mathbf{1}\{P^b_n \le P^b_{n-1}\} \\ & - d^a_n\,\mathbf{1}\{P^a_n \le P^a_{n-1}\} + d^a_{n-1}\,\mathbf{1}\{P^a_n \ge P^a_{n-1}\}\end{aligned}\), \(\;\mathrm{OFI}_k = \sum_{n \in k} e_n\); \(\mathbf{1}\{\cdot\}\): 1 if the condition holds, else 0
                        • a rising bid or a rising ask contributes positively; a falling bid or a falling ask contributes negatively
                        • at an unchanged ask the quantity matters: a fall in \(d^a\) contributes positively
                        • \(\Delta m_k = \beta\,\mathrm{OFI}_k + \varepsilon_k\), with \(\beta\) inversely proportional to the average depth at the best quotes: a linear relation, unlike the concave impact of trade volume
                      • OFI counts limit orders and cancellations, not only trades
                        • so it explains short-horizon price changes better than the trade imbalance
                        • it needs order-book events, which the bars used here do not contain

                      Optimal Execution: the Trade-Off

                      Slide 41 in the PDF
                      • Sell \(X\) shares within \(T\) days, in \(N\) steps of length \(\tau = T/N\) Almgren & Chriss (2001)
                        • holdings \(x_k\) from \(x_0 = X\) to \(x_N = 0\), trades \(n_k = x_{k-1} - x_k\)
                      • The market price moves with news and is pushed down by our own sales
                        • \(S_k = S_{k-1} + \sigma\sqrt{\tau}\,\xi_k - \gamma\,n_k\)
                        • the price we receive: \(\tilde S_k = S_{k-1} - \eta\,n_k/\tau\)
                        • \(\sigma\): price volatility (USD per day\(^{1/2}\)); \(\xi_k\): news shock, i.i.d., mean 0, variance 1
                        • \(\gamma \ge 0\): permanent impact, lowers all later prices; \(\eta > 0\): temporary impact, paid only by the trade itself
                      • Trading fast: high temporary impact, low price risk; trading slowly: the opposite
                      • Objective, over deterministic schedules: \(\min\ \E[C] + \lambda\Var[C]\)
                        • \(C = X S_0 - \sum_k n_k \tilde S_k\): the implementation cost
                        • value at the initial price \(S_0\) minus what the sales bring
                        • \(\lambda \ge 0\): risk aversion (not Kyle's \(\lambda\)); \(\lambda = 0\) minimises the expected cost only

                      Almgren–Chriss: the Optimal Trajectory

                      Slide 42 in the PDF
                      • Continuous time, holdings \(x(t)\), \(t \in [0, T]\), selling speed \(-\dot x\)
                        • \(\;\min \int_0^T (\eta\,\dot x^2 + \lambda\sigma^2 x^2)\,dt, \quad x(0) = X,\ x(T) = 0\)
                        • \(\eta\,\dot x^2\): selling fast is costly; \(\lambda\sigma^2 x^2\): holding a large position is risky
                      • Solution (derivation: Appendix, seminar A7): \(\;x(t) = X\,\dfrac{\sinh(\kappa(T - t))}{\sinh(\kappa T)}, \qquad \kappa = \sqrt{\lambda\sigma^2/\eta}\), with \(\sinh z = (e^z - e^{-z})/2\)
                        • \(\kappa\): the urgency of the liquidation, it rises with risk aversion and volatility, falls with impact; \(1/\kappa\): the time scale of the liquidation
                        • \(\lambda = 0\): \(\kappa = 0\) and holdings fall linearly (trade evenly)
                        • Large \(\lambda\): most of the order is traded at the start, cutting the exposure to price risk

                          Price Discovery across Venues: Information Shares (1/2)

                          Slide 51 in the PDF
                          • Two log prices of one asset on two venues, \(y_t = (p_{1t}, p_{2t})'\)
                            • each is I(1) (a random walk-like series)
                            • their gap \(\beta' y_t = p_{1t} - p_{2t}\), with \(\beta = (1, -1)'\), is stationary (cointegration)
                          • VECM (vector error-correction model) Johansen (1991): each price reacts to yesterday's gap and to past changes
                            • \(\Delta y_t = \alpha\,\beta' y_{t-1} + \sum_{i=1}^{p}\Gamma_i\,\Delta y_{t-i} + e_t, \qquad \Var(e_t) = \Omega\)
                            • \(\alpha = (\alpha_1, \alpha_2)'\): adjustment coefficients, the speed at which each price closes the gap
                            • \(\Gamma_i\): \(2 \times 2\) matrices of short-run dynamics, \(p\) lags; \(e_t\): innovations of the two prices
                          • The common efficient price, the stochastic trend shared by both prices, has innovation \(\psi' e_t\)
                            • \(\psi' \propto \alpha_\perp'\) (\(\propto\): proportional to; \(\alpha_\perp'\alpha = 0\))

                          Price Discovery across Venues: Information Shares (2/2)

                          Slide 52 in the PDF
                          • Hasbrouck information share Hasbrouck (1995), venue \(j\)'s share of the variance of the common innovation
                            • \(\;IS_j = \dfrac{([\psi' F]_j)^2}{\psi'\Omega\psi} \in [0, 1], \quad IS_1 + IS_2 = 1\)
                            • \(F\): Cholesky factor of \(\Omega\) (lower triangular, \(FF' = \Omega\)); \([\cdot]_j\): the \(j\)-th element
                            • Depends on the ordering when \(\Omega\) is not diagonal: report the bounds over both orderings
                          • Gonzalo–Granger component share Gonzalo & Granger (1995): \(\alpha_\perp\) normalised to sum 1
                            • for two venues \(CS_1 = \alpha_2/(\alpha_2 - \alpha_1)\)
                            • in \([0, 1]\) only if the two adjustment coefficients have opposite signs
                          • A price that does not adjust (\(\alpha_j = 0\), weak exogeneity) has a component share of 1
                            • Hasbrouck's bounds can stay below 1 when the innovations are correlated, and they depend on the ordering: with \(\alpha_1 = 0\), equal variances and correlation \(\rho\), the bounds of the first market can be \([1 - \rho^2, 1]\)
                            • Inference: a Wald test of \(\alpha_j = 0\); non-rejection does not prove \(\alpha_j = 0\)

                                Nonsynchronous Trading: the Lo–MacKinlay Model

                                Slide 78 in the PDF
                                • Question: what do observed returns look like when an asset does not trade every period? Lo & MacKinlay (1990)
                                  • the case of many BVB stocks
                                • Virtual returns of the unobserved price: \(r_{it} = \mu_i + \beta_i f_t + \varepsilon_{it}\)
                                  • \(f_t\): common factor, i.i.d., with variance \(\sigma_f^2\); \(\varepsilon_{it}\): idiosyncratic part, independent of \(f\)
                                  • In each period asset \(i\) does not trade with probability \(\pi_i\), independently over time and across assets
                                • Observed return \(r^o_{it}\): 0 in a period without trades
                                  • otherwise the sum of the virtual returns accumulated since the last trade

                                A Structural Model of Trading Costs: MRR (1/2)

                                Slide 93 in the PDF
                                • Roll assumes i.i.d. trade signs; real signs are persistent (a buy tends to follow a buy)
                                  • First-order Markov signs: \(\Corr(q_t, q_{t-k}) = \rho^k\), \(\rho \in [0, 1)\) the persistence; \(\Corr\): correlation
                                  • Signs independent of \(\varepsilon\) and no information: \(\Cov(\Delta p_t, \Delta p_{t-1}) = -c^2(1 - \rho)^2\), closer to 0 than \(-c^2\), so Roll underestimates \(c\)
                                • MRR model Madhavan, Richardson & Roomans (1997): the trade price is the efficient price plus a transitory cost
                                  • the efficient price moves with the surprise in the order flow
                                  • \(p_t = m_t + \phi\,q_t + \xi_t, \qquad m_t = m_{t-1} + \theta\,(q_t - \E[q_t \mid q_{t-1}]) + \varepsilon_t\)
                                  • \(\theta \ge 0\): permanent (adverse-selection) impact of the surprise \(q_t - \E[q_t \mid q_{t-1}] = q_t - \rho q_{t-1}\); \(\phi \ge 0\): transitory cost (processing, inventory)
                                  • \(q_t\) a first-order Markov chain; public news \(\varepsilon_t\) and rounding noise \(\xi_t\) i.i.d., mean zero, independent of each other and of \(q\)

                                A Structural Model of Trading Costs: MRR (2/2)

                                Slide 94 in the PDF
                                • Estimation by GMM: choose \((\theta, \phi, \rho, a)\) so that the sample versions of four moments are zero
                                  • Residual: \(u_t = \Delta p_t - (\phi + \theta)q_t + (\phi + \rho\theta)q_{t-1}\), the price change not explained by the trades; \(a\): its drift (constant)
                                  • \(\E[q_t q_{t-1} - \rho q_{t-1}^2] = 0, \quad \E[u_t - a] = 0, \quad \E[(u_t - a)q_t] = 0, \quad \E[(u_t - a)q_{t-1}] = 0\)
                                  • First: defines \(\rho\) as the autocorrelation of signs; the others: the residual has mean \(a\) and is uncorrelated with current and past signs
                                • Outputs
                                  • Implied effective spread \(2(\phi + \theta)\); adverse-selection share \(\theta/(\phi + \theta) \in [0, 1]\): 0 means pure processing and inventory costs, 1 pure information
                                • Needs signed trades (trade-and-quote data)
                                  • the public 5-minute bars do not identify \(q_t\), so MRR is not estimated here

                                Estimating PIN, and Why It Is Fragile (1/2)

                                Slide 97 in the PDF
                                • EKOP model Easley, Kiefer, O'Hara & Paperman (1996): each day an information event occurs with probability \(\alpha\)
                                  • EKOP: initials of Easley, Kiefer, O'Hara and Paperman

                                  • Rates (expected orders per day): uninformed \(\varepsilon_b\), \(\varepsilon_s\); informed \(\mu\) added to the buy side on good-news days, to the sell side on bad-news days

                                  • The sample likelihood is the product of \(L\) over days, maximised in \((\alpha, \delta, \mu, \varepsilon_b, \varepsilon_s)\)

                                Slide 102 in the PDF

                                Case Study: Data and Method

                                Slide 108 in the PDF
                                • Data: London Stock Exchange message data for all FTSE 350 stocks
                                  • 17 August – 16 October 2015, 43 trading days
                                  • about 2.2 billion messages, obtained by the British regulator
                                  • Time stamps accurate to the microsecond (\(\mu\)s), taken where a message reaches the exchange's network, before any exchange processing
                                • Race: 2+ participants on the same stock, price and side
                                  • 1+ take and 1+ cancel attempts, or 2+ takes
                                  • at least one success and one failure
                                  • “Same time”: within the information horizon, the first message's observed processing time plus the fastest reaction time seen (29 \(\mu\)s), capped at 500 \(\mu\)s
                                • Race profit: the signed gap between the race price and the mid-price 10 s later
                                  • times the displayed depth
                                • Without races, the cost of liquidity would fall by about 17%
                                  • the cut: non-race tax / non-race effective half-spread
                                  • averaged over dates
                                • Extrapolation: OLS of daily race profits, without a constant
                                  • on traded value \(V_t\)
                                  • and on \(V_t\) and 1-minute realised volatility \(\sigma_t\) times mean traded value \(\bar V\)
