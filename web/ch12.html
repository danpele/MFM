Black & Scholes (1973) obtain the price of an option from the absence of arbitrage: a portfolio of the stock and cash, rebalanced continuously, replicates the option, and its price does not depend on the stock's expected return. In this model all options on the same asset have the same implied volatility. Replication in practice is discrete. In the chapter's simulation, daily delta hedging of a three-month call leaves an error with a standard deviation of 9.5% of the premium, and weekly rebalancing one of 21.5%.

Market prices do not respect the assumption of a single volatility. Since the crash of October 1987, low-strike puts have been much more expensive than at-the-money options, and implied volatility depends on the strike: the smile and the skew. The historical distribution of 9,225 overlapping monthly S&P 500 returns gives a one-month implied volatility of 30.6% at a strike of 80% of the current price and of 13.9% at the money. Option prices therefore allow one to recover the risk-neutral distribution of the future price. On the Bitcoin option surface of 22 September 2026, at the 38-day expiry, the probability of a fall of more than 30% is 1.24%, against 0.14% under the log-normal benchmark. These probabilities are prices of insurance, not forecasts.

The gap between the volatility priced by the market and realised volatility is measurable. Over 9,203 days in 1990–2026, the VIX has a mean of 19.4, and the realised volatility of the S&P 500 over the next 21 trading days a mean of 15.3. The ex-post variance risk premium (VRP) is 4.09 vol points, with a Newey–West standard error of 0.27, and it is positive on 86% of days. The exceptions are crashes: the worst value, −72.0 points, belongs to the window starting on 20 February 2020. As a forecast of next month's variance, the VIX has a Mincer–Zarnowitz slope of 0.86, and the joint test rejects unbiasedness. The chapter therefore studies what information options carry about future risk and with which standard errors it must be read.

Parity and Static Replication

Put–Call Parity: What Does No Arbitrage Alone Imply?

Slide 5 in the PDF
  • Two portfolios with the same pay-off at \(T\) (European options, same \(K\) and \(T\), no dividends):
    • A: one call + cash \(K e^{-r\tau}\) (worth \(K\) at \(T\)) \(\Rightarrow\) \(\max(S_T, K)\)
    • B: one put + one share \(\Rightarrow\) \(\max(K, S_T)\)
  • Same pay-off in every state, so the same price today, otherwise there is an arbitrage:
    • \(C_t - P_t = S_t - K e^{-r\tau}\) (with dividend yield \(q\): \(S_t e^{-q\tau}\) instead of \(S_t\))
  • No model for \(S\) is needed: parity uses only no-arbitrage Merton (1973)
  • Consequence: a call and a put with the same \(K\) and \(T\) have the same implied volatility

Static Replication: Any Pay-Off from a Bond, a Forward and Options

Slide 6 in the PDF
  • Carr–Madan spanning: any twice-differentiable pay-off \(f(S_T)\) and any point \(F\):
    • Taylor with integral remainder; proof in the Appendix
    • \(f(S_T) = f(F) + f'(F)(S_T - F) + \displaystyle\int_0^F f''(K)\,(K - S_T)^+ dK + \int_F^\infty f''(K)\,(S_T - K)^+ dK\)
    • Reading: a bond, a forward and \(f''(K)\,dK\) puts below \(F\) and calls above \(F\)
    • Priced today (\(F\) the forward): \(V_0 = e^{-r\tau} f(F) + \int_0^F f''(K)\,P(K)\,dK + \int_F^\infty f''(K)\,C(K)\,dK\)
    • \(P(K)\), \(C(K)\): today's put and call prices at strike \(K\), whatever the model for \(S\) Bakshi, Kapadia & Madan (2003)
  • Why this price: each piece has a market price today
    • the bond \(e^{-r\tau}\) per unit, the forward 0, the options \(P(K)\) and \(C(K)\)
    • No model for \(S\) and no pricing measure are needed for \(V_0\); the measure \(Q\) of Section 3 is needed only to read option prices as expectations, as in the density bullet of the next slide

Static Replication: Three Results Used Later

Slide 7 in the PDF
  • “Risk-neutral”: a price is the discounted mean pay-off under the pricing measure \(Q\)
    • not under real-world probabilities
    • \(Q\) is constructed in Section 3
  • Variance: \(f(S) = -2\ln(S/F)\) has \(f''(K) = 2/K^2\)
    • the strip with weights \(1/K^2\) is the variance swap and the VIX (Section 8)
    • Low strikes get the largest weights, so an expensive put wing makes variance expensive
  • Density: a butterfly \([C(K - h) - 2C(K) + C(K + h)]/h^2 \to \partial^2 C/\partial K^2 = e^{-r\tau} q(K)\) Breeden & Litzenberger (1978)
    • \(h\): the strike step; the risk-neutral density is a price (Section 7)
  • Straddles, spreads and protective puts are finite strips
    • \(f''\) concentrated at one or two strikes
    • moments and SVIX (simple VIX, the variance index of Martin (2017)): Appendix

The Binomial Tree

The Binomial Tree: How Do We Price an Option by Replication?

Slide 8 in the PDF
  • One period of length \(\Delta t\): \(S \to uS\) or \(dS\), with \(d < R = e^{r\Delta t} < u\)
    • \(u\), \(d\): up and down factors
    • \(R\): gross riskless return
  • Replicate the call with \(\Delta\) shares and cash \(B\): \(\Delta uS + RB = C_u\) and \(\Delta dS + RB = C_d\)
    • \(C_u\), \(C_d\): the call values after an up and a down move
    • Solution: \(\Delta = \dfrac{C_u - C_d}{(u - d)S}\), and the price is \(C = \Delta S + B = \dfrac{q\,C_u + (1 - q)\,C_d}{R}\)
    • \(q = \dfrac{R - d}{u - d} \in (0, 1)\) (here a probability, not the dividend yield) is the unique probability under which \(S_t/R^t\) is a martingale
  • The real probability of an up move never enters
    • risk preferences are already in the price \(S\)
  • Fundamental theorem: no arbitrage \(\Leftrightarrow\) an equivalent martingale measure \(Q\) exists Harrison & Pliska (1981), Delbaen & Schachermayer (1994)
    • \(Q\) is unique exactly when the market is complete; with jumps or stochastic volatility there are many \(Q\), and option prices carry risk premia (Section 8)

The CRR Tree: \(N\) Steps and Convergence to Black–Scholes

Slide 9 in the PDF
  • Why \(N\) steps: one period has only two final states
    • with \(N\) steps the \(N + 1\) final states approximate the distribution of \(S_T\)
  • The CRR (Cox–Ross–Rubinstein) tree Cox, Ross & Rubinstein (1979): \(\Delta t = T/N\), \(u = e^{\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}}\), \(d = 1/u\)
    • This choice gives the one-step log return the variance \(\sigma^2\Delta t\) (up to terms of order \(\Delta t^2\)), so the tree converges to the geometric Brownian motion of the Black–Scholes model
    • \(q = (e^{r\Delta t} - d)/(u - d)\) is the same at every node
  • European call: \(C_0 = e^{-rT}\displaystyle\sum_{j = 0}^{N}\binom{N}{j} q^j (1 - q)^{N - j}\,\big(S_0 u^j d^{N - j} - K\big)^+\)
    • \(j\): the number of up moves; the sum is the discounted risk-neutral mean of the pay-off
  • American options: value backwards from \(T\)
    • taking \(\max\)(continuation value, exercise value) at every node

Black–Scholes and the Greeks

The Black–Scholes Equation: What Price Does Continuous Replication Give?

Slide 10 in the PDF
  • Geometric Brownian motion \(dS_t = \mu S_t\,dt + \sigma S_t\,dW_t\)
    • constant \(\sigma\) and \(r\)
    • continuous trading, no costs, short selling allowed
  • Replicate the option value \(V(S, t)\) with a self-financing portfolio \(\Pi\)
    • \(\Delta_t\) shares and cash \(B_t\) at \(r\): \(d\Pi = \Delta\,dS + rB\,dt\)
  • Itô's lemma: \(dV = \left(V_t + \mu S V_S + \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2 S^2 V_{SS}\right)dt + \sigma S V_S\,dW_t\)
    • \(V_t\), \(V_S\), \(V_{SS}\): the partial derivatives
  • Choose \(\Delta = V_S\), start from \(\Pi = V\) (\(B = V - S V_S\))
    • \(d(V - \Pi) = \left(V_t + \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2 S^2 V_{SS} - rB\right)dt\)
    • both \(dW_t\) and \(\mu\) drop out
  • A riskless position that costs nothing must earn nothing
    • hence the Black–Scholes equation Black & Scholes (1973), Merton (1973): \(V_t + \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2 S^2 V_{SS} + r S V_S - r V = 0\)
    • with \(V(S, T)\) = pay-off
  • That \(\mu\) is irrelevant is a result, not an assumption
    • Feynman–Kac: \(V = e^{-r\tau} E^Q[\text{pay-off}]\) with \(S\) drifting at \(r\) under \(Q\)

The Black–Scholes Formula

Slide 11 in the PDF
  • European call and put, no dividends (with yield \(q\): replace \(S\) by \(S e^{-q\tau}\)):
    • \(C = S\,\Phi(d_1) - K e^{-r\tau}\,\Phi(d_2), \qquad P = K e^{-r\tau}\,\Phi(-d_2) - S\,\Phi(-d_1)\)
    • \(d_1 = \dfrac{\ln(S/K) + (r + \sigma^2/2)\tau}{\sigma\sqrt{\tau}}, \qquad d_2 = d_1 - \sigma\sqrt{\tau}\), with \(\Phi\) the standard Normal distribution function
  • Reading the two terms:
    • \(\Phi(d_2)\) = risk-neutral probability that the call ends in the money
    • \(\Phi(d_1)\) = number of shares in the replicating portfolio (the delta)
  • Five inputs: \(S\), \(K\), \(\tau\), \(r\) observed
    • \(\sigma\) is the only one not observed: it must be estimated or implied

    The Greeks: One Identity, All Sensitivities (2/2)

    Slide 13 in the PDF
    • Greeks: partial derivatives of the price
      • \(\Delta = \partial C/\partial S\), \(\Gamma = \partial^2 C/\partial S^2\)
      • vega \(\mathcal{V} = \partial C/\partial\sigma\), \(\Theta = \partial C/\partial t\), rho \(= \partial C/\partial r\)
    • Delta \(\Delta = \Phi(d_1)\) (put: \(\Phi(d_1) - 1\))
      • gamma \(\Gamma = \varphi(d_1)/(S\sigma\sqrt{\tau})\), \(\varphi\) the standard Normal density
      • key identity \(S\varphi(d_1) = K e^{-r\tau}\varphi(d_2)\)
    • Vega \(= S\varphi(d_1)\sqrt{\tau} = \Gamma S^2 \sigma \tau\): for one expiry gamma and vega are proportional
      • Seminar 12, A1, A7
      • theta \(\Theta = rC - rS\Delta - \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2 S^2 \Gamma\)
      • rho \(= K\tau e^{-r\tau}\Phi(d_2)\)
    • Call with \(K = 100\)
      • short maturities concentrate gamma and theta at the strike
      • long maturities carry the most vega
      • vega per vol point, theta per calendar day

    Delta Hedging in Practice

    Discrete Delta Hedging: How Much Risk Is Left with \(N\) Rebalancings?

    Slide 14 in the PDF
    • Black–Scholes replication needs continuous rebalancing
      • in practice the hedge is adjusted \(N\) times, at \(t_i = iT/N\)
    • At \(t_0 = 0\): sell the call at its Black–Scholes price \(C_0\)
      • buy \(\Delta_0 = \Phi(d_1)\) shares
      • keep the cash \(B_0 = C_0 - \Delta_0 S_0\)
    • At \(t_i\), \(i = 1, \dots, N - 1\): the portfolio is self-financing
      • \(\Delta_i = \Phi\big(d_1(S_{t_i}, T - t_i)\big)\)
      • \(B_i = B_{i - 1}e^{rT/N} - (\Delta_i - \Delta_{i - 1})S_{t_i}\)
    • Hedging error at expiry: \(\varepsilon_N = \Delta_{N - 1}S_T + B_{N - 1}e^{rT/N} - (S_T - K)^+\)
    • Simulation: geometric Brownian motion with \(\mu = 8\%\) and \(\sigma = 20\%\)
      • \(S_0 = K = 100\), \(r = 4\%\), \(T = 0.25\)
      • 20 000 paths; \(N = 3, 12, 63, 252\)
    • Why \(\mu \neq r\): replication never uses \(\mu\)
      • a real drift different from \(r\) shows that the error stays centred at 0

    Discrete Delta Hedging: Algorithm and Error (1/2)

    Slide 15 in the PDF
      Question for the room With daily rebalancing, the standard deviation of the error is 9.5% of the premium. How much more often must the hedge be adjusted to halve it?
      Answer

      About four times as often. Doubling N approximately halves the error variance, not its standard deviation: the standard deviation falls like 1/√N (the estimated log-log slope is −0.48). Halving the standard deviation requires approximately four times as many rebalancings: from 63 to 252 over the three months. In the simulation, the standard deviation falls from 9.5% to 4.8% of the premium. Transaction costs, however, grow with N: like N with a fixed cost per rebalancing, roughly like √N with costs proportional to the traded value.

      Derivation: the Gamma-Weighted Volatility P&L

      Slide 17 in the PDF
      • Sell the option at \(\sigma_{imp}\) and hold \(\Delta = \partial_S V^{imp}\)
        • \(V^{imp}\): its Black–Scholes value at \(\sigma_{imp}\)
        • the price moves with \(\sigma_{real}\): \(dS = \mu S\,dt + \sigma_{real} S\,dW_t\)
      • Itô on \(V^{imp}\): \(dV = V_t\,dt + \Delta\,dS + \tfrac{1}{2}\Gamma\sigma_{real}^2 S^2 dt\)
        • the equation at \(\sigma_{imp}\) gives \(V_t = rV - rS\Delta - \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma_{imp}^2 S^2 \Gamma\)
      • Short option + \(\Delta\) shares + cash at \(r\), self-financing:
        • \(d\,\text{P\&L} = r\,\text{P\&L}\,dt + \tfrac{1}{2}\,\Gamma_t S_t^2\left(\sigma_{imp}^2 - \sigma_{real}^2\right)dt\), so over the life
        • \(\text{P\&L} = \displaystyle\int_0^T e^{r(T - t)}\,\tfrac{1}{2}\,\Gamma_t S_t^2\left(\sigma_{imp}^2 - \sigma_{real, t}^2\right)dt\): path-dependent, weighted by \(\Gamma\), large near \(K\) and near \(T\)
      • A short variance swap pays \(\sigma_{imp}^2 - \sigma_{real}^2\) with constant weights
        • a delta-hedged option is a variance bet with random weights
      • First-order size: vega \(\times\) (implied \(-\) realised), checked on the next slide
        • Why: vega \(= \Gamma S^2\sigma\tau\) (Section 4), so \(\tfrac{1}{2}\Gamma S^2(\sigma_{imp}^2 - \sigma_{real}^2)\tau = \tfrac{1}{2}\Gamma S^2(\sigma_{imp} + \sigma_{real})(\sigma_{imp} - \sigma_{real})\tau \approx \Gamma S^2\sigma(\sigma_{imp} - \sigma_{real})\tau = \text{vega}\times(\sigma_{imp} - \sigma_{real})\)

        Implied Volatility: Which \(\sigma\) Explains the Market Price?

        Slide 19 in the PDF
        • Implied volatility \(\sigma_{imp}\): the \(\sigma\) that makes the Black–Scholes price equal the market price
          • \(BS(S, K, \tau, r, \sigma_{imp}) = C^{market}\); \(BS(\cdot)\): the Black–Scholes formula, \(C^{market}\): the observed price

        • Traders quote options in implied volatility, not in money
          • it is comparable across strikes, maturities and assets
        • If Black–Scholes were right, all options on one asset would have the same implied volatility

          The SVI Smile: Why This Shape and How We Fit It

          Slide 27 in the PDF
          • Why \(w\) and \(k\): the no-arbitrage conditions (next slide) are written directly in \(w(k)\)
            • different expiries become comparable
          • Why SVI: as \(|k| \to \infty\), \(w\) becomes linear in \(k\)

          • Fit, for each expiry: \(\min \sum_j \big(w(k_j) - \sigma_{imp, j}^2\tau\big)^2\)

            • with parameter bounds and six starting points

          No-Arbitrage Constraints on the Smile

          Slide 28 in the PDF
          • Wings: Lee (2004) bounds the asymptotic slope, \(\limsup_{|k| \to \infty} w(k)/|k| \le 2\)
            • for SVI \(b(1 + |\rho|) \le 2\) is necessary
            • \(w \ge 0\) needs \(a + b s\sqrt{1 - \rho^2} \ge 0\)
            • A steeper wing means fatter tails of \(S_T\) under \(Q\) (moment formula: Appendix)
          • Butterfly: call prices convex in \(K\), density \(q \ge 0\) Gatheral & Jacquier (2014)
            • with \(w' = \partial_k w\), \(w'' = \partial_k^2 w\)
            • \(g(k) \ge 0\) is exactly a nonnegative density
            • \(g(k) = \left(1 - \dfrac{k w'}{2w}\right)^2 - \dfrac{w'^2}{4}\left(\dfrac{1}{w} + \dfrac{1}{4}\right) + \dfrac{w''}{2} \ge 0\) for all \(k\), plus \(d_+(k) = -k/\sqrt{w} + \sqrt{w}/2 \to -\infty\) as \(k \to \infty\) (Gatheral & Jacquier (2014), Lemma 2.2)
          • Calendar: \(\partial_\tau w(k, \tau) \ge 0\) for every \(k\): total variance cannot fall with maturity
          • Slice-by-slice least squares imposes none of them
            • SSVI, the surface version of SVI, has simple sufficient conditions (Gatheral & Jacquier (2014), Theorems 4.1–4.2)

              Variance Swaps: What Does Future Variance Cost?

              Slide 33 in the PDF

                The Variance Risk Premium

                Slide 36 in the PDF
                • Variance risk premium (VRP) Carr & Wu (2009), Bollerslev, Tauchen & Zhou (2009)
                  • three versions that must not be confused:
                  • Economic (ex ante): \(E^Q_t[RV_{t, t + 21}] - E^P_t[RV_{t, t + 21}]\); \(VIX_t^2\) estimates the first term, the second needs a forecast
                  • Ex post (used here): \(VIX_t^2 - RV_{t, t + 21}\), the realised P&L of the seller of a variance swap, known only at \(t + 21\)

                • \(RV_{t, t + 21} = \frac{252}{21}\sum_{i = 1}^{21} r_{t + i}^2\) (annualised, in %\(^2\))
                  • \(r_{t+i}\): the daily log returns in %
                  • roughly the same month as the 30 calendar days of the VIX
                • Positive on average if investors dislike states with high volatility
                  • these are also bad states for wealth
                • Overlapping 21-day windows are autocorrelated: Newey–West with at least 21 lags Newey & West (1987)
                  • 21 lags cover the overlap, not the persistence of volatility
                  • standard error of the premium, in vol points: \(0.27\) with 21 lags, \(0.31\) with 42, \(0.32\) with 63, \(0.33\) with 126

                        Does the VIX Underreact to Volatility?

                        Slide 52 in the PDF
                        • Lochstoer & Muir (2022): investors update their expectations of market volatility too slowly
                          • underreaction first, after a volatility shock, then delayed overreaction
                        • Why it matters: VIX\(^2\) is the risk-neutral price of 30-day variance
                          • if the VIX rises less than expected variance, the VRP (variance risk premium) falls exactly when risk rises
                          • VRP \(=\) VIX\(^2\) minus the objective expected variance \(\hat\sigma^2_t\)
                        • The market's expectation as a weighted average of the past (equation 4 of the paper): \(\mathrm{VIX}^2_t = a + b \sum_{j=0}^{11} \phi^j\, rv_{t-j} + \varepsilon_t\)
                          • \(rv_t\): realised variance of month \(t\), the sum of squared daily S&P 500 log returns, annualised
                          • \(\phi = 0\): only the current month counts; a larger \(\phi\) means expectations that move more slowly
                        • The paper's numbers (Journal of Finance, Section I): \(\phi = 0.42\) (standard error \(0.12\)) for VIX\(^2\), January 1990 to April 2020
                          • after a shock to expected variance, the VRP response is negative at first and changes sign after the third month (Figure 2)
                        • The consequence the authors propose: a weak risk–return tradeoff, because the premium rises only once volatility has started to fall

                          Why \(1/\sqrt{N}\)? The Discrete Hedging Error

                          Slide 103 in the PDF
                          • Over one interval \(\Delta t = T/N\): time decay against realised squared return
                            • the hedged book earns \(\tfrac{1}{2}\Gamma S^2\left[\sigma^2\Delta t - (\Delta S/S)^2\right]\)
                          • Mean 0 under the model to leading order in \(\Delta t\)
                            • \(\mathrm{Var}[(\Delta S/S)^2] \approx 2\sigma^4\Delta t^2\)
                            • the terms are approximately uncorrelated
                            • so \(\mathrm{Var}(\text{error}) \approx \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^4 \Delta t\, E\!\int_0^T \Gamma_t^2 S_t^4\,dt\)

                          Slide 104 in the PDF

                          VRP: Inference with Overlapping Horizons

                          Slide 111 in the PDF
                          • If one-month errors are serially uncorrelated, overlap makes \(u_t\) an MA(\(h - 1\))
                            • a moving average of order \(h - 1\)
                            • Newey–West (\(t_{NW}\), \(h\) lags), Hansen–Hodrick (\(t_{HH}\)) Hansen & Hodrick (1980), Hodrick 1B (\(t_{Hodrick}\)) Hodrick (1992): under the null its size is closer to the nominal level
                            • In a given sample \(t_{Hodrick}\) can still exceed \(t_{NW}\): at \(h = 3\), \(1.33\) vs \(0.69\)
                            • These bandwidths are choices, so check their sensitivity
                          • \(p_{boot}\): one-sided, under \(b = 0\) Stambaugh (1999)
                            • VRP simulated as AR(1), a first-order autoregression (\(\hat\phi = 0.13\))
                            • residuals resampled jointly
                          • On the full 1990–2026 sample no horizon is significant
                            • on 1990–2007 (Section 11) \(h = 3\) is significant
