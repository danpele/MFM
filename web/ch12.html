Black & Scholes (1973) obtain the price of an option from the absence of arbitrage: a portfolio of the stock and cash, rebalanced continuously, replicates the option, and its price does not depend on the stock's expected return. In this model all options on the same asset have the same implied volatility. Replication in practice is discrete. In the chapter's simulation, daily delta hedging of a three-month call leaves an error with a standard deviation of 9.5% of the premium, and weekly rebalancing one of 21.5%.

Market prices do not respect the assumption of a single volatility. Since the crash of October 1987, low-strike puts have been much more expensive than at-the-money options, and implied volatility depends on the strike: the smile and the skew. The historical distribution of 9,225 overlapping monthly S&P 500 returns gives a one-month implied volatility of 30.6% at a strike of 80% of the current price and of 13.9% at the money. Option prices therefore allow one to recover the risk-neutral distribution of the future price. On the Bitcoin option surface of 22 September 2026, at the 38-day expiry, the probability of a fall of more than 30% is 1.24%, against 0.14% under the log-normal benchmark. These probabilities are prices of insurance, not forecasts.

The gap between the volatility priced by the market and realised volatility is measurable. Over 9,203 days in 1990–2026, the VIX has a mean of 19.4, and the realised volatility of the S&P 500 over the next 21 trading days a mean of 15.3. The ex-post variance risk premium (VRP) is 4.09 vol points, with a Newey–West standard error of 0.27, and it is positive on 86% of days. The exceptions are crashes: the worst value, −72.0 points, belongs to the window starting on 20 February 2020. As a forecast of next month's variance, the VIX has a Mincer–Zarnowitz slope of 0.86, and the joint test rejects unbiasedness. The chapter therefore studies what information options carry about future risk and with which standard errors it must be read.

Motivation

An option pays only in some states of the world, so its price depends on the distribution of the underlying asset at expiry, not only on the direction in which the price is expected to move. The chapter first derives option prices from the absence of arbitrage and then reads the prices observed in the market as information about future volatility and about crash risk. Throughout, \(S_t\) is the price of the underlying at time \(t\), \(K\) the strike, \(T\) the expiry, \(\tau = T - t\) the time left in years and \(r\) the continuously compounded risk-free rate; the full notation is in Further Topics.

What Do Option Prices Reveal About the Future Distribution?

Slide 2 in the PDF

A put with a low strike pays only after a large fall of the underlying, and a call with a high strike only after a large rise. The prices of such contracts therefore measure how the market values particular regions of the distribution of the future price. Taken across many strikes and expiries, option prices reveal what the market expects about volatility and about crashes, and at what price it insures against them.

The chapter has two parts. Part I prices options by replication: put–call parity, static spanning of a pay-off with options, the binomial tree, the Black–Scholes equation, the sensitivities of the price (the Greeks) and the error left by hedging at discrete dates. It then introduces implied volatility, the smile and a full Bitcoin option surface with its risk-neutral density. Part II reads implied volatility as information: variance swaps and the VIX, the Cboe index of 30-day S&P 500 implied volatility, the variance risk premium and 0DTE options, which have zero days to expiry.

Two case studies follow: lower bounds on the equity premium computed from option prices, and the reaction of the VIX to volatility shocks. The last part applies the discovery loop with an artificial intelligence (AI) assistant to an open question about the variance risk premium.

The Formula and Its Authors

Slide 4 in the PDF

The key idea of Black & Scholes (1973) is that a portfolio of the stock and cash, adjusted continuously, can replicate the pay-off of an option. If two positions have the same pay-off in every state, they must have the same price, otherwise there is an arbitrage. The price of the option follows from this argument, without any assumption about investors' preferences or about the expected return of the stock.

Myron Scholes and Robert C. Merton received the 1997 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences; Fischer Black had died in 1995. The derivation is given in the Black–Scholes section; the data and notation are in Further Topics.

Parity and Static Replication

This section collects the results that follow from the absence of arbitrage alone. They hold for any model of the underlying price: put–call parity, the static replication of a smooth pay-off with a bond, a forward and a strip of options, and three consequences used later in the chapter, the variance strip, the risk-neutral density and finite option strategies. The options are European, with strike \(K\) and expiry \(T\); \(C\) and \(P\) denote the prices of a call and a put.

Put–Call Parity: What Does No Arbitrage Alone Imply?

Slide 5 in the PDF

Consider two portfolios, built today from European options with the same strike \(K\) and expiry \(T\) on a stock without dividends. Portfolio A holds one call and cash \(K e^{-r\tau}\), which grows to \(K\) at \(T\); its pay-off is \(\max(S_T, K)\). Portfolio B holds one put and one share; its pay-off is \(\max(K, S_T)\). The two portfolios pay the same amount in every state of the world, so they must cost the same today:

\[C_t - P_t = S_t - K e^{-r\tau}.\]
(12.1)

If the left-hand side of (12.1) exceeded the right-hand side, an investor could sell portfolio A, buy portfolio B and keep the difference today, with a zero net pay-off at \(T\); the opposite inequality is exploited the other way round. With a continuous dividend yield \(q\), \(S_t\) is replaced by \(S_t e^{-q\tau}\).

The argument uses no model for \(S\): parity follows from the absence of arbitrage alone (Merton, 1973). A consequence is used throughout the chapter. The Black–Scholes prices of a call and a put satisfy (12.1) for every value of \(\sigma\), so a volatility that reproduces the market price of the call also reproduces the market price of the put. A call and a put with the same \(K\) and \(T\) therefore have the same implied volatility.

In brief

  • Two portfolios with the same pay-off at \(T\) (European options, same \(K\) and \(T\), no dividends):
    • A: one call + cash \(K e^{-r\tau}\) (worth \(K\) at \(T\)) \(\Rightarrow\) \(\max(S_T, K)\)
    • B: one put + one share \(\Rightarrow\) \(\max(K, S_T)\)
  • Same pay-off in every state, so the same price today, otherwise there is an arbitrage:
    • \(C_t - P_t = S_t - K e^{-r\tau}\) (with dividend yield \(q\): \(S_t e^{-q\tau}\) instead of \(S_t\))
  • No model for \(S\) is needed: parity uses only no-arbitrage Merton (1973)
  • Consequence: a call and a put with the same \(K\) and \(T\) have the same implied volatility

Static Replication: Any Pay-Off from a Bond, a Forward and Options

Slide 6 in the PDF

Parity replicates one pay-off with a put, a call, a share and cash. The spanning result of Carr and Madan generalises it to any twice-differentiable pay-off \(f(S_T)\). For any point \(F\), Taylor's formula with integral remainder gives

\[f(S_T) = f(F) + f'(F)(S_T - F) + \int_0^F f''(K)\,(K - S_T)^+\,dK + \int_F^\infty f''(K)\,(S_T - K)^+\,dK,\]
(12.2)

where \((x)^+ = \max(x, 0)\). The proof, by integration by parts, is in the Appendix.

The terms of (12.2) are positions in traded contracts. The constant \(f(F)\) is a zero-coupon bond, \(f'(F)(S_T - F)\) is \(f'(F)\) forward contracts with delivery price \(F\), and the two integrals are strips of puts with strikes below \(F\) and calls with strikes above \(F\), with \(f''(K)\,dK\) options at each strike. The curvature of the pay-off is bought with options; its level and slope with a bond and a forward.

Each piece has a market price today: the bond costs \(e^{-r\tau}\) per unit, a forward with delivery price equal to the forward price \(F\) costs 0, and the options cost \(P(K)\) and \(C(K)\), today's put and call prices at strike \(K\). The value of the pay-off today is therefore

\[V_0 = e^{-r\tau} f(F) + \int_0^F f''(K)\,P(K)\,dK + \int_F^\infty f''(K)\,C(K)\,dK,\]

whatever the model for \(S\) (Bakshi, Kapadia & Madan, 2003). No pricing measure is needed for \(V_0\). The pricing measure \(Q\) of Section 3 is needed only to read option prices as expectations, as in the density result of the next slide.

In brief

  • Carr–Madan spanning: any twice-differentiable pay-off \(f(S_T)\) and any point \(F\):
    • Taylor with integral remainder; proof in the Appendix
    • \(f(S_T) = f(F) + f'(F)(S_T - F) + \displaystyle\int_0^F f''(K)\,(K - S_T)^+ dK + \int_F^\infty f''(K)\,(S_T - K)^+ dK\)
    • Reading: a bond, a forward and \(f''(K)\,dK\) puts below \(F\) and calls above \(F\)
    • Priced today (\(F\) the forward): \(V_0 = e^{-r\tau} f(F) + \int_0^F f''(K)\,P(K)\,dK + \int_F^\infty f''(K)\,C(K)\,dK\)
    • \(P(K)\), \(C(K)\): today's put and call prices at strike \(K\), whatever the model for \(S\) Bakshi, Kapadia & Madan (2003)
  • Why this price: each piece has a market price today
    • the bond \(e^{-r\tau}\) per unit, the forward 0, the options \(P(K)\) and \(C(K)\)
    • No model for \(S\) and no pricing measure are needed for \(V_0\); the measure \(Q\) of Section 3 is needed only to read option prices as expectations, as in the density bullet of the next slide

Static Replication: Three Results Used Later

Slide 7 in the PDF

A price is called "risk-neutral" when it is the discounted mean pay-off under the pricing measure \(Q\), not under the real-world probabilities. The measure \(Q\) is constructed in Section 3, on the binomial tree. Three applications of (12.2) recur in the chapter.

The first is variance. The pay-off \(f(S) = -2\ln(S/F)\) has \(f''(K) = 2/K^2\), so it is replicated by a strip of out-of-the-money (OTM) options with weights proportional to \(1/K^2\). This strip is the variance swap and the VIX of Section 8. Low strikes receive the largest weights, so an expensive put wing makes variance expensive.

The second is the density. A butterfly spread, long one call at \(K - h\) and one at \(K + h\) and short two calls at \(K\), where \(h\) is the strike step, costs \(C(K - h) - 2C(K) + C(K + h)\); divided by \(h^2\), this price converges to the second derivative of the call price in the strike. Breeden & Litzenberger (1978) show that this derivative is the discounted risk-neutral density \(q\) of \(S_T\):

\[q(K) = e^{r\tau}\,\frac{\partial^2 C(K)}{\partial K^2}.\]
(12.3)

The risk-neutral density is thus a price, recovered from a continuum of call prices (Section 7).

The third concerns common strategies. Straddles, spreads and protective puts are finite strips, with \(f''\) concentrated at one or two strikes. Pay-offs that are powers of the log return give the risk-neutral moments, and the pay-off \(S^2\) gives SVIX, the simple VIX of Martin (2017); both are in the Appendix.

In brief

  • “Risk-neutral”: a price is the discounted mean pay-off under the pricing measure \(Q\)
    • not under real-world probabilities
    • \(Q\) is constructed in Section 3
  • Variance: \(f(S) = -2\ln(S/F)\) has \(f''(K) = 2/K^2\)
    • the strip with weights \(1/K^2\) is the variance swap and the VIX (Section 8)
    • Low strikes get the largest weights, so an expensive put wing makes variance expensive
  • Density: a butterfly \([C(K - h) - 2C(K) + C(K + h)]/h^2 \to \partial^2 C/\partial K^2 = e^{-r\tau} q(K)\) Breeden & Litzenberger (1978)
    • \(h\): the strike step; the risk-neutral density is a price (Section 7)
  • Straddles, spreads and protective puts are finite strips
    • \(f''\) concentrated at one or two strikes
    • moments and SVIX (simple VIX, the variance index of Martin (2017)): Appendix

Section summary

Parity (12.1), the spanning formula (12.2) and the density (12.3) need no model for the underlying. They turn option prices into the prices of other pay-offs: the forward, variance and the probability of each region of \(S_T\).

The Binomial Tree

The binomial tree is the simplest model in which an option can be replicated dynamically. It shows how the pricing measure \(Q\) arises from replication, why the real probability of an up move does not enter the price, and how, with many steps, the tree converges to the Black–Scholes model.

The Binomial Tree: How Do We Price an Option by Replication?

Slide 8 in the PDF

Over one period of length \(\Delta t\), the stock moves from \(S\) to \(uS\) or to \(dS\), where \(u\) and \(d\) are the up and down factors. The gross riskless return is \(R = e^{r\Delta t}\), and absence of arbitrage requires \(d < R < u\). A call worth \(C_u\) after an up move and \(C_d\) after a down move is replicated by \(\Delta\) shares and cash \(B\) if \(\Delta uS + RB = C_u\) and \(\Delta dS + RB = C_d\).

The two equations give \(\Delta = (C_u - C_d)/[(u - d)S]\), and the price of the call is the cost of the replicating portfolio:

\[C = \Delta S + B = \frac{q\,C_u + (1 - q)\,C_d}{R}, \qquad q = \frac{R - d}{u - d}.\]
(12.4)

Because \(d < R < u\), \(q\) lies in \((0, 1)\). It is the unique probability under which the discounted stock price \(S_t/R^t\) is a martingale; on the tree, \(q\) denotes this probability, not the dividend yield. The real probability of an up move never enters (12.4): risk preferences are already contained in the stock price \(S\).

The fundamental theorem of asset pricing generalises this result: as shown by Harrison & Pliska (1981) and, in full generality, by Delbaen & Schachermayer (1994), there is no arbitrage if and only if there exists an equivalent martingale measure \(Q\). \(Q\) is unique exactly when the market is complete. With jumps or stochastic volatility there are many measures \(Q\), and option prices carry risk premia, the subject of Section 8.

In brief

  • One period of length \(\Delta t\): \(S \to uS\) or \(dS\), with \(d < R = e^{r\Delta t} < u\)
    • \(u\), \(d\): up and down factors
    • \(R\): gross riskless return
  • Replicate the call with \(\Delta\) shares and cash \(B\): \(\Delta uS + RB = C_u\) and \(\Delta dS + RB = C_d\)
    • \(C_u\), \(C_d\): the call values after an up and a down move
    • Solution: \(\Delta = \dfrac{C_u - C_d}{(u - d)S}\), and the price is \(C = \Delta S + B = \dfrac{q\,C_u + (1 - q)\,C_d}{R}\)
    • \(q = \dfrac{R - d}{u - d} \in (0, 1)\) (here a probability, not the dividend yield) is the unique probability under which \(S_t/R^t\) is a martingale
  • The real probability of an up move never enters
    • risk preferences are already in the price \(S\)
  • Fundamental theorem: no arbitrage \(\Leftrightarrow\) an equivalent martingale measure \(Q\) exists Harrison & Pliska (1981), Delbaen & Schachermayer (1994)
    • \(Q\) is unique exactly when the market is complete; with jumps or stochastic volatility there are many \(Q\), and option prices carry risk premia (Section 8)

The CRR Tree: \(N\) Steps and Convergence to Black–Scholes

Slide 9 in the PDF

One period has only two final states. With \(N\) steps the tree has \(N + 1\) final states, which approximate the distribution of \(S_T\). The Cox–Ross–Rubinstein (CRR) tree (Cox, Ross & Rubinstein, 1979) sets \(\Delta t = T/N\), \(u = e^{\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}}\) and \(d = 1/u\). This choice gives the one-step log return the variance \(\sigma^2\Delta t\) up to terms of order \(\Delta t^2\), so as \(N\) grows the tree converges to the geometric Brownian motion of the Black–Scholes model. The risk-neutral probability \(q = (e^{r\Delta t} - d)/(u - d)\) is the same at every node.

A European call depends only on the final node, reached after \(j\) up moves and \(N - j\) down moves. Applying (12.4) backwards through the \(N\) steps gives

\[C_0 = e^{-rT}\sum_{j=0}^{N}\binom{N}{j} q^j (1 - q)^{N - j}\,\big(S_0 u^j d^{N - j} - K\big)^+,\]
(12.5)

the discounted risk-neutral mean of the pay-off, with a binomial distribution for the number of up moves \(j\).

An American option can be exercised at any time up to \(T\), so it is valued backwards from \(T\), taking at every node the larger of the continuation value and the exercise value. The convergence of (12.5) to the Black–Scholes price and the early-exercise premium of an American put are shown in Further Topics.

In brief

  • Why \(N\) steps: one period has only two final states
    • with \(N\) steps the \(N + 1\) final states approximate the distribution of \(S_T\)
  • The CRR (Cox–Ross–Rubinstein) tree Cox, Ross & Rubinstein (1979): \(\Delta t = T/N\), \(u = e^{\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}}\), \(d = 1/u\)
    • This choice gives the one-step log return the variance \(\sigma^2\Delta t\) (up to terms of order \(\Delta t^2\)), so the tree converges to the geometric Brownian motion of the Black–Scholes model
    • \(q = (e^{r\Delta t} - d)/(u - d)\) is the same at every node
  • European call: \(C_0 = e^{-rT}\displaystyle\sum_{j = 0}^{N}\binom{N}{j} q^j (1 - q)^{N - j}\,\big(S_0 u^j d^{N - j} - K\big)^+\)
    • \(j\): the number of up moves; the sum is the discounted risk-neutral mean of the pay-off
  • American options: value backwards from \(T\)
    • taking \(\max\)(continuation value, exercise value) at every node

Black–Scholes and the Greeks

In continuous time, replication leads to a partial differential equation. This section derives the Black–Scholes equation, gives its solution for European options and defines the Greeks, the partial derivatives of the price. The Greeks measure the exposures of an option position and are the quantities a hedger adjusts.

The Black–Scholes Equation: What Price Does Continuous Replication Give?

Slide 10 in the PDF

The model assumes that the stock follows a geometric Brownian motion (GBM), \(dS_t = \mu S_t\,dt + \sigma S_t\,dW_t\), with constant volatility \(\sigma\) and constant rate \(r\); trading is continuous, without costs, and short selling is allowed. The option value \(V(S, t)\) is to be replicated by a self-financing portfolio \(\Pi\) of \(\Delta_t\) shares and cash \(B_t\) invested at \(r\), whose value changes only through gains: \(d\Pi = \Delta\,dS + rB\,dt\).

By Itô's lemma, the option value evolves as

\[dV = \big(V_t + \mu S V_S + \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2 S^2 V_{SS}\big)\,dt + \sigma S V_S\,dW_t,\]

where \(V_t\), \(V_S\) and \(V_{SS}\) are partial derivatives. Choosing \(\Delta = V_S\) and starting from \(\Pi = V\), so that \(B = V - S V_S\), gives \(d(V - \Pi) = \big(V_t + \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2 S^2 V_{SS} - rB\big)\,dt\). Both the Brownian increment \(dW_t\) and the drift \(\mu\) drop out.

The difference \(V - \Pi\) starts at zero and is riskless. A riskless position that costs nothing must earn nothing, which gives the Black–Scholes equation of Black & Scholes (1973) and Merton (1973):

\[V_t + \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2 S^2 V_{SS} + r S V_S - r V = 0, \qquad V(S, T) = \text{pay-off}.\]
(12.6)

That \(\mu\) is irrelevant is a result of replication, not an assumption. By the Feynman–Kac formula, the solution of (12.6) is \(V = e^{-r\tau}E^Q[\text{pay-off}]\), where under \(Q\) the stock drifts at the rate \(r\).

In brief

  • Geometric Brownian motion \(dS_t = \mu S_t\,dt + \sigma S_t\,dW_t\)
    • constant \(\sigma\) and \(r\)
    • continuous trading, no costs, short selling allowed
  • Replicate the option value \(V(S, t)\) with a self-financing portfolio \(\Pi\)
    • \(\Delta_t\) shares and cash \(B_t\) at \(r\): \(d\Pi = \Delta\,dS + rB\,dt\)
  • Itô's lemma: \(dV = \left(V_t + \mu S V_S + \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2 S^2 V_{SS}\right)dt + \sigma S V_S\,dW_t\)
    • \(V_t\), \(V_S\), \(V_{SS}\): the partial derivatives
  • Choose \(\Delta = V_S\), start from \(\Pi = V\) (\(B = V - S V_S\))
    • \(d(V - \Pi) = \left(V_t + \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2 S^2 V_{SS} - rB\right)dt\)
    • both \(dW_t\) and \(\mu\) drop out
  • A riskless position that costs nothing must earn nothing
    • hence the Black–Scholes equation Black & Scholes (1973), Merton (1973): \(V_t + \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2 S^2 V_{SS} + r S V_S - r V = 0\)
    • with \(V(S, T)\) = pay-off
  • That \(\mu\) is irrelevant is a result, not an assumption

The Black–Scholes Formula

Slide 11 in the PDF

For a European call and put on a stock without dividends, (12.6) has the closed-form solution

\[C = S\,\Phi(d_1) - K e^{-r\tau}\,\Phi(d_2), \qquad P = K e^{-r\tau}\,\Phi(-d_2) - S\,\Phi(-d_1),\]
(12.7)

with

\[d_1 = \frac{\ln(S/K) + (r + \sigma^2/2)\tau}{\sigma\sqrt{\tau}}, \qquad d_2 = d_1 - \sigma\sqrt{\tau},\]

where \(\Phi\) is the distribution function of the standard Normal distribution. With a dividend yield \(q\), \(S\) is replaced by \(S e^{-q\tau}\).

The two terms have a direct reading. \(\Phi(d_2)\) is the risk-neutral probability that the call ends in the money, so the second term is the present value of paying \(K\) in those states. \(\Phi(d_1)\) is the number of shares in the replicating portfolio, the delta. The put formula follows from the call formula and parity (12.1).

The formula has five inputs. The price \(S\), the strike \(K\), the time to expiry \(\tau\) and the rate \(r\) are observed. Volatility \(\sigma\) is the only input that is not observed: it must be estimated from past prices or implied from option prices, the subject of Section 6.

    Delta Hedging in Practice

    Black–Scholes replication requires continuous rebalancing, which no trader can carry out. This section measures the error left when the hedge is adjusted at \(N\) dates, shows how it falls with \(N\), and derives the P&L of a hedged option when realised volatility differs from the volatility at which the option was sold.

    Discrete Delta Hedging: How Much Risk Is Left with \(N\) Rebalancings?

    Slide 14 in the PDF

    In practice the hedge is adjusted \(N\) times, at the dates \(t_i = iT/N\). At \(t_0 = 0\) the hedger sells the call at its Black–Scholes price \(C_0\), buys \(\Delta_0 = \Phi(d_1)\) shares and keeps the cash \(B_0 = C_0 - \Delta_0 S_0\). At each date \(t_i\), \(i = 1, \dots, N - 1\), the position is rebalanced to the new delta, \(\Delta_i = \Phi\big(d_1(S_{t_i}, T - t_i)\big)\), and the purchase or sale of shares is financed from the cash account, which earns interest:

    \[B_i = B_{i-1}e^{rT/N} - (\Delta_i - \Delta_{i-1})\,S_{t_i}.\]

    The portfolio is therefore self-financing. At expiry the hedger holds \(\Delta_{N-1}\) shares and the cash, and pays the call pay-off. The hedging error is

    \[\varepsilon_N = \Delta_{N-1}S_T + B_{N-1}e^{rT/N} - (S_T - K)^+.\]
    (12.9)

    With continuous rebalancing in the Black–Scholes model, \(\varepsilon_N\) would be zero on every path.

    The chapter's simulation uses a geometric Brownian motion with drift \(\mu = 8\%\) and volatility \(\sigma = 20\%\), a call with \(S_0 = K = 100\), a rate \(r = 4\%\) and an expiry \(T = 0.25\) years, and 20,000 paths for each of \(N = 3\), 12, 63 and 252 rebalancings. The drift is deliberately different from \(r\): replication never uses \(\mu\), and a real drift different from \(r\) shows that the error stays centred at 0.

      Discrete Delta Hedging: Algorithm and Error (2/2)

      Slide 16 in the PDF

      The error of discrete hedging comes from gamma. Between two rebalancing dates the delta held is fixed, while the delta required by the model moves with the price; the position gains or loses according to whether the squared return over the interval is below or above its expected value \(\sigma^2\Delta t\). These local errors have mean close to zero and are approximately uncorrelated, so their variance adds up over the \(N\) intervals and the standard deviation of the total falls like \(1/\sqrt{N}\). The Appendix derives the approximation

      \[\mathrm{sd}(\varepsilon_N) \approx \sqrt{\pi/4}\;\frac{\mathcal{V}\sigma}{\sqrt{N}},\]
      (12.10)

      where \(\mathcal{V}\) is the vega of the option.

With costs proportional to the traded value, in the standard diffusion setting, the expected cost grows roughly like \(\sqrt{N}\). The hedging frequency is therefore chosen by trading off the error (12.10) against the cost model.

      Slide 17 in the PDF

      Suppose the option is sold at the implied volatility \(\sigma_{imp}\) and hedged with \(\Delta = \partial_S V^{imp}\), where \(V^{imp}\) is its Black–Scholes value at \(\sigma_{imp}\), while the price moves with the realised volatility \(\sigma_{real}\): \(dS = \mu S\,dt + \sigma_{real} S\,dW_t\).       \[\text{P\&L} = \int_0^T e^{r(T - t)}\,\tfrac{1}{2}\,\Gamma_t S_t^2\big(\sigma_{imp}^2 - \sigma_{real,t}^2\big)\,dt.\]

        Implied Volatility: Which \(\sigma\) Explains the Market Price?

        Why Smiles Exist and How Models Produce Them (1/2)

        Slide 21 in the PDF

        Four mechanisms explain the smile. The first is fat tails: large moves are more frequent than the Normal distribution allows (Chapter 1), so deep out-of-the-money options are worth more than Black–Scholes says.

        The second is leverage and volatility feedback: when prices fall, volatility rises (Christie, 1982). The resulting negative skewness of returns makes low-strike puts dearer. The third is crash fear: the market prices the risk of a negative jump, which is visible in put prices (Bates, 1991).

        The fourth is the demand for insurance: investors buy puts, and intermediaries, who cannot fully hedge the risk of the positions they take on, charge a premium for them (Gârleanu, Pedersen & Poteshman, 2009). The first three mechanisms concern the distribution of returns; the fourth concerns who bears the risk.

          No-Arbitrage Constraints on the Smile

          Slide 28 in the PDF

          Three constraints restrict an implied-volatility surface. The first concerns the wings. Lee (2004) bounds their asymptotic slope: \(\limsup_{|k| \to \infty} w(k)/|k| \le 2\). For SVI, \(b(1 + |\rho|) \le 2\) is necessary, and \(w \ge 0\) requires \(a + b s\sqrt{1 - \rho^2} \ge 0\). A steeper wing means fatter tails of \(S_T\) under \(Q\); the moment formula is in the Appendix.

          The second is the butterfly constraint: call prices must be convex in \(K\), so that the density \(q\) of (12.3) is nonnegative (Gatheral & Jacquier, 2014). Written in \(w\), with \(w' = \partial_k w\) and \(w'' = \partial_k^2 w\), it requires

          \[g(k) = \Big(1 - \frac{k w'}{2w}\Big)^2 - \frac{w'^2}{4}\Big(\frac{1}{w} + \frac{1}{4}\Big) + \frac{w''}{2} \ge 0 \quad \text{for all } k,\]
          (12.14)

          together with \(d_+(k) = -k/\sqrt{w} + \sqrt{w}/2 \to -\infty\) as \(k \to \infty\) (Lemma 2.2 of Gatheral & Jacquier (2014)). The condition \(g(k) \ge 0\) is exactly a nonnegative density.

          The third is the calendar constraint: \(\partial_\tau w(k, \tau) \ge 0\) for every \(k\), so total variance cannot fall with maturity. Slice-by-slice least squares imposes none of the three. SSVI, the surface version of SVI, has simple sufficient conditions for all of them (Theorems 4.1–4.2 of Gatheral & Jacquier (2014)).

            The Risk-Neutral Density: From Prices to a Density

            Slide 30 in the PDF

            By (12.3), the risk-neutral density is \(q(K) = e^{r\tau}\,\partial^2 C(K)/\partial K^2\), and the butterfly condition (12.14) is exactly the requirement \(q \ge 0\). From noisy, discrete quotes, the second derivative is an ill-posed inverse problem: differentiating twice amplifies the noise in the prices. The chapter therefore fits a smooth smile, the SVI of (12.13), and differentiates the fitted call prices; other estimators are in the Appendix.

            The uncertainty of the density is measured with a pairs bootstrap of the quotes: the quotes are resampled with their strikes, the smile is refitted and the density recomputed. The bootstrap gives pointwise bands for \(q\) and intervals for tail probabilities. Beyond the last quoted strike the tail is an extrapolation of the SVI wing, so the report must say where the quotes end.

            As Aït-Sahalia & Lo (1998) and Grith, Härdle & Park (2013) stress, the density \(q\) mixes probabilities and risk aversion. The pricing kernel is proportional to \(q/p\), where \(p\) is the physical density. Its estimated shape is not monotone, which is the pricing-kernel puzzle (Jackwerth, 2000).

              Variance Swaps: What Does Future Variance Cost?

              Slide 33 in the PDF

              A variance swap pays at \(T\) the notional times the difference between the realised variance and a strike \(K_{var}\) fixed today. Realised variance is \(\frac{252}{n}\sum r_t^2\), where \(r_t\) are the \(n\) daily log returns up to \(T\). The fair strike, at which the swap costs nothing, is the risk-neutral expected variance (Demeterfi, Derman, Kamal & Zou, 1999). By the spanning formula (12.2) with \(f = -2\ln(S/F)\), it is a strip of OTM puts and calls weighted by \(1/K^2\):

              \[K_{var} = \frac{2e^{r\tau}}{\tau}\left(\int_0^F \frac{P(K)}{K^2}\,dK + \int_F^\infty \frac{C(K)}{K^2}\,dK\right).\]
              (12.15)

              The strip prices variance because of Itô's lemma applied to \(\ln S\). With continuous paths and \(r = q = 0\) (Britten-Jones & Neuberger, 2000),

              \[-2\ln(S_T/F) = \int_0^T \sigma_t^2\,dt - 2\int_0^T \frac{dS_t}{S_t},\]

              and the last term is a \(Q\)-martingale. The log pay-off is therefore equal to integrated variance plus a position in the underlying that has zero value under \(Q\). "Model-free" means free of a model for \(\sigma_t\), not free of the assumption of continuous paths: with crash jumps the strip understates expected realised variance (Carr & Wu, 2009). The size of this bias is in the Appendix.

              The two sides of the swap have different exposures. The buyer of variance holds insurance against turbulence; the seller earns a premium and pays in crashes (Carr & Wu, 2009).

                • Fair strike: the risk-neutral expected variance Demeterfi, Derman, Kamal & Zou (1999)

              The VIX Index: the Cboe Formula

              Slide 34 in the PDF

              The VIX is the discrete version of the strip (12.15), computed by Cboe from S&P 500 options (Cboe, 2025):

              \[\sigma^2 = \frac{2}{T}\sum_i \frac{\Delta K_i}{K_i^2}\,e^{RT}\,Q(K_i) - \frac{1}{T}\Big(\frac{F}{K_0} - 1\Big)^2.\]
              (12.16)

              Here \(T\) is the time to expiry in years, \(R\) the risk-free rate, \(K_i\) the strikes and \(\Delta K_i\) the spacing between adjacent strikes. \(K_0\) is the first strike at or below the forward index level \(F\), and \(Q(K_i)\) is the bid–ask mid of the put for \(K_i < K_0\), of the call for \(K_i > K_0\) and the average of both at \(K_0\). In this formula \(Q(K_i)\) denotes a quoted price, not the pricing measure.

              The sum is the integral of (12.15) on the grid of listed strikes. The second term corrects for the strip being split at \(K_0\) rather than exactly at \(F\): at strikes between \(K_0\) and \(F\) the formula uses a put where the strip requires a call.                 The Variance Risk Premium

                Slide 36 in the PDF

                The variance risk premium (VRP) is the difference between the variance priced by options and the variance expected by investors, as defined by Carr & Wu (2009) and Bollerslev, Tauchen & Zhou (2009). Three versions must not be confused. The economic, ex-ante premium is \(E^Q_t[RV_{t,t+21}] - E^P_t[RV_{t,t+21}]\), where \(P\) is the real-world measure; \(VIX_t^2\) estimates the first term, while the second needs a forecast. The ex-post premium, used in this section,

                \[VRP_t = VIX_t^2 - RV_{t,t+21}, \qquad RV_{t,t+21} = \frac{252}{21}\sum_{i=1}^{21} r_{t+i}^2,\]
                (12.17)

                is the realised P&L of the seller of a variance swap and is known only at \(t + 21\). The ex-ante proxy, \(VIX_t^2 - RV_{t-21,t}\), uses past variance as the forecast of \(E^P_t\); it is known at \(t\), so it can serve as a predictor (Section 11).

                In (12.17), \(r_{t+i}\) are the daily log returns in %, so \(RV\) is annualised and measured in %\(^2\), like \(VIX^2\). The 21 trading days cover roughly the same month as the 30 calendar days of the VIX. The premium is positive on average if investors dislike states with high volatility, which are also bad states for their wealth.

                The windows of consecutive days overlap by 20 of 21 days, so the series (12.17) is autocorrelated by construction. The standard error of its mean needs Newey–West with at least 21 lags (Newey & West, 1987). Twenty-one lags cover the overlap but not the persistence of volatility, so the sensitivity to longer bandwidths is reported: the standard error of the premium, in volatility points, is 0.27 with 21 lags, 0.31 with 42, 0.32 with 63 and 0.33 with 126.

                      Case Study: the Kodde–Palm Tests

                      Slide 46 in the PDF

                      The moment inequalities are tested jointly with the statistics of Kodde & Palm (1986) (eqs. 11–14 of the paper). Let \(\bar\lambda\) be the vector of the 10 sample means of the moments and \(\Sigma\) their covariance, estimated with the method of Hansen & Hodrick (1980) with as many lags as days in the horizon. The Hansen–Hodrick covariance keeps, with equal weights, exactly the autocovariances created by the overlap of the return windows.

                      Validity, \(H_0\): \(\lambda_0 \ge 0\), is tested by the distance from \(\bar\lambda\) to the region in which all moments are nonnegative, and tightness, \(H_0\): \(\lambda_0 = 0\) against \(\lambda_0 \ge 0\), by the remaining part of the Wald statistic:

                      \[D_1 = \min_{\lambda \ge 0}\,(\lambda - \bar\lambda)^\top\Sigma^{-1}(\lambda - \bar\lambda), \qquad D_2 = \bar\lambda^\top\Sigma^{-1}\bar\lambda - D_1.\]
                      (12.20)

                      \(D_1\) is zero if all sample moments are already positive. A large \(D_2\) rejects tightness.

                      A two-moment example with \(\Sigma = I\) shows the mechanics. With \(\bar\lambda = (0.5, -0.2)\), the closest point with nonnegative coordinates is \((0.5, 0)\), so \(D_1 = 0.2^2 = 0.04\) and \(D_2 = 0.29 - 0.04 = 0.25\). With \(\bar\lambda = (0.5, 0.2)\), \(D_1 = 0\) and \(D_2 = 0.29\).

                      Under \(H_0\) some inequality constraints bind and others do not, so the statistics do not have a single \(\chi^2\) distribution. Both follow weighted mixtures of \(\chi^2_0, \dots, \chi^2_{10}\), and Kodde & Palm (1986) tabulate bounds on the critical values.

                        Slide 52 in the PDF

The authors propose that this explains a weak risk–return tradeoff: the premium rises only once volatility has started to fall.

                          Section summary

                          The first case study finds that the option-implied bounds on the equity premium are not rejected as valid and are not tight in 1990–2020: they cover less than half of the realised premium, and out of sample they do not beat the historical mean.                           The Section's Question and the Tools to Test It

                          Slide 57 in the PDF

Its score is the one-month residual under \(H_0\) times the sum of the last \(h\) values of the regressor, so the covariance needs no autocovariances of overlapping errors.

                          The second concerns out-of-sample evaluation. \(R^2_{OS} > 0\) means that the predictive model has a lower mean squared forecast error than the historical mean. For nested models, the Clark–West test (Clark & West, 2007) tests this; it adjusts for the noise that estimating a truly zero slope adds to the larger model, which otherwise penalises the predictive model under the null.

                          The third is the Holm correction for \(m\) tests, for example four horizons. The \(p\)-values are sorted; the smallest is compared with \(\alpha/m\), the next with \(\alpha/(m - 1)\), and so on, and the procedure stops at the first that fails. Only one hypothesis is rejected, although all three \(p\)-values are below 0.05.

                          What Option Prices Measure, and Their Limits

                          Slide 65 in the PDF

                          Parity, the bounds on option prices and static spanning need no model. The binomial tree and the Black–Scholes equation price by dynamic replication. Discrete hedging leaves an error of order \(1/\sqrt{N}\), and a delta-hedged option is a bet on realised against implied variance.

                          Implied variance exceeded realised variance on average, on the S&P 500 and on Bitcoin. The surface and the risk-neutral density are estimates: the no-arbitrage constraints must be checked, estimates that violate them rejected, and bands reported.                           Forecast-Evaluation Tools

                          Slide 91 in the PDF

                          Four tools are used to evaluate the VIX as a forecast. Instrumental variables use the lagged \(VIX^2\) as an instrument for the \(VIX^2\) measured with error.                           Why \(1/\sqrt{N}\)? The Discrete Hedging Error

                          Slide 103 in the PDF

                          Over one interval \(\Delta t = T/N\), the hedged book earns \(\tfrac{1}{2}\Gamma S^2[\sigma^2\Delta t - (\Delta S/S)^2]\), which has mean zero under the model to leading order. Since \(\mathrm{Var}[(\Delta S/S)^2] \approx 2\sigma^4\Delta t^2\) and the terms are approximately uncorrelated, the error has variance \(\tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^4\Delta t\,E\int_0^T\Gamma_t^2S_t^4\,dt\). For an ATM call this integral is \(K^2/(4\sigma^2)\), so the standard deviation is \(\sigma K\sqrt{T/(8N)} = \sqrt{\pi/4}\,\mathcal{V}\sigma/\sqrt{N}\), the approximation (12.10); at \(N = 12\) the two forms give 1.02 and 1.01. The asymptotic theory (Bertsimas, Kogan & Lo, 2000) fails with jumps, with stochastic volatility and with costs.

                          In brief

                          • Mean 0 under the model to leading order in \(\Delta t\)
