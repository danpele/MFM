The paper by Markowitz (1952) deals only with the second stage: choosing the portfolio given beliefs about the securities. When the means and covariances of returns are known, it has closed-form solutions, for example the global minimum-variance (GMV) portfolio and the tangency portfolio. In applications, the first stage means estimating these parameters from data. With 16 assets, 152 parameters must be estimated, 16 means and 136 variances and covariances, from a window of 60 monthly observations. The optimiser puts large weights precisely on the assets whose parameters carry the largest estimation errors.

The chapter's simulation measures the effect of these errors. The true parameters are the moments of the nine US sectors, and each simulated sample has 60 months. Across 2,000 estimated tangency portfolios, the median in-sample estimated Sharpe ratio is 1.56, while the median true Sharpe ratio, computed with the population parameters, is 0.30. Of these portfolios, 96% have a true Sharpe ratio below that of equal weights, 1/N, which is 0.54. In the same simulation, the estimated mean–variance (MV) portfolio beats 1/N only with windows of 480 months.

The out-of-sample backtest on real data points the same way. The wealth of the unconstrained MV portfolio reaches zero in all three US universes. In the combined universe of 16 ETFs, before costs, 1/N has a Sharpe ratio of 0.76 and the GMV rules between 0.20 and 0.36. The MV-LO rule, the long-only tangency portfolio, has the best point estimate, 0.81, but no longer beats 1/N at costs above about 22 basis points (bp). The chapter therefore studies the repairs of the inputs and the risk-based rules, which reduce the effect of estimation error, and the tests that decide whether a difference from 1/N remains significant after correcting for multiple testing.

Why Equal Weights Are Hard to Beat

The chapter deals with choosing the weights of a portfolio of risky assets. Classical theory gives the optimal portfolio in closed form, as a function of the means and covariances of the returns. In practice these parameters are not known but estimated from data, and the estimation errors pass into the weights. The benchmark used throughout the chapter is the equal-weight rule, 1/N, which estimates nothing. The question is whether any estimated rule beats it out of sample, after trading costs.

The theoretical results start in the next section.

Why Does Estimating the Optimum Change the Problem?

Slide 2 in the PDF

A portfolio of \(N\) assets is a weight vector \(\mathbf{w} = (w_1, \dots, w_N)^\top\) with \(\mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{1} = 1\), where \(\mathbf{1}\) is the vector of ones. When the means and covariances of the returns are known, the optimal portfolio has a closed form. The difficulty arises when they are replaced by estimates: the optimiser puts large weights precisely on the assets whose means and covariances carry the largest estimation errors.

The size of the problem shows why the errors are large. For 16 assets, 16 means and 136 distinct variances and covariances must be estimated, 152 parameters in all. With a window of 60 monthly observations, the parameters outnumber the observations by more than two to one. The question of the chapter is whether a rule estimated under these conditions can beat equal weights out of sample, after costs.

Let \(P_{i,t}\) be the price of asset \(i\) at the end of month \(t\), adjusted for dividends and other distributions; BET remains a price index. The simple (total) return is \(R_{i,t} = P_{i,t}/P_{i,t-1} - 1\), and the excess return is \(R^e_{i,t} = R_{i,t} - R_{f,t}\), where \(R_{f,t}\) is the risk-free rate. In the seminar the same quantities are written \(r_t\) and \(r^e_t\). The log return \(\ln(P_{i,t}/P_{i,t-1}) = \ln(1 + R_{i,t})\) adds up over time but not across assets. Portfolios therefore use simple returns: the portfolio return is exactly \(R_{p,t} = \sum_i w_i R_{i,t}\).

For a portfolio of \(N\) assets, \(\mathbf{w}\) is the vector of weights, \(\boldsymbol\mu\) the vector of expected (excess) returns, \(\mathbf{1}\) the vector of ones and \(\boldsymbol\Sigma\) the \(N\times N\) covariance matrix of the returns. The portfolio mean is \(\mathbf{w}^\top\boldsymbol\mu\) and the portfolio variance is \(\mathbf{w}^\top\boldsymbol\Sigma\mathbf{w}\).

Mean–Variance Optimisation

This section solves the portfolio choice problem under the assumption that the parameters are known. There are \(N\) risky assets, with vector of expected excess returns \(\boldsymbol\mu\) and covariance matrix \(\boldsymbol\Sigma\), assumed positive definite. A portfolio is a weight vector \(\mathbf{w}\) with \(\mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{1} = 1\); its mean is \(\mathbf{w}^\top\boldsymbol\mu\) and its variance \(\mathbf{w}^\top\boldsymbol\Sigma\mathbf{w}\). The horizon is a single period, negative weights are allowed until the last part of the section, and there are no transaction costs.

The problem of choosing by mean and variance is that of Markowitz (1952). The results are closed-form formulas: the global minimum-variance (GMV) portfolio, the tangency portfolio and the minimum-variance frontier (Merton, 1972). All of them are expressed through \(\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}\) and three scalars, \(A\), \(B\) and \(C\). They describe the choice of an investor who knows \(\boldsymbol\mu\) and \(\boldsymbol\Sigma\). In applications these parameters are estimated, and the following sections show how far the same formulas degrade when they are fed estimates. The known-parameter version is the benchmark against which this loss is measured.

The Markowitz Problem

Slide 7 in the PDF

Markowitz (1952) framed portfolio choice as a trade-off between two parameters of the distribution of the portfolio return: its mean and its variance. If \(\mathbf{R}\) is the vector of returns on the \(N\) assets, the portfolio return is \(R_p = \mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{R}\), with mean \(\mathbf{w}^\top\boldsymbol\mu\) and variance \(\mathbf{w}^\top\boldsymbol\Sigma\mathbf{w}\). Other features of the distribution, such as skewness and the tails, do not enter the criterion.

The minimum-variance frontier is obtained by fixing a target mean \(m\) and searching for the fully invested portfolio with the smallest variance:

\[\min_{\mathbf{w}}\ \mathbf{w}^\top\boldsymbol\Sigma\mathbf{w} \quad \text{s.t.} \quad \mathbf{w}^\top\boldsymbol\mu = m,\ \ \mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{1} = 1.\]
(4.1)

In (4.1), the constraint \(\mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{1} = 1\) requires the whole wealth to be invested in the \(N\) assets, and the constraint \(\mathbf{w}^\top\boldsymbol\mu = m\) fixes the mean. The objective is strictly convex, because \(\boldsymbol\Sigma\) is positive definite, and the constraints are linear. If \(\boldsymbol\mu\) is not proportional to \(\mathbf{1}\), there is therefore a single optimal portfolio for every \(m\). Its variance, denoted \(\sigma^2(m)\), traces a curve in the (volatility, mean) plane.

That \(\boldsymbol\mu\) contains excess returns does not change this problem. Because the weights sum to 1, switching to total returns adds the same constant \(R_f\) to the mean of every admissible portfolio: the frontier shifts vertically and its weights stay the same.

Not every point of the curve is a rational choice. The curve has a point of minimum variance, the GMV portfolio, with mean \(\mu_{\text{GMV}}\). For a target mean \(m < \mu_{\text{GMV}}\) there is a portfolio with the same variance and a higher mean, its mirror image about \(\mu_{\text{GMV}}\); the symmetry follows from the closed form (4.11) of the frontier, derived below. Only the upper branch, \(m \ge \mu_{\text{GMV}}\), is therefore the efficient frontier.

The second formulation starts from preferences. An investor with risk aversion \(\gamma > 0\) solves

\[\max_{\mathbf{w}}\ \mathbf{w}^\top\boldsymbol\mu - \frac{\gamma}{2}\,\mathbf{w}^\top\boldsymbol\Sigma\mathbf{w} \quad \text{s.t.} \quad \mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{1} = 1;\]
(4.2)

each unit of variance costs \(\gamma/2\) units of mean. For every \(\gamma\) the solution of (4.2) lies on the efficient branch. As \(\gamma\) runs over \((0, \infty)\), the solution traces the whole branch, with the GMV as the limit for \(\gamma \to \infty\). The explicit solution, (4.13), uses the scalars \(A\), \(B\) and \(C\) and is given with the closed-form frontier.

The problem does not restrict the sign of the weights. A negative weight \(w_i < 0\) is a short sale: the investor borrows asset \(i\), sells it and uses the proceeds to buy other assets. The closed-form results of this section assume that short sales are allowed and cost nothing; the constraint \(w_i \ge 0\) appears in the last part of the section.

In brief

  • The question of this section: which weights do we choose if \(\boldsymbol\mu\) and \(\boldsymbol\Sigma\) are known?
  • Markowitz (1952): investors care about the mean and the variance of portfolio returns
  • Minimum-variance frontier: minimum variance for every target mean \(m\)
    • its upper branch, \(m \ge \mu_{\text{GMV}}\) (the mean of the GMV portfolio), is the efficient frontier
    \[ \min_{\mathbf{w}}\ \mathbf{w}^\top\boldsymbol\Sigma\mathbf{w} \quad \text{s.t.} \quad \mathbf{w}^\top\boldsymbol\mu = m,\ \ \mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{1} = 1 \]
    • constraints: the target mean \(m\) and full investment (weights sum to 1)
  • Utility form, risk aversion \(\gamma > 0\): \(\max_{\mathbf{w}}\ \mathbf{w}^\top\boldsymbol\mu - \frac{\gamma}{2}\mathbf{w}^\top\boldsymbol\Sigma\mathbf{w}\) s.t. \(\mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{1} = 1\)

Two Assets: How Correlation Diversifies

Slide 8 in the PDF

The two-asset case shows the mechanism of diversification without matrix algebra. Let \(w\) be the weight of asset 1 and \(1-w\) the weight of asset 2, with volatilities \(\sigma_1\) and \(\sigma_2\) and correlation \(\rho\). The portfolio variance is

\[\sigma_p^2(w) = w^2\sigma_1^2 + (1-w)^2\sigma_2^2 + 2w(1-w)\rho\sigma_1\sigma_2,\]
(4.3)

and the portfolio mean is linear in \(w\). The correlation enters only through the cross term. The lower \(\rho\), the lower the variance of every portfolio with \(0 < w < 1\).

The first-order condition \(\mathrm{d}\sigma_p^2/\mathrm{d}w = 0\) reads

\[w\,(\sigma_1^2 + \sigma_2^2 - 2\rho\sigma_1\sigma_2) = \sigma_2^2 - \rho\sigma_1\sigma_2,\]

which gives the minimum-variance weight

\[w^* = \frac{\sigma_2^2 - \rho\sigma_1\sigma_2}{\sigma_1^2 + \sigma_2^2 - 2\rho\sigma_1\sigma_2}.\]
(4.4)

The denominator of (4.4) is the variance of the difference \(R_1 - R_2\), positive outside the degenerate case \(\rho = 1\), \(\sigma_1 = \sigma_2\), so \(w^*\) is a minimum. The numerator shows that \(w^*\) is negative when \(\rho > \sigma_2/\sigma_1\). If the more volatile asset is sufficiently correlated with the other one, minimum variance requires selling it short.

Two limiting cases of (4.3) fix the intuition. For \(\rho = 1\), \(\sigma_p = |w\sigma_1 + (1-w)\sigma_2|\): when \(0 \le w \le 1\), the portfolio volatility is the weighted average of the volatilities and diversification does not reduce risk. For \(\rho = -1\), \(\sigma_p = |w\sigma_1 - (1-w)\sigma_2|\), which vanishes at \(w = \sigma_2/(\sigma_1 + \sigma_2)\): two perfectly negatively correlated risky assets form a riskless portfolio.

    The Global Minimum-Variance (GMV) Portfolio

    Slide 9 in the PDF

    The global minimum-variance (GMV) portfolio is the leftmost point of the frontier. Among all fully invested portfolios it has the smallest variance, whatever its mean. The problem drops the constraint on the mean and keeps only the budget:

    \[\min_{\mathbf{w}}\ \mathbf{w}^\top\boldsymbol\Sigma\mathbf{w} \quad \text{s.t.} \quad \mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{1} = 1.\]
    (4.5)

    Let \(\lambda\) be the Lagrange multiplier of the budget constraint in (4.5). The Lagrangian is \(\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{w}, \lambda) = \mathbf{w}^\top\boldsymbol\Sigma\mathbf{w} - \lambda(\mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{1} - 1)\), and its gradient with respect to \(\mathbf{w}\) is \(2\boldsymbol\Sigma\mathbf{w} - \lambda\mathbf{1}\). Setting the gradient to zero gives \(\mathbf{w} = \tfrac{\lambda}{2}\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf{1}\); the inverse exists because \(\boldsymbol\Sigma\) is positive definite. Premultiplying by \(\mathbf{1}^\top\) and using the budget gives

    \[1 = \mathbf{1}^\top\mathbf{w} = \tfrac{\lambda}{2}\,\mathbf{1}^\top\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf{1} = \tfrac{\lambda}{2}\,A, \qquad A = \mathbf{1}^\top\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf{1}.\]

    The scalar \(A\) is strictly positive, because \(\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}\) is positive definite too. Hence

    \[\mathbf{w}_{\text{GMV}} = \frac{\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf{1}}{A}, \qquad \sigma^2_{\text{GMV}} = \frac{\mathbf{1}^\top\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}\boldsymbol\Sigma\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf{1}}{A^2} = \frac{1}{A}.\]
    (4.6)

    The objective is convex and the constraint is linear, so the first-order condition is also sufficient: the point found is the unique minimum. The same point is the vertex of the closed-form frontier of Merton (1972), (4.11) below.

    By (4.6), the weight of asset \(i\) is proportional to the sum of the entries in row \(i\) of \(\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}\). Nothing in the formula fixes the sign of this sum, so the GMV can contain short positions, as in the two-asset example for \(\rho > \sigma_2/\sigma_1\). The mean of the portfolio is \(\mu_{\text{GMV}} = \mathbf{w}_{\text{GMV}}^\top\boldsymbol\mu = B/A\), with \(B = \mathbf{1}^\top\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}\boldsymbol\mu\); the scalar \(B\) reappears with the tangency portfolio.

    The solution (4.6) does not contain \(\boldsymbol\mu\). Means are estimated far less precisely than covariances (Merton, 1980); the argument is given in the next section. This is why the GMV reappears in the chapter as GMV-LO (long-only) and as GMV-LW, with the covariance shrinkage of Ledoit & Wolf (2004b).

    In brief

    • The problem: \(\min_{\mathbf{w}} \mathbf{w}^\top\boldsymbol\Sigma\mathbf{w}\) s.t. \(\mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{1} = 1\), \(\boldsymbol\Sigma\) positive definite
    • Lagrangian \(\mathcal{L} = \mathbf{w}^\top\boldsymbol\Sigma\mathbf{w} - \lambda(\mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{1} - 1)\)
      • \(\lambda\): the Lagrange multiplier of the budget constraint
    • First-order condition \(2\boldsymbol\Sigma\mathbf{w} = \lambda\mathbf{1}\), so \(\mathbf{w} = \tfrac{\lambda}{2}\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf{1}\)
    • The budget gives \(\tfrac{\lambda}{2} = 1/A\), with \(A = \mathbf{1}^\top\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf{1}\)
    • Result: \(\mathbf{w}_{\text{GMV}} = \boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf{1}/A\), variance \(\sigma^2_{\text{GMV}} = 1/A\)
    • Needs only \(\boldsymbol\Sigma\), not the means

    The Tangency Portfolio

    Slide 10 in the PDF

    The tangency portfolio is the fully invested portfolio with the highest Sharpe ratio (Sharpe, 1966). Here \(\boldsymbol\mu\) must be the vector of expected returns in excess of the riskless rate, because the Sharpe ratio measures the mean excess return per unit of volatility. The problem is

    \[\max_{\mathbf{w}}\ S(\mathbf{w}) = \frac{\mathbf{w}^\top\boldsymbol\mu}{\sqrt{\mathbf{w}^\top\boldsymbol\Sigma\mathbf{w}}} \quad \text{s.t.} \quad \mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{1} = 1.\]
    (4.7)

    The function \(S\) does not depend on the scale of \(\mathbf{w}\), only on its direction and sign: for every \(c \neq 0\), \(S(c\,\mathbf{w}) = \operatorname{sign}(c)\,S(\mathbf{w})\). The budget constraint in (4.7) therefore fixes only the scale, and the problem reduces to the choice of a direction. The gradient of \(S\) is

    \[\nabla S(\mathbf{w}) = \frac{\boldsymbol\mu}{(\mathbf{w}^\top\boldsymbol\Sigma\mathbf{w})^{1/2}} - \frac{\mathbf{w}^\top\boldsymbol\mu}{(\mathbf{w}^\top\boldsymbol\Sigma\mathbf{w})^{3/2}}\,\boldsymbol\Sigma\mathbf{w},\]

    and setting it to zero gives the condition \(\boldsymbol\mu = \frac{\mathbf{w}^\top\boldsymbol\mu}{\mathbf{w}^\top\boldsymbol\Sigma\mathbf{w}}\,\boldsymbol\Sigma\mathbf{w}\). The factor in front of \(\boldsymbol\Sigma\mathbf{w}\) is a scalar, so \(\mathbf{w} \propto \boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}\boldsymbol\mu\). Dividing by the sum of the components gives

    \[\mathbf{w}_{\text{MV}} = \frac{\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}\boldsymbol\mu}{B}, \qquad B = \mathbf{1}^\top\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}\boldsymbol\mu.\]
    (4.8)

    The maximum value follows from the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality. Let \(\boldsymbol\Sigma^{1/2}\) be the symmetric square root of \(\boldsymbol\Sigma\). From \(\mathbf{w}^\top\boldsymbol\mu = (\boldsymbol\Sigma^{1/2}\mathbf{w})^\top(\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1/2}\boldsymbol\mu)\),

    \[|\mathbf{w}^\top\boldsymbol\mu| \le \sqrt{\mathbf{w}^\top\boldsymbol\Sigma\mathbf{w}}\;\sqrt{\boldsymbol\mu^\top\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}\boldsymbol\mu},\]
    (4.9)

    so \(|S(\mathbf{w})| \le \theta\), with \(\theta^2 = \boldsymbol\mu^\top\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}\boldsymbol\mu\). Equality holds only for \(\mathbf{w}\) proportional to \(\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}\boldsymbol\mu\): the direction \(\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}\boldsymbol\mu\) attains \(+\theta\) and the opposite direction attains \(-\theta\). Unlike the GMV, the result depends on both \(\boldsymbol\mu\) and \(\boldsymbol\Sigma\).

    Normalising to the budget in (4.8) can reverse the direction. The mean of \(\mathbf{w}_{\text{MV}}\) is \(\theta^2/B\) and its volatility is \(\theta/|B|\), so its Sharpe ratio is \(\operatorname{sign}(B)\,\theta\). If \(B > 0\), \(\mathbf{w}_{\text{MV}}\) is the maximum-Sharpe portfolio. If \(B < 0\), dividing by \(B\) flips the sign, and \(\mathbf{w}_{\text{MV}}\) is the fully invested portfolio with the lowest Sharpe ratio, \(-\theta\). If \(B = 0\), the optimal direction has weights summing to zero and cannot be scaled so that the weights sum to 1.

    The sign of \(B\) has a direct interpretation. From the previous part, \(B = A\,\mu_{\text{GMV}}\) with \(A > 0\), so \(B > 0\) exactly when the GMV portfolio has a positive expected excess return. A calculation that applies \(\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}\boldsymbol\mu/B\) without checking the sign of \(B\) can return the minimum-Sharpe portfolio. With estimated parameters, the denominator \(\mathbf{1}^\top\hat{\boldsymbol\Sigma}^{-1}\hat{\boldsymbol\mu}\) can be close to zero, and the estimated tangency weights then have very heavy tails (appendix on the distribution of the weights).

    Separation and the Frontier in Closed Form

    Slide 11 in the PDF

    Tobin (1958) adds a riskless asset paying \(R_f\). In the (volatility, excess mean) plane this asset sits at the origin. A portfolio that invests the fraction \(\alpha \ge 0\) in the risky portfolio \(\mathbf{w}\) and the rest in the riskless asset has excess mean \(\alpha\,\mathbf{w}^\top\boldsymbol\mu\) and volatility \(\alpha\sqrt{\mathbf{w}^\top\boldsymbol\Sigma\mathbf{w}}\). The mixes therefore lie on a ray from the origin whose slope is the Sharpe ratio of \(\mathbf{w}\). The steepest ray passes through the tangency portfolio and has slope \(\theta\) from (4.9); \(\alpha > 1\) means borrowing at the rate \(R_f\).

    We now assume \(B > 0\), so the fully invested tangency portfolio has Sharpe ratio \(+\theta\). Let \(m_T\) and \(\sigma_T\) be its excess mean and volatility. An investor with risk aversion \(\gamma\) chooses \(\alpha\) to maximise \(\alpha m_T - \frac{\gamma}{2}\alpha^2\sigma_T^2\), which gives

    \[\alpha^* = \frac{m_T}{\gamma\,\sigma_T^2}.\]
    (4.10)

    Because \(m_T > 0\), the solution (4.10) satisfies \(\alpha \ge 0\); for an arbitrary risky fund subject to this constraint, the solution is \(\alpha^* = \max\{0,\ m_T/(\gamma\sigma_T^2)\}\). By (4.10), risk aversion changes only the share invested in the risky part, not its composition: this is two-fund separation. If all investors use the same \(\boldsymbol\mu\) and \(\boldsymbol\Sigma\), they all hold the same risky portfolio. This is the bridge to the Capital Asset Pricing Model (CAPM), covered in Chapter 3.

    Without a riskless asset, the frontier has a closed form (Merton, 1972). The problem with two constraints has the Lagrangian

    \[\mathcal{L} = \tfrac12\mathbf{w}^\top\boldsymbol\Sigma\mathbf{w} - \lambda_1(\mathbf{w}^\top\boldsymbol\mu - m) - \lambda_2(\mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{1} - 1),\]

    and the first-order condition gives \(\mathbf{w} = \lambda_1\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}\boldsymbol\mu + \lambda_2\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf{1}\). With \(A\) and \(B\) from the previous parts and \(C = \boldsymbol\mu^\top\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}\boldsymbol\mu = \theta^2\), the two constraints become a \(2\times 2\) linear system:

    \[\lambda_1 C + \lambda_2 B = m, \qquad \lambda_1 B + \lambda_2 A = 1.\]

    Its solution is \(\lambda_1 = (Am - B)/D\) and \(\lambda_2 = (C - Bm)/D\), with \(D = AC - B^2\). The minimum variance follows from \(\mathbf{w}^\top\boldsymbol\Sigma\mathbf{w} = \mathbf{w}^\top(\lambda_1\boldsymbol\mu + \lambda_2\mathbf{1}) = \lambda_1 m + \lambda_2\):

    \[\sigma^2(m) = \frac{Am^2 - 2Bm + C}{D} = \frac{1}{A} + \frac{A}{D}\Big(m - \frac{B}{A}\Big)^2.\]
    (4.11)

    All the steps are in the Appendix.

    The condition \(D > 0\) is another Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, this time for the inner product defined by \(\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}\): \(B^2 = (\mathbf{1}^\top\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}\boldsymbol\mu)^2 \le (\mathbf{1}^\top\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf{1})(\boldsymbol\mu^\top\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}\boldsymbol\mu) = AC\). Equality holds only if \(\boldsymbol\mu\) is proportional to \(\mathbf{1}\). In that case all fully invested portfolios have the same mean, and the frontier collapses to the GMV portfolio.

    The second form in (4.11) shows the geometry. In the \((\sigma^2, m)\) plane, \(\sigma^2(m)\) is a parabola with vertex at \((1/A,\ B/A)\), that is, at the GMV portfolio. In the \((\sigma, m)\) plane of the charts, the same relation is a hyperbola. The curve is symmetric about \(m = B/A\), which justifies taking the upper branch as the efficient frontier.

    The frontier weights combine two portfolios already computed. Since \(\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf{1} = A\,\mathbf{w}_{\text{GMV}}\) and \(\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}\boldsymbol\mu = B\,\mathbf{w}_{\text{MV}}\), for \(B \neq 0\)

    \[\mathbf{w}(m) = \lambda_1 B\,\mathbf{w}_{\text{MV}} + \lambda_2 A\,\mathbf{w}_{\text{GMV}}, \qquad \lambda_1 B + \lambda_2 A = 1.\]
    (4.12)

    The coefficients in (4.12) sum to 1, but one of them can be negative. More generally, any two distinct frontier portfolios generate the whole frontier (Merton, 1972).

    The same algebra completes the utility formulation (4.2) from the first part of the section. Its first-order condition gives

    \[\mathbf{w}_\gamma = \mathbf{w}_{\text{GMV}} + \frac{1}{\gamma}\Big(\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}\boldsymbol\mu - \frac{B}{A}\,\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf{1}\Big), \qquad \mathbf{w}_\gamma^\top\boldsymbol\mu = \frac{B}{A} + \frac{D}{\gamma A}.\]
    (4.13)

    The portfolio \(\mathbf{w}_\gamma\) in (4.13) has the form \(\lambda_1\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}\boldsymbol\mu + \lambda_2\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf{1}\), so it lies on the frontier. Its mean falls from \(+\infty\) towards \(B/A\) as \(\gamma\) rises from 0 towards \(\infty\), so the solutions trace the efficient branch.

      Constraints

      Slide 13 in the PDF

      The formulas so far allow any weight. The basic constraint is long-only (LO): \(w_i \ge 0\) for every asset. Other linear constraints limit concentration: position caps, for example \(w_i \le 0.2\), and group caps, \(\sum_{i\in G} w_i \le c_G\), where \(G\) is a group of assets (a sector or an asset class) and \(c_G\) its cap.

      A leverage limit concerns the gross exposure \(\sum_i |w_i|\). Because the weights sum to 1,

      \[\sum_i |w_i| = 1 + 2\sum_i \max(-w_i, 0),\]
      (4.14)

      so the gross exposure exceeds 1 by twice the sum of the short positions. By (4.14), the constraint \(\sum_i |w_i| \le L\) with \(L = 1\) is therefore equivalent to ruling out short sales.

      With inequalities the closed forms no longer hold. The optimality conditions add a multiplier \(\lambda_i \ge 0\) for each constraint \(w_i \ge 0\), equal to zero when the constraint is not active (\(w_i > 0\)). Which constraints are active is not known in advance; it follows from the solution. For the GMV, the long-only problem (GMV-LO) is

      \[\min_{\mathbf{w}}\ \mathbf{w}^\top\boldsymbol\Sigma\mathbf{w} \quad \text{s.t.} \quad \mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{1} = 1,\ \ w_i \ge 0,\ i = 1, \dots, N.\]
      (4.15)

      The objective remains quadratic and convex, and the constraints are linear, as for the long-only frontier. The problem is one of quadratic programming, solved numerically.

      Constraints cannot improve the in-sample optimum: on the US sectors, the Sharpe ratio of the tangency portfolio falls from 0.66 to 0.61. Out of sample the effect can go the other way. Frost & Savarino (1988) showed that constraining the weights improved out-of-sample performance. Jagannathan & Ma (2003) explained the mechanism: the solution of (4.15) is the GMV portfolio (4.6) of a modified covariance matrix. An active constraint \(w_i \ge 0\) is equivalent to shrinking the estimated covariances of asset \(i\), that is, to shrinkage of the covariance matrix. The proof and a two-asset example are in the shrinkage section (Constraints Are Shrinkage Too).

        • sector or asset-class limits: \(\sum_{i\in G} w_i \le c_G\)
        • \(G\) a group of assets, \(c_G\) its cap
      • Leverage limit: \(\sum_i |w_i| \le L\)
        • \(\sum_i |w_i|\): gross exposure
        • \(L = 1\) means no short sales

      Section summary

      With known parameters, mean–variance allocation has explicit solutions. The GMV portfolio (4.6) uses only the covariances. The tangency portfolio (4.8) has the maximum Sharpe ratio \(\theta = \sqrt{\boldsymbol\mu^\top\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}\boldsymbol\mu}\) only if \(B > 0\). The frontier (4.11) is a hyperbola determined by \(A\), \(B\) and \(C\), and any two of its distinct portfolios generate it (Merton, 1972). With a riskless asset, all investors combine the same tangency portfolio with the riskless asset (Tobin, 1958). Constraints on the weights remove the closed forms and require quadratic programming.

      On the US sectors, even with parameters estimated on the full sample, the tangency portfolio raises the Sharpe ratio only from 0.54 (1/N) to 0.66. All the formulas of this section assume that \(\boldsymbol\mu\) and \(\boldsymbol\Sigma\) are known. The next section replaces them with estimates and shows how estimation errors pass into the weights and the Sharpe ratio.

      Estimation Risk

      The previous section assumed that \(\boldsymbol\mu\) and \(\boldsymbol\Sigma\) are known. Here they are replaced by sample (plug-in) estimates, and the question is how much the optimal portfolio loses through this substitution. The answer has four parts. The first shows that means are estimated far less precisely than variances, whatever the sampling frequency. The second shows by simulation that estimated frontiers are systematically too optimistic and that the portfolios chosen on them have poor true performance. The third quantifies the bias of the estimated maximum Sharpe ratio. The fourth shows the instability of the weights on real data and the length of the estimation window that the mean–variance rule would need to beat 1/N.

      Throughout the section, a simulation means the following experiment: the "true" parameters are the moments of the US sectors over 1999–2026; samples of returns are drawn from the Normal distribution with these parameters; the weights are computed from the moments of each sample; and the weights are evaluated with the true parameters. The gap between the estimated and the true Sharpe ratio of the same portfolio measures the effect of estimation error.

      Means Are Hard to Estimate: Merton's Argument

      Slide 14 in the PDF

      Merton (1980) compares the precision with which the mean and the variance of returns can be estimated. Consider a sample of \(Y\) years, observed \(h\) times a year, with i.i.d. returns from the Normal distribution (a diffusion), with annual mean \(\mu\) and annual variance \(\sigma^2\). Each of the \(hY\) returns has mean \(\mu/h\) and variance \(\sigma^2/h\). The natural estimator of the annual mean is the sum of all returns divided by \(Y\), and its variance is \(hY(\sigma^2/h)/Y^2\). For the variance, the sample estimator has, under the Normal distribution, approximate variance \(2\sigma^4/(hY)\):

      \[\operatorname{Var}(\hat\mu) = \frac{\sigma^2}{Y}, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(\hat\sigma^2) \approx \frac{2\sigma^4}{hY}.\]
      (4.16)

      The argument uses additive increments of a constant-parameter diffusion. The first relation in (4.16) does not depend on \(h\): in such a model, increasing the observation frequency over a fixed calendar span does not reduce the variance of the mean estimator, whose precision grows only with the span of the sample, in years. The second relation tends to zero as \(h \to \infty\): in the idealised model, the variance can be estimated as precisely as desired over a fixed interval if the observations are frequent enough. For simple returns, the realised annual return compounds as \(\prod_t(1 + R_t) - 1\); it is not the sum of the monthly returns. Without Normality, \(\operatorname{Var}(\hat\sigma^2) \approx (\kappa - 1)\sigma^4/(hY)\), where \(\kappa\) is the kurtosis of the returns; for the Normal distribution, \(\kappa = 3\). The general formula requires a finite fourth moment, an assumption questioned in Chapter 1.

      For SPY, with \(Y = 24\) years and \(\sigma = 14.7\%\), the standard error of the annual mean is \(0.147/\sqrt{24}\), that is 3.0%. The 95% confidence interval for the estimated mean of 11.7% is [5.8%, 17.6%]: the data are compatible with means that differ by a factor of three. By (4.16), a standard error of 1% would require \((0.147/0.01)^2\), about 217 years of data.

      Chopra & Ziemba (1993) measured the consequence for portfolio choice: in the configurations the authors analyse, errors in the means cost about ten times more than errors in the variances. It follows that rules which do not use estimated means, such as GMV and risk parity, are more robust to estimation error than the tangency portfolio.

        Error Maximisation: Estimated versus Realised Frontiers (2/2)

        Slide 16 in the PDF

        Michaud (1989) called mean–variance optimisers estimation-error maximisers. The optimiser looks for the combination with the highest estimated Sharpe ratio. Assets whose means are overestimated or whose covariances are underestimated look more attractive than they are, and the optimiser selects precisely those. The estimated frontier therefore reflects, besides the true parameters, the most favourable estimation errors of the sample. Best & Grauer (1991) showed the same sensitivity from the other side: a small change in a single estimated mean moves many weights of the optimal portfolio.

        The lecture's simulation uses 2000 tangency portfolios estimated from samples of 60 months. The median of \(\hat\theta\), the absolute value of the in-sample Sharpe ratio, is 1.56 a year. Evaluated with the true parameters, the same portfolios have a median signed Sharpe ratio of 0.30, although the true optimum is 0.66. Of these portfolios, 96% have a true Sharpe ratio below that of 1/N, 0.54, and 7.5% have a negative true Sharpe ratio.

        Part of these results comes from the sign problem discussed with the tangency portfolio. In 11% of the samples, \(\hat B = \mathbf{1}^\top\hat{\boldsymbol\Sigma}^{-1}\hat{\boldsymbol\mu} < 0\), where \(\hat{\boldsymbol\mu}\) and \(\hat{\boldsymbol\Sigma}\) are the sample estimates. In these samples, the tangency formula (4.8) applied to the estimates returns the portfolio with the minimum estimated Sharpe ratio. The signed in-sample Sharpe ratio of the budget-normalised portfolio is therefore \(\operatorname{sign}(\hat B)\,\hat\theta\), and when \(\hat B < 0\), \(\hat\theta\) is not the Sharpe ratio of the fully invested portfolio chosen.

        The Plug-in Sharpe Ratio Is Biased (1/2)

        Slide 17 in the PDF

        The simulation shows that the estimated maximum Sharpe ratio is far too high. Kan & Zhou (2007) give the exact expression of this bias. Excess returns are i.i.d. \(N(\boldsymbol\mu, \boldsymbol\Sigma)\), observed over \(T\) months, for \(N\) assets. Let \(\hat{\boldsymbol\mu}\) and \(\hat{\boldsymbol\Sigma}\) be the maximum-likelihood estimators, and

        \[\hat\theta^2 = \hat{\boldsymbol\mu}^\top\hat{\boldsymbol\Sigma}^{-1}\hat{\boldsymbol\mu}\]

        the in-sample squared maximum Sharpe ratio, the plug-in estimator of \(\theta^2 = \boldsymbol\mu^\top\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}\boldsymbol\mu\) from (4.9).

        Under these assumptions, \(\hat{\boldsymbol\mu} \sim N(\boldsymbol\mu, \boldsymbol\Sigma/T)\), and \(T\hat{\boldsymbol\Sigma}\) has a Wishart distribution with \(T-1\) degrees of freedom, the matrix analogue of the \(\chi^2\) distribution; the two estimators are independent. For \(T > N + 2\), the inverse Wishart moment is \(\mathbb{E}[\hat{\boldsymbol\Sigma}^{-1}] = \frac{T}{T-N-2}\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}\) (appendix on Wishart moments). Conditioning on \(\hat{\boldsymbol\mu}\) and using \(\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{x}^\top\mathbf{K}\mathbf{x}] = \operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{K}\operatorname{Var}(\mathbf{x})) + \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{x}]^\top\mathbf{K}\,\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{x}]\) for the random vector \(\mathbf{x} = \hat{\boldsymbol\mu}\) and the fixed matrix \(\mathbf{K} = \boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}\), where \(\operatorname{tr}\) is the trace, gives

        \[\mathbb{E}[\hat\theta^2] = \frac{T}{T-N-2}\Big(\theta^2 + \frac{N}{T}\Big) = \frac{T\theta^2 + N}{T - N - 2}, \qquad T > N + 2.\]
        (4.17)

        Equations (4.17) and (4.18) use the covariance estimator with divisor \(T\). With divisor \(T-1\), used in the simulation, the expectation of \(\hat\theta^2\) becomes \(\frac{T-1}{T-N-2}\big(\theta^2 + \frac{N}{T}\big)\). Formula (4.17) contains two sources of upward bias. The term \(N/T\) comes from the noise in the estimated means: even with \(\theta = 0\), the \(N\) estimated means are not all zero, and the optimiser exploits them. The factor \(T/(T-N-2) > 1\) comes from inverting the estimated covariance matrix. Both grow with the ratio \(N/T\).

        On the US sectors, with \(N = 9\), \(T = 60\) and \(\theta = 0.66\) a year in the simulation, the factor \(T/(T-N-2)\) is 1.22, and (4.17) gives \(\sqrt{12\,\mathbb{E}[\hat\theta^2]} = 1.65\) a year. This figure is a root mean square, not a median. The simulated median of the previous part, 1.56, is lower because the distribution of \(\hat\theta\) is right-skewed.

        In brief

        • i.i.d. \(N(\boldsymbol\mu, \boldsymbol\Sigma)\) returns, \(T\) months, \(N\) assets (Kan & Zhou (2007))

        The Plug-in Sharpe Ratio Is Biased (2/2)

        Slide 18 in the PDF

        Solving (4.17) for \(\theta^2\) gives the unbiased estimator

        \[\hat\theta^2_u = \frac{(T-N-2)\,\hat\theta^2 - N}{T}.\]
        (4.18)

        The estimator (4.18) can be negative, although \(\theta^2 \ge 0\); \(\sqrt{\hat\theta^2_u}\) is then not even real. Truncating it at zero gives a nonnegative but upward-biased estimator. The real-valued estimator \(\sqrt{\max(0,\ \hat\theta^2_u)}\) of \(\theta\) is nonnegative but generally biased.

        On the US sectors, over the full sample (\(T = 331\)) and under i.i.d. Normality, the bias correction of \(\hat\theta^2\) lowers the equivalent point estimate of the Sharpe ratio from 0.66, the plug-in value, to about 0.31 a year. The correction concerns \(\theta^2\), not a particular portfolio: it does not show which part of the observed Sharpe ratio of the tangency portfolio is noise.

        The sample tangency weights can also be obtained from a regression (Britten-Jones, 1999). Let \(\mathbf{r}_t\) be the vector of excess returns in month \(t\) and \(u_t\) the regression error. Regressing the constant 1 on the excess returns, without an intercept, by ordinary least squares (OLS) gives the coefficients \(\mathbf{b}\), and

        \[1 = \mathbf{b}^\top\mathbf{r}_t + u_t, \qquad \mathbf{w} = \frac{\mathbf{b}}{\mathbf{1}^\top\mathbf{b}}.\]
        (4.19)

        The weights (4.19) are the sample tangency weights, because the OLS coefficients are proportional to \(\hat{\boldsymbol\Sigma}^{-1}\hat{\boldsymbol\mu}\). The advantage of this formulation is that inference on the weights becomes inference on regression coefficients: the \(t\)-test of \(b_i = 0\) checks whether asset \(i\) has a zero weight in the tangency portfolio. The derivation and the tests on the sectors are in the appendix on the Britten-Jones regression.

        In brief

        • Unbiased estimator of \(\theta^2\): \(\hat\theta^2_u = \big[(T-N-2)\,\hat\theta^2 - N\big]/T\)

        • Sectors, full sample (\(T = 331\)), under i.i.d. Normality: the bias correction of \(\hat\theta^2\) lowers the equivalent point estimate of the Sharpe ratio from \(0.66\) (plug-in) to about \(0.31\) a year

        • Tangency weights: \(\mathbf{w} = \mathbf{b}/\mathbf{1}^\top\mathbf{b}\) (Britten-Jones (1999))

            Covariance Shrinkage

            The previous section showed that the mean–variance formulas, applied to estimated parameters, pass estimation errors on to the weights. This section deals with the second input of the formulas, the covariance matrix. The GMV portfolio (4.6) does not use the means, but it does use the inverse \(\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}\), and the inverse amplifies precisely the errors that the sample covariance matrix makes systematically: its eigenvalues are more dispersed than the true ones.

            The first part describes the problem through the spectrum of the sample matrix and the Marchenko–Pastur noise band. The second introduces the shrinkage estimator of Ledoit and Wolf, which pulls the sample matrix towards a structured target, and applies it to the US sectors. The last part shows that the long-only constraint in (4.15), introduced in the previous section as a constraint on the weights, also acts as shrinkage of the covariances (Jagannathan & Ma, 2003).

            Why the Sample Covariance Matrix Fails

            Slide 23 in the PDF

            Let \(\mathbf{S}\) be the sample covariance matrix of \(N\) assets, computed from \(T\) observations. Being symmetric and positive semidefinite, it has the spectral decomposition \(\mathbf{S} = \sum_{k=1}^{N} \lambda_k \mathbf{u}_k\mathbf{u}_k^\top\), with eigenvalues \(\lambda_1 \ge \dots \ge \lambda_N \ge 0\) and orthonormal eigenvectors \(\mathbf{u}_k\). If \(\lambda_N > 0\), the inverse is

            \[\mathbf{S}^{-1} = \sum_{k=1}^{N} \frac{1}{\lambda_k}\,\mathbf{u}_k\mathbf{u}_k^\top .\]
            (4.21)

            By (4.21), each direction \(\mathbf{u}_k\) enters the inverse with weight \(1/\lambda_k\). The directions with the smallest estimated variance receive the largest weight, and an optimiser that uses \(\mathbf{S}^{-1}\), for instance in \(\mathbf{w}_{\text{GMV}} \propto \mathbf{S}^{-1}\mathbf{1}\), puts large weights on the combinations of assets that look almost riskless in the sample. How strongly the inverse amplifies errors is measured by the condition number, the ratio \(\lambda_1/\lambda_N\) of the largest to the smallest eigenvalue.

            The problem would be minor if the sample eigenvalues were good estimators of the true ones. They are, however, too dispersed: the largest sample eigenvalues tend to be too large and the smallest too small. In the extreme case, the demeaned matrix has rank at most \(T-1\), because the \(T\) deviations from the mean sum to zero. For \(T \le N\), \(\mathbf{S}\) is singular: at least one eigenvalue is zero and (4.21) does not exist.

            The quantitative benchmark for this dispersion comes from Marčenko & Pastur (1967). If returns are independent and identically distributed with \(\boldsymbol\Sigma = \sigma^2\mathbf{I}\) (\(\mathbf{I}\) being the identity matrix), all true eigenvalues equal \(\sigma^2\). As \(N\) and \(T\) grow together, with \(N/T \to c \in (0, 1)\), the sample eigenvalues do not concentrate at \(\sigma^2\) but fill the interval

            \[\big[\lambda_-, \lambda_+\big] = \big[\sigma^2(1-\sqrt{c})^2,\ \sigma^2(1+\sqrt{c})^2\big],\]
            (4.22)

            where \(\lambda_-\) and \(\lambda_+\) are the edges of the support of the limiting eigenvalue distribution under the independent-noise model. Finite-sample extreme eigenvalues fluctuate around these benchmarks. The width of the band depends only on the ratio \(c\): the fewer observations per asset, the wider it is.

            For 16 assets and a 60-month window, \(c = 16/60 = 0.27\). For a correlation matrix (\(\sigma^2 = 1\)), (4.22) gives the band \([0.23, 2.30]\): noise alone produces a ten-fold spread between the extreme eigenvalues, although all true eigenvalues are equal. Laloux et al. (1999) used this band to separate structure from noise: eigenvalues outside the band are candidates for structure, while those inside cannot be told from noise by this comparison alone.

            In brief

            • The question of this section: how do we estimate \(\boldsymbol\Sigma\) so that \(\hat{\boldsymbol\Sigma}^{-1}\) does not amplify noise?

              • a large value means \(\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}\) amplifies noise

              The Shrinkage Estimator

              Slide 26 in the PDF

              Shrinkage replaces the sample matrix \(\mathbf{S}\) by a convex combination of it with a structured target \(\mathbf{F}\):

              \[\hat{\boldsymbol\Sigma}_{\text{LW}} = \delta\,\mathbf{F} + (1-\delta)\,\mathbf{S}, \qquad 0 \le \delta \le 1.\]
              (4.23)

              The parameter \(\delta\) in (4.23) is the shrinkage intensity. The target has few free parameters: it is estimated with little error, but it is in general misspecified. The sample matrix has \(N(N+1)/2\) free parameters: it is unbiased, but has a large estimation error. The combination trades part of the estimation error for a bias with respect to the true matrix.

              Ledoit and Wolf proposed three targets: the covariance implied by the single-index model (Ledoit & Wolf, 2003), the scaled identity (Ledoit & Wolf, 2004a) and the constant-correlation matrix (Ledoit & Wolf, 2004b). The target used in this chapter is the last one. It keeps the sample variances and replaces every correlation by their average:

              \[f_{ii} = s_{ii}, \qquad f_{ij} = \bar r\,\sqrt{s_{ii}\,s_{jj}}, \quad i \ne j,\]
              (4.24)

              where \(s_{ij}\) are the elements of \(\mathbf{S}\) and \(\bar r\) is the average sample correlation. There are two reasons for choosing it for the US sectors: all sectors are US equities with similar correlations, and the target needs no choice of a market index.

              The intensity is chosen to minimise the expected squared distance, in the Frobenius norm, between \(\hat{\boldsymbol\Sigma}_{\text{LW}}\) and \(\boldsymbol\Sigma\). The logic is visible in a simplified case, with \(\mathbf{F}\) fixed and \(\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{S}] = \boldsymbol\Sigma\). Then \(\hat{\boldsymbol\Sigma}_{\text{LW}} - \boldsymbol\Sigma = (1-\delta)(\mathbf{S} - \boldsymbol\Sigma) + \delta(\mathbf{F} - \boldsymbol\Sigma)\), the cross term has mean zero, and

              \[\mathbb{E}\big\|\hat{\boldsymbol\Sigma}_{\text{LW}} - \boldsymbol\Sigma\big\|^2 = (1-\delta)^2\,\mathbb{E}\|\mathbf{S} - \boldsymbol\Sigma\|^2 + \delta^2\,\|\mathbf{F} - \boldsymbol\Sigma\|^2 ,\]

              which is minimised by \(\delta = \mathbb{E}\|\mathbf{S} - \boldsymbol\Sigma\|^2 / (\mathbb{E}\|\mathbf{S} - \boldsymbol\Sigma\|^2 + \|\mathbf{F} - \boldsymbol\Sigma\|^2)\): the weight of the target rises with the noise in \(\mathbf{S}\) and falls with the misspecification of the target. Ledoit & Wolf (2004b) treat the case in which the target is also estimated from the same data, and replace the unknown quantities by consistent estimators. The result is

              \[\delta^* = \max\Big\{0,\ \min\Big\{1,\ \frac{\hat\kappa}{T}\Big\}\Big\}, \qquad \hat\kappa = \frac{\hat\pi - \hat\rho}{\hat\gamma}.\]
              (4.25)

              In (4.25), \(\hat\pi\) is the sum of the asymptotic variances of the elements of \(\mathbf{S}\) and \(\hat\rho\) the sum of their asymptotic covariances with the elements of the target; the difference \(\hat\pi - \hat\rho\) is the estimated noise of \(\mathbf{S}\). The denominator \(\hat\gamma = \|\mathbf{F} - \mathbf{S}\|^2\) measures the observed distance between the target and the sample covariance matrix; it is unrelated to the risk aversion \(\gamma\) in (4.2). With fixed \(N\) and a persistently misspecified target, the intensity typically vanishes at order \(1/T\). This conclusion is not general when the dimension grows with the sample size or the target is correctly specified. Truncation to \([0, 1]\) keeps the combination convex. The full formulas are in the appendix.

                Constraints Are Shrinkage Too

                Slide 29 in the PDF

                Jagannathan & Ma (2003) showed that the long-only GMV portfolio (4.15) equals the unconstrained GMV portfolio of a modified covariance matrix. The long-only problem has the Lagrangian

                \[\mathcal{L} = \tfrac12\,\mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{S}\mathbf{w} - \lambda_0(\mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{1} - 1) - \boldsymbol\lambda^\top\mathbf{w},\]

                where \(\lambda_0\) is the multiplier of the budget constraint and \(\boldsymbol\lambda = (\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_N)^\top \ge \mathbf{0}\) is the vector of multipliers of the constraints \(w_i \ge 0\). The Karush–Kuhn–Tucker conditions require \(\mathbf{S}\mathbf{w} = \lambda_0\mathbf{1} + \boldsymbol\lambda\) and complementary slackness \(\lambda_i w_i = 0\) for every \(i\), hence \(\boldsymbol\lambda^\top\mathbf{w} = 0\): \(\lambda_i > 0\) only where the constraint binds, that is, where \(w_i = 0\).

                Define the modified matrix

                \[\tilde{\mathbf{S}} = \mathbf{S} - (\boldsymbol\lambda\mathbf{1}^\top + \mathbf{1}\boldsymbol\lambda^\top), \qquad \tilde s_{ij} = s_{ij} - \lambda_i - \lambda_j .\]
                (4.26)

                With the budget \(\mathbf{1}^\top\mathbf{w} = 1\) and complementary slackness, \(\tilde{\mathbf{S}}\mathbf{w} = \mathbf{S}\mathbf{w} - \boldsymbol\lambda(\mathbf{1}^\top\mathbf{w}) - \mathbf{1}(\boldsymbol\lambda^\top\mathbf{w}) = \lambda_0\mathbf{1} + \boldsymbol\lambda - \boldsymbol\lambda - \mathbf{0} = \lambda_0\mathbf{1}\). This is the first-order condition of the GMV problem (4.5) for the matrix \(\tilde{\mathbf{S}}\): the long-only solution satisfies the unconstrained GMV condition of \(\tilde{\mathbf{S}}\).

                By (4.26), each covariance is adjusted by \(\lambda_i + \lambda_j\) and each variance by \(2\lambda_i\): a binding constraint on asset \(i\) lowers all its covariances. The active constraints are determined jointly by solving the long-only problem; they cannot generally be identified by removing the negative unconstrained weights, because reoptimisation changes all weights at once. The adjustment lowers the estimated covariances of the assets it touches, like shrinkage; this regularisation can reduce out-of-sample risk without identifying the estimation error of each covariance.

                The consequence is that a "wrong" constraint, in the sense that an investor who knew \(\boldsymbol\Sigma\) would have no reason to impose it, can reduce out-of-sample risk. The effect is largest when the covariances are estimated from few observations, that is, exactly when the noise in \(\mathbf{S}\) is large.

                Constraints as Shrinkage: A Two-Asset Example

                Slide 30 in the PDF

                The mechanism of (4.26) can be followed on two assets with volatilities \(\sigma_1 = 25\%\) and \(\sigma_2 = 10\%\) and correlation \(\rho = 0.5\). The elements of the matrix are \(s_{11} = 0.0625\), \(s_{22} = 0.0100\) and \(s_{12} = \rho\sigma_1\sigma_2 = 0.0125\). Formula (4.4) gives the unconstrained GMV weight of the first asset:

                \[w_1^* = \frac{s_{22} - s_{12}}{s_{11} + s_{22} - 2s_{12}} = \frac{-0.0025}{0.0475} = -0.053 .\]

                The volatile asset is shorted because the correlation exceeds the ratio of the volatilities, \(\rho > \sigma_2/\sigma_1\): it co-moves strongly with the other asset, and the short position reduces the variance. The portfolio volatility is 9.93%.

                Under the long-only constraint, \(w_1 \ge 0\) binds and the solution is \(\mathbf{w} = (0, 1)\), with volatility 10%. The first-order condition gives \(\mathbf{S}\mathbf{w} = (s_{12}, s_{22}) = (0.0125, 0.0100) = \lambda_0\mathbf{1} + \boldsymbol\lambda\). Because \(w_2 > 0\), complementary slackness requires \(\lambda_2 = 0\), so \(\lambda_0 = 0.0100\) and \(\boldsymbol\lambda = (0.0025, 0)\).

                The modified matrix of (4.26) has \(\tilde s_{12} = 0.0125 - 0.0025 = 0.0100\), \(\tilde s_{11} = 0.0625 - 2(0.0025) = 0.0575\) and \(\tilde s_{22} = 0.0100\). The correlation falls from 0.5 to \(0.0100/\sqrt{0.0575\times0.0100} \approx 0.42\), and the variance of the first asset falls as well. For \(\tilde{\mathbf{S}}\), the numerator of the GMV formula is \(\tilde s_{22} - \tilde s_{12} = 0.0100 - 0.0100 = 0\), so \(w_1^* = 0\): the unconstrained GMV of the modified matrix is exactly the long-only solution.

                The long-only constraint corrects only the covariances of the assets it touches, by an amount set by the multipliers. Nonlinear shrinkage, presented in the appendix, keeps the sample eigenvectors and corrects each eigenvalue separately; on the chapter's data no estimator dominates, and the choice is made out of sample.

                Black–Litterman

                The section on estimation risk showed that the main problem of the tangency portfolio (4.8) is the estimated means: they are imprecise, and the optimiser amplifies them. Covariance shrinkage does not solve this problem. Black & Litterman (1992) apply the idea of shrinkage to the means: the means used in the optimisation are pulled towards those implied by a benchmark portfolio, and they depart from them only through the investor's explicit views, weighted by the confidence in them.

                From Covariance Shrinkage to Mean Shrinkage

                Slide 31 in the PDF

                The question of this section is which means to use if the sample means are too imprecise. The answer of Black & Litterman (1992) starts from reverse optimisation. A mean–variance investor with risk aversion \(\delta_{\text{RA}}\) and access to cash, hence without the budget constraint of (4.2), maximises \(\mathbf{w}^\top\boldsymbol\mu - \frac{\delta_{\text{RA}}}{2}\mathbf{w}^\top\boldsymbol\Sigma\mathbf{w}\). The first-order condition \(\boldsymbol\mu = \delta_{\text{RA}}\boldsymbol\Sigma\mathbf{w}\) gives the weights in risky assets

                \[\mathbf{w}^* = (\delta_{\text{RA}}\boldsymbol\Sigma)^{-1}\boldsymbol\mu .\]
                (4.27)

                Reverse optimisation reads (4.27) from right to left. Instead of asking "which weights are optimal for the given means?", it asks "for which means is a given portfolio optimal?". If the benchmark portfolio \(\mathbf{w}_b\) is to be optimal, the means are

                \[\boldsymbol\pi = \delta_{\text{RA}}\,\boldsymbol\Sigma\,\mathbf{w}_b .\]
                (4.28)

                The vector \(\boldsymbol\pi\) in (4.28) contains the implied excess returns, also called equilibrium returns. Component \(i\) is proportional to the covariance of asset \(i\) with the benchmark portfolio, \((\boldsymbol\Sigma\mathbf{w}_b)_i\). With market weights as the benchmark, \(\boldsymbol\pi\) are the returns implied by the CAPM, covered in Chapter 3. Computing \(\boldsymbol\pi\) requires no estimated mean, only \(\boldsymbol\Sigma\), which is estimated much more precisely.

                For the US sectors, market capitalisations of the sectors are not available, so the benchmark portfolio is 1/N and the risk aversion is \(\delta_{\text{RA}} = 2.5\); the role of this parameter is discussed in the appendix. The second step of the method adds the investor's views. Without views the means are \(\boldsymbol\pi\), and (4.27) returns exactly the benchmark portfolio.

                  Views and the Posterior

                  Slide 34 in the PDF

                  Black–Litterman treats the unknown mean \(\boldsymbol\mu\) as a random variable. The prior is centred on the implied means,

                  \[\boldsymbol\mu \sim N(\boldsymbol\pi,\ \tau\boldsymbol\Sigma),\]
                  (4.29)

                  where the scalar \(\tau > 0\) measures the uncertainty about \(\boldsymbol\pi\) relative to the covariance of returns. The \(K\) views are written \(\mathbf{q} = \mathbf{P}\boldsymbol\mu + \boldsymbol\varepsilon\), with \(\boldsymbol\varepsilon \sim N(\mathbf{0}, \boldsymbol\Omega)\) independent of \(\boldsymbol\mu\). The matrix \(\mathbf{P}\) is \(K \times N\) and its row \(k\) is the portfolio of view \(k\); \(\mathbf{q}\) holds the returns stated in the views and \(\boldsymbol\Omega\) their uncertainty. On the US sectors, \(K = 1\) and \(\mathbf{P}\) has \(+1\) on XLK, \(-1\) on XLU and zeros elsewhere.

                  Both components of the model are Normal, and the view error is independent of the prior. The precision, the inverse of the variance, of the posterior is then the sum of the precisions: \(\mathbf{M}^{-1} = (\tau\boldsymbol\Sigma)^{-1} + \mathbf{P}^\top\boldsymbol\Omega^{-1}\mathbf{P}\). The posterior mean is the precision-weighted average of the two sources:

                  \[\boldsymbol\mu_{\text{BL}} = \big[(\tau\boldsymbol\Sigma)^{-1} + \mathbf{P}^\top\boldsymbol\Omega^{-1}\mathbf{P}\big]^{-1}\big[(\tau\boldsymbol\Sigma)^{-1}\boldsymbol\pi + \mathbf{P}^\top\boldsymbol\Omega^{-1}\mathbf{q}\big].\]
                  (4.30)

                  An equivalent form, which does not require inverting \(\tau\boldsymbol\Sigma\), writes the posterior mean as the prior mean plus a correction proportional to the gap between the views and what the prior implies for the view portfolios:

                  \[\boldsymbol\mu_{\text{BL}} = \boldsymbol\pi + \tau\boldsymbol\Sigma\mathbf{P}^\top\big(\mathbf{P}\,\tau\boldsymbol\Sigma\,\mathbf{P}^\top + \boldsymbol\Omega\big)^{-1}\big(\mathbf{q} - \mathbf{P}\boldsymbol\pi\big).\]
                  (4.31)

                  The Black–Litterman weights follow from (4.27) with the posterior mean:

                  \[\mathbf{w}_{\text{BL}} = (\delta_{\text{RA}}\boldsymbol\Sigma)^{-1}\boldsymbol\mu_{\text{BL}} = \mathbf{w}_b + \frac{\tau}{\delta_{\text{RA}}}\,\mathbf{P}^\top\big(\mathbf{P}\,\tau\boldsymbol\Sigma\,\mathbf{P}^\top + \boldsymbol\Omega\big)^{-1}\big(\mathbf{q} - \mathbf{P}\boldsymbol\pi\big),\]
                  (4.32)

                  where the second equality uses (4.28) and (4.31). With cash access, no additional constraints and the same matrix \(\boldsymbol\Sigma\) in the optimisation, the weights depart from \(\mathbf{w}_b\) only along the directions given by \(\mathbf{P}^\top\), and the departure grows with the gap \(\mathbf{q} - \mathbf{P}\boldsymbol\pi\) between the views and the implied means. The predictive covariance \(\boldsymbol\Sigma + \mathbf{M}\) or portfolio constraints can change this result.

                  The limiting cases show the role of \(\boldsymbol\Omega\). As \(\boldsymbol\Omega \to \mathbf{0}\), the views hold exactly, \(\mathbf{P}\boldsymbol\mu_{\text{BL}} = \mathbf{q}\); as \(\boldsymbol\Omega \to \infty\), they are ignored and \(\boldsymbol\mu_{\text{BL}} = \boldsymbol\pi\). The confidence in a view is set by \(\boldsymbol\Omega\) relative to \(\tau\boldsymbol\Sigma\), not by \(\tau\) alone (appendix on the limiting cases, \(\delta_{\text{RA}}\) and \(\tau\)). The same estimator is the solution of a mixed regression estimated by generalised least squares (GLS), in which the prior and the views are observations of \(\boldsymbol\mu\) (Theil & Goldberger, 1961); the derivation is in the appendix.

                  With a single asset, (4.30) is a weighted average of two scalars. Let \(\pi = 5\%\), \(\tau\sigma^2 = 0.00128\) and the view \(8\%\) with variance \(0.0004\). The weight of the view is the ratio of its precision to the total precision, \(0.00128/(0.00128 + 0.0004) = 0.76\), so \(\mu_{\text{BL}} = 7.29\%\). The view receives the larger weight because its variance is smaller than the prior uncertainty.

                  • Prior: \(\boldsymbol\mu \sim N(\boldsymbol\pi, \tau\boldsymbol\Sigma)\)
                    • \(\tau\) scales the uncertainty about \(\boldsymbol\pi\)
                  • \(K\) views: \(\mathbf{P}\boldsymbol\mu = \mathbf{q} + \boldsymbol\varepsilon\), \(\boldsymbol\varepsilon \sim N(\mathbf{0}, \boldsymbol\Omega)\)
                    • \(\mathbf{P}\): \(K\times N\) matrix, row \(k\) = the portfolio of view \(k\)
                    • \(\mathbf{q}\): the view returns; \(\boldsymbol\Omega\): their uncertainty

                  Section summary

                  Black–Litterman replaces the sample means by the posterior mean (4.30), a precision-weighted average of the implied means (4.28) and the investor's views (Black & Litterman, 1992). The implied means require no estimated mean, only \(\boldsymbol\Sigma\), and without views the optimal portfolio is the benchmark itself. By (4.32), without additional constraints and with the same \(\boldsymbol\Sigma\) in the optimisation, a view changes only the weights of the assets in its portfolio.

                  On the US sectors, the view XLK minus XLU \(= 3\%\) moves the implied difference from 2.2% to 2.6% and the XLK and XLU weights by 3.3 percentage points each, whereas the 10-year sample means would produce positions of \(+115\%\) in XLK and \(-68\%\) in XLY. The method shrinks the means towards a benchmark portfolio chosen by the investor. The next section drops the means altogether and builds portfolios from the risk of the assets alone.

                  Risk Parity and Hierarchical Risk Parity

                  The previous sections showed that estimating the means is the main source of error in mean–variance weights, and that inverting the covariance matrix amplifies the errors in \(\hat{\boldsymbol\Sigma}\). The rules of this section avoid both problems. They use only the covariance matrix and do not require its inverse: the weights are chosen so that the portfolio's risk is distributed in a preset way across assets or across groups of assets.

                  The first rule, equal risk contribution (ERC), starts from the decomposition of portfolio volatility into the contributions of the assets and requires these contributions to be equal (Maillard, Roncalli & Teïletche, 2010). The second, Hierarchical Risk Parity (HRP), orders the assets by a correlation tree and splits the capital recursively between groups (López de Prado, 2016). Both produce only positive weights and use no expected returns. The price is that neither is mean–variance optimal except under special assumptions.

                    Risk Contributions

                    Slide 37 in the PDF

                    Portfolio volatility \(\sigma_p(\mathbf{w}) = \sqrt{\mathbf{w}^\top\boldsymbol\Sigma\mathbf{w}}\) is homogeneous of degree one in the weights: \(\sigma_p(c\,\mathbf{w}) = c\,\sigma_p(\mathbf{w})\) for every \(c > 0\). Differentiating this identity with respect to \(c\) at \(c = 1\) gives Euler's theorem for homogeneous functions. Since \(\partial\sigma_p/\partial w_i = (\boldsymbol\Sigma\mathbf{w})_i/\sigma_p\),

                    \[\sigma_p = \sum_{i=1}^N w_i\,\frac{\partial\sigma_p}{\partial w_i} = \sum_{i=1}^N RC_i, \qquad RC_i = \frac{w_i(\boldsymbol\Sigma\mathbf{w})_i}{\sigma_p}.\]
                    (4.33)

                    The quantity \(RC_i\) in (4.33) is the risk contribution of asset \(i\): its weight times its marginal volatility \(\partial\sigma_p/\partial w_i\). The contributions add up exactly to the portfolio volatility, and the percentage share of asset \(i\) is \(RC_i/\sigma_p = w_i(\boldsymbol\Sigma\mathbf{w})_i/\sigma_p^2\). The term \((\boldsymbol\Sigma\mathbf{w})_i\) is the covariance between the return of asset \(i\) and the portfolio return, so the share can be negative for an asset whose covariance with the portfolio is negative.

                    The worked example uses a 50/50 portfolio in SPY and IEF, with annualised moments from monthly data 2002–2026: \(\sigma_1^2 = 0.02166\), \(\sigma_2^2 = 0.00441\) and \(\sigma_{12} = -0.00098\). Then \((\boldsymbol\Sigma\mathbf{w})_1 = 0.01034\) and \((\boldsymbol\Sigma\mathbf{w})_2 = 0.00172\), and \(\sigma_p^2 = 0.5 \times 0.01034 + 0.5 \times 0.00172 = 0.00603\), so \(\sigma_p = 7.76\%\). The shares of risk are 85.8% for SPY and 14.2% for IEF: half of the capital in equities produces almost all of the risk.

                    Risk budgeting reverses the question: the budgets \(b_i \ge 0\), with \(\sum_i b_i = 1\), are fixed in advance, and the weights are chosen so that each asset's share of risk equals its budget. ERC is the special case of equal budgets.

                    In brief

                    • Volatility is homogeneous of degree one in the weights (Euler)
                      \[ \sigma_p = \sum_{i=1}^N w_i\,\frac{\partial\sigma_p}{\partial w_i} = \sum_{i=1}^N \frac{w_i(\boldsymbol\Sigma\mathbf{w})_i}{\sigma_p} \]
                    • Risk contribution of asset \(i\): \(RC_i = w_i(\boldsymbol\Sigma\mathbf{w})_i/\sigma_p\)
                      • percentage share \(RC_i/\sigma_p = w_i(\boldsymbol\Sigma\mathbf{w})_i/\sigma_p^2\)
                    • Worked example, 50/50 in SPY and IEF (monthly 2002–2026, annualised)
                      • \(\sigma_1^2 = 0.02166\), \(\sigma_2^2 = 0.00441\), \(\sigma_{12} = -0.00098\)
                      • \((\boldsymbol\Sigma\mathbf{w})_1 = 0.5(0.02166) + 0.5(-0.00098) = 0.01034\), \((\boldsymbol\Sigma\mathbf{w})_2 = 0.5(-0.00098) + 0.5(0.00441) = 0.00172\)

                    Equal Risk Contribution

                    Slide 38 in the PDF

                    The ERC portfolio of Maillard, Roncalli & Teïletche (2010) requires \(RC_i = RC_j\) for all \(i\) and \(j\). The condition is a nonlinear system in \(\mathbf{w}\), but the solution follows from a convex problem in unnormalised weights \(\mathbf{y}\):

                    \[\min_{\mathbf{y} > 0}\ \tfrac12\,\mathbf{y}^\top\boldsymbol\Sigma\mathbf{y} - \frac1N\sum_{i=1}^N \ln y_i, \qquad \mathbf{w} = \frac{\mathbf{y}}{\mathbf{1}^\top\mathbf{y}}.\]
                    (4.34)

                    The gradient of the objective in (4.34) has components \((\boldsymbol\Sigma\mathbf{y})_i - 1/(N y_i)\). Setting them to zero gives \(y_i(\boldsymbol\Sigma\mathbf{y})_i = 1/N\) for every \(i\). After the normalisation \(\mathbf{w} = \mathbf{y}/s\), with \(s = \mathbf{1}^\top\mathbf{y}\), the products \(w_i(\boldsymbol\Sigma\mathbf{w})_i = 1/(N s^2)\) are equal, so the contributions \(RC_i\) of (4.33) are equal too, each \(\sigma_p/N\). The log term tends to \(+\infty\) as any \(y_i\) tends to zero, so the minimum lies in the interior of the domain and every weight is strictly positive.

                    For positive definite \(\boldsymbol\Sigma\) the quadratic form is strictly convex and \(-\ln y_i\) is convex. The objective is therefore strictly convex and tends to infinity at the boundary of the domain, which guarantees that a solution exists and is unique. The volatilities satisfy \(\sigma_{\text{GMV}} \le \sigma_{\text{ERC}} \le \sigma_{1/N}\) (Maillard, Roncalli & Teïletche, 2010): ERC is a compromise between equal capital weights and minimum variance.

                    With two assets the ERC condition has an explicit form. The equality \(w_1(\boldsymbol\Sigma\mathbf{w})_1 = w_2(\boldsymbol\Sigma\mathbf{w})_2\) reads \(w_1^2\sigma_1^2 + w_1w_2\sigma_{12} = w_2^2\sigma_2^2 + w_1w_2\sigma_{12}\). The covariance term cancels, and for positive weights what remains is \(w_1\sigma_1 = w_2\sigma_2\), that is,

                    \[w_1 = \frac{\sigma_1^{-1}}{\sigma_1^{-1} + \sigma_2^{-1}},\]
                    (4.35)

                    the inverse-volatility weights, for every correlation \(\rho \in (-1, 1)\). For SPY (volatility 14.7%) and IEF (6.6%), ERC holds 31% SPY, has a volatility of 6.1%, and each asset contributes 50% of the variance. With more than two assets the correlations enter the solution: inverse-volatility weights yield ERC when the correlation matrix has equal row sums, and equal pairwise correlations are a special case (other risk-based rules).

                    ERC uses no means, but it can coincide with the tangency portfolio (Maillard, Roncalli & Teïletche, 2010). If the correlation matrix has equal row sums and the individual Sharpe ratios are also equal and positive, the inverse-volatility portfolio is both ERC and a tangency portfolio. These conditions are sufficient, not necessary. In general, ERC gives up part of the in-sample Sharpe ratio in exchange for weights that do not depend on estimated means.

                    In brief

                    • ERC (Maillard, Roncalli & Teïletche (2010)): \(RC_i = RC_j\) for all \(i, j\)
                      • computed from a convex problem, then normalised
                      \[ \min_{\mathbf{y} > 0}\ \tfrac12\mathbf{y}^\top\boldsymbol\Sigma\mathbf{y} - \tfrac1N\textstyle\sum_i \ln y_i, \qquad \mathbf{w} = \mathbf{y}/\mathbf{1}^\top\mathbf{y} \]
                      • \(\mathbf{y}\): unnormalised positive weights; the log term keeps every \(y_i > 0\) and its first-order condition gives \(y_i(\boldsymbol\Sigma\mathbf{y})_i = 1/N\) for all \(i\)
                    • For \(\boldsymbol\Sigma\) positive definite a solution exists and is unique
                      • strictly convex objective, infinite at the boundary
                    • Volatility ordering: \(\sigma_{\text{GMV}} \le \sigma_{\text{ERC}} \le \sigma_{1/N}\)
                    • Two assets: ERC is inverse volatility, \(w_1 = \sigma_1^{-1}/(\sigma_1^{-1} + \sigma_2^{-1})\)
                      • for any \(\rho \in (-1, 1)\)
                      • SPY (\(14.7\%\)) and IEF (\(6.6\%\)): ERC holds \(31\%\) SPY, volatility \(6.1\%\), each asset \(50\%\) of the variance
                    • ERC is the tangency portfolio if all assets have the same Sharpe ratio and equal correlations

                    Hierarchical Risk Parity: From a Tree to Weights

                    Slide 39 in the PDF

                    HRP, proposed by López de Prado (2016), builds the weights in three steps, without inverting the covariance matrix and with positive weights only. Since nothing is inverted, the method also works when \(\boldsymbol\Sigma\) is singular, for example when the number of assets exceeds the number of observations in the window.

                    Step 1 builds a tree. For each pair of assets the correlation distance is \(d_{ij} = \sqrt{(1 - \rho_{ij})/2}\), where \(\rho_{ij}\) is the correlation of assets \(i\) and \(j\); the distance is 0 for \(\rho_{ij} = 1\) and 1 for \(\rho_{ij} = -1\). Then whole distance profiles are compared: \(\tilde d_{ij}\) is the Euclidean distance between columns \(i\) and \(j\) of the matrix \(\mathbf{D} = (d_{ij})\),

                    \[d_{ij} = \sqrt{\tfrac12(1 - \rho_{ij})}, \qquad \tilde d_{ij} = \Big(\sum_{k=1}^N (d_{ki} - d_{kj})^2\Big)^{1/2}.\]
                    (4.36)

                    Two assets are close in the sense of \(\tilde d_{ij}\) in (4.36) if they correlate in the same way with all other assets. Hierarchical clustering by single linkage repeatedly merges the two closest clusters, the distance between clusters being the smallest distance between their members. The result is a dendrogram, the tree of successive merges.

                    Step 2, quasi-diagonalisation, orders the assets as the leaves of the tree, so that similar assets are adjacent. In this order the large covariances are concentrated near the diagonal of the matrix.

                    Step 3 is recursive bisection. The ordered list is split into two halves. Inside each half \(j\) the portfolio with weights proportional to the inverse individual variances, \(\tilde w_i \propto 1/\sigma_i^2\), is formed; its variance is \(V_j = \tilde{\mathbf{w}}_j^\top\boldsymbol\Sigma_j\tilde{\mathbf{w}}_j\), where \(\boldsymbol\Sigma_j\) is the covariance of the assets in half \(j\). The two halves receive the fractions

                    \[\alpha = 1 - \frac{V_1}{V_1 + V_2} = \frac{V_2}{V_1 + V_2}, \qquad 1 - \alpha = \frac{V_1}{V_1 + V_2},\]
                    (4.37)

                    of the capital of the current node, and the procedure is repeated inside each half until single assets remain. By (4.37), the half with the lower variance receives more capital. The covariances enter only through the variances \(V_j\) of the group portfolios, as products of the form \(\tilde{\mathbf{w}}^\top\boldsymbol\Sigma\tilde{\mathbf{w}}\), never through an inverse.

                    The same correlation distance and the minimum spanning tree were used by Mantegna (1999) to describe the hierarchy of a stock market. Hierarchical clustering methods are covered in the textbook of Härdle & Simar (2019), under cluster analysis.

                    In brief

                    • HRP (López de Prado (2016)): three steps, no matrix inversion, all weights positive
                      • works even when \(\boldsymbol\Sigma\) is singular
                    • Step 1, tree
                      • correlation distance \(d_{ij} = \sqrt{(1 - \rho_{ij})/2} \in [0, 1]\); \(\rho_{ij}\): correlation of assets \(i\) and \(j\)
                      • Euclidean distance between the columns of \(\mathbf{D} = (d_{ij})\): \(\tilde d_{ij} = \sqrt{\textstyle\sum_k (d_{ki} - d_{kj})^2}\)
                      • single linkage on \(\tilde d_{ij}\) (repeatedly merge the two closest clusters) gives a dendrogram
                    • Step 2, quasi-diagonalisation
                      • order the assets as the leaves of the tree, so that similar ones are adjacent
                    • Step 3, recursive bisection: split the ordered list in two halves
                      • the halves get \(\alpha\) and \(1-\alpha\), \(\alpha = 1 - V_1/(V_1 + V_2)\), \(V_j = \tilde{\mathbf{w}}_j^\top\boldsymbol\Sigma_j\tilde{\mathbf{w}}_j\) the variance of half \(j\)
                      • \(\tilde{\mathbf{w}}_j\): weights \(\tilde w_i \propto 1/\sigma_i^2\) inside half \(j\), \(\boldsymbol\Sigma_j\) its covariance
                      • repeat inside each half until single assets remain
                    • Mantegna (1999): the same distance and the minimum spanning tree reveal the hierarchy of a stock market
                    • Textbook: Härdle & Simar (2019), cluster analysis

                          Turnover and Costs: One Worked Month (1/2)

                          Slide 44 in the PDF

                          The weights set at one rebalancing do not stay constant until the next. Assets with high returns gain weight, and assets with low returns lose weight. If \(w_{i,t}\) is the weight of asset \(i\) at the start of month \(t\) and \(R_{i,t}\) its total return in month \(t\), the weight at the end of the month is

                          \[w^+_{i,t} = \frac{w_{i,t}(1 + R_{i,t})}{\sum_{j} w_{j,t}(1 + R_{j,t})}, \qquad TO_{t+1} = \sum_{i=1}^N \big|w_{i,t+1} - w^+_{i,t}\big|.\]
                          (4.38)

                          The denominator in (4.38) is \(1 + R_{p,t}\), one plus the portfolio's total return. Turnover \(TO_{t+1}\) is the fraction of wealth traded to move from the drifted weights \(w^+_{i,t}\) to the target weights of the next month, \(w_{i,t+1}\), summed over all \(N\) assets.

                          The example uses 1/N on the sectors. In January 2004 XLB returned \(-4.4\%\) and XLK \(+4.3\%\), and the total return of the 1/N portfolio was 1.18% (1.11% in excess, plus 0.07% on the Treasury bill). The initial weights of 11.11% drifted to 10.50% for XLB and to 11.45% for XLK:

                          \[\tfrac19\,(1 - 0.044)/1.0118 = 10.50\%, \qquad \tfrac19\,(1 + 0.043)/1.0118 = 11.45\%.\]

                          An asset whose return is below the portfolio's loses weight, and one whose return is above the portfolio's gains weight.

                            Inference on One Sharpe Ratio: Lo (2002)

                            Slide 47 in the PDF

                            An estimated Sharpe ratio is a statistic with sampling error. Let \(SR = \mu/\sigma\) be the true monthly Sharpe ratio, with \(\mu\) and \(\sigma\) the mean and standard deviation of the monthly excess return, and \(\widehat{SR} = \hat\mu/\hat\sigma\) its estimator from \(T\) months. Lo (2002) shows that, for i.i.d. returns from the Normal distribution,

                            \[\sqrt{T}\,\big(\widehat{SR} - SR\big) \xrightarrow{d} N\Big(0,\ 1 + \tfrac12 SR^2\Big), \qquad \text{SE}(\widehat{SR}) \approx \sqrt{\big(1 + \tfrac12 SR^2\big)/T}.\]
                            (4.40)

                            The asymptotic variance in (4.40) follows from the delta method. Under the Normal distribution, \(\hat\mu\) and \(\hat\sigma\) are asymptotically independent, with \(\operatorname{Var}(\hat\mu) \approx \sigma^2/T\) and \(\operatorname{Var}(\hat\sigma) \approx \sigma^2/(2T)\). The derivatives of \(\mu/\sigma\) are \(1/\sigma\) and \(-\mu/\sigma^2\), so \(\operatorname{Var}(\widehat{SR}) \approx 1/T + SR^2/(2T)\). The first term comes from the imprecision of the mean, the second from the imprecision of the volatility. Without Normality, the standard error requires a finite fourth moment of the returns, an assumption that Chapter 1 questions.

                            For 1/N on the sectors, with \(T = 271\), the estimated monthly Sharpe ratio is 0.191 and the standard error from (4.40) is 0.061. Annualised by \(\sqrt{12}\), the Sharpe ratio is 0.66 and the standard error 0.21, so the 95% confidence interval is \(0.66 \pm 1.96 \times 0.21\), that is, \([0.25,\ 1.08]\). Even after 271 months, the Sharpe ratio of 1/N is estimated imprecisely.

                            Annualising by \(\sqrt{q}\), with \(q = 12\) months per year, is exact only if monthly returns are not autocorrelated. The sum of \(q\) monthly returns has mean \(q\mu\) and variance \(\sigma^2\big[q + 2\sum_{k=1}^{q-1}(q-k)\rho_k\big]\), where \(\rho_k\) is the autocorrelation of monthly returns at lag \(k\). Their ratio gives \(SR(q) = \eta(q)\,SR\), with

                            \[\eta(q) = \frac{q}{\sqrt{q + 2\sum_{k=1}^{q-1}(q-k)\,\rho_k}}.\]
                            (4.41)

                            Without autocorrelation, (4.41) reduces to \(\sqrt{q}\). Positive autocorrelations lower the factor \(\eta\), and negative ones raise it.

                            For 1/N on the sectors, \(\rho_1 = -0.03\) and the weighted sum \(\sum_{k=1}^{11}(12-k)\rho_k\) is \(-0.50\). Then \(\eta = 12/\sqrt{12 - 2 \times 0.50} = 12/\sqrt{11.0} = 3.62\) instead of \(\sqrt{12} = 3.46\), and the annual Sharpe ratio becomes \(0.191 \times 3.62 = 0.69\). This is the Sharpe ratio of the sum of the 12 monthly returns, not of the compounded annual return \(\prod_t(1 + R_t) - 1\).

                            • Lo (2002), i.i.d. Normal returns: \(\sqrt{T}(\widehat{SR} - SR) \to N\big(0,\ 1 + SR^2/2\big)\)

                              • without Normality, the standard error requires a finite fourth moment, which Chapter 1 questions

                            Testing a Difference in Sharpe Ratios

                            Slide 48 in the PDF

                            The comparison with 1/N concerns the difference \(\Delta = SR_1 - SR_2\) between the Sharpe ratios of two strategies evaluated on the same months, with null hypothesis \(H_0\colon SR_1 = SR_2\). The two return series are correlated, so the estimators of the two Sharpe ratios are not independent, and the standard error of the difference must account for this correlation.

                            The classical test is that of Jobson & Korkie (1981), in the form corrected by Memmel and given in Ledoit & Wolf (2008). For i.i.d. returns from the Normal distribution the statistic is

                            \[z = \frac{\hat\Delta}{\sqrt{\hat\theta/T}}, \qquad \hat\theta = 2(1-\hat\rho) + \tfrac12\big(\widehat{SR}_1^2 + \widehat{SR}_2^2 - 2\,\widehat{SR}_1\widehat{SR}_2\,\hat\rho^2\big),\]
                            (4.42)

                            where \(\hat\rho\) is the correlation of the two strategies' returns, and the Sharpe ratios have the same frequency as the \(T\) observations; annualising the difference requires annualising its standard error accordingly. High correlation reduces the first variance component. As \(\rho \to 1\), the full expression tends to zero only when the Sharpe ratios are also equal. For strategies with similar Sharpe ratios, high correlation can make their difference precisely estimable.

                            Financial returns can exhibit serial dependence and departures from Normality. Ledoit & Wolf (2008) propose a robust version, again by the delta method. Let \(r_{1t}\) and \(r_{2t}\) be the excess returns of the two strategies, with means \(\mu_1\) and \(\mu_2\), and \(\mathbf{v} = (\mu_1, \mu_2, \mathbb{E}[r_1^2], \mathbb{E}[r_2^2])\) the vector of their first two uncentred moments. The difference of the Sharpe ratios is a smooth function of these moments:

                            \[\hat\Delta = f(\hat{\mathbf{v}}), \qquad f(\mathbf{v}) = \frac{\mu_1}{\sqrt{\mathbb{E}[r_1^2] - \mu_1^2}} - \frac{\mu_2}{\sqrt{\mathbb{E}[r_2^2] - \mu_2^2}}, \qquad \text{SE}(\hat\Delta) = \sqrt{\nabla f^\top\hat{\boldsymbol\Psi}\,\nabla f/T}.\]
                            (4.43)

                            In (4.43), \(\hat{\boldsymbol\Psi}\) is a HAC (heteroskedasticity and autocorrelation consistent) estimate of the long-run covariance of the four moments, and \(\nabla f\) is the gradient of \(f\); the step-by-step computation is in the Appendix. The HAC estimator handles heteroskedasticity and autocorrelation, but not the moment condition: the test still requires a finite fourth moment, an assumption that Chapter 1 questions.

                            In brief

                            • \(H_0\): \(SR_1 = SR_2\) for two strategies evaluated on the same months

                              • \(\hat\rho\): the correlation of the two strategies
                            • Ledoit & Wolf (2008): a robust version, by the delta method

                            The Block Bootstrap

                            Slide 49 in the PDF

                            The HAC test relies on the Normal approximation of the statistic \(\hat\Delta/\text{SE}(\hat\Delta)\), which can be inaccurate in finite samples. The bootstrap, introduced by Bradley Efron in 1979, replaces the asymptotic approximation by the distribution of the statistic over resamples of the data. Monthly returns are dependent, however, for example through volatility clustering, and resampling single months would destroy this dependence. Blocks of consecutive months are therefore resampled, by the block bootstrap of Künsch (1989).

                            Ledoit & Wolf (2008) (Section 3.2.2) use circular blocks of pairs \((r_{1t}, r_{2t})\): a block that runs past the end of the sample continues from its start, and the two strategies are resampled in the same months, so the correlation between them is kept. In each draw \(m\) the difference \(\hat\Delta^{*m}\) and its standard error \(s(\hat\Delta^{*m})\) are computed, and the statistic is

                            \[\tilde d^{*m} = \frac{|\hat\Delta^{*m} - \hat\Delta|}{s(\hat\Delta^{*m})}, \qquad p = \frac{\#\{m\colon \tilde d^{*m} \ge |\hat\Delta|/s(\hat\Delta)\} + 1}{M + 1},\]
                            (4.44)

                            with \(M = 4999\) draws. Centring at \(\hat\Delta\) makes the bootstrap distribution mimic the distribution of the statistic under the null hypothesis, and dividing by the standard error makes the statistic studentized.

                            Under regularity conditions and consistent standard-error estimation, the studentized statistic is asymptotically pivotal: its limit distribution contains no unknown parameters. For such statistics the bootstrap can improve, in finite samples, on the Normal approximation of the HAC test. The \(+1\) in the numerator and denominator of (4.44) counts the observed statistic among the \(M + 1\) values compared.

                            The block length is chosen by the calibration of Ledoit & Wolf (2008), Algorithm 3.1, described in the Appendix: the length is chosen whose bootstrap interval has effective coverage closest to 95%. For all six sector rules the chosen block has length 1. Blocks of length one do not preserve serial dependence, and their selection by calibration does not establish that returns are independent. For the multi-asset and combined rules the length is between 1 and 10, most often 10.

                              Holm and Benjamini–Hochberg on This Chapter's Tests

                              Slide 54 in the PDF

                              Two error measures are used for a family of tests. The family-wise error rate (FWER) is the probability of at least one false rejection in the family. The false discovery rate (FDR) is the expected share of false rejections among the rejections. Controlling the FWER is stricter; controlling the FDR accepts a few false rejections in exchange for higher power.

                              Both procedures start from the \(m\) p-values sorted in increasing order, \(p_{(1)} \le \dots \le p_{(m)}\). The Holm procedure controls the FWER under any form of dependence between the tests, and the Benjamini–Hochberg (BH) procedure of Benjamini & Hochberg (1995) controls the FDR. The adjusted p-values are

                              \[\tilde p^{\,\text{Holm}}_{(i)} = \max_{j \le i}\ \min\big\{1,\ (m - j + 1)\,p_{(j)}\big\}, \qquad \tilde p^{\,\text{BH}}_{(i)} = \min_{j \ge i}\ \min\big\{1,\ \tfrac{m}{j}\,p_{(j)}\big\}.\]
                              (4.45)

                              Holm multiplies the smallest p-value by \(m\), as the Bonferroni procedure does, but the following ones by \(m - 1\), \(m - 2\) and so on; the running maximum from the top keeps the adjusted p-values in order. BH multiplies \(p_{(j)}\) by \(m/j\) and takes the running minimum from the bottom up. A hypothesis is rejected where \(\tilde p < 0.05\) for Holm, or where \(\tilde p\) is below the chosen FDR for BH.

                              BH controls the FDR for valid p-values under independence or under a form of positive dependence, the PRDS condition (positive regression dependency on a subset). Positive correlations between strategies do not by themselves establish PRDS. Under arbitrary dependence one uses Holm, which gives the main result of the chapter, or the Benjamini–Yekutieli procedure.

                              The table applies (4.45) to the four smallest two-sided HAC p-values, with \(m = 18\). For Holm, \(18 \times 0.0238 = 0.428\) (GMV-LW, combined), \(17 \times 0.0304 = 0.516\) (GMV, combined), \(16 \times 0.0477 = 0.763\) (GMV-LO, combined), and \(15 \times 0.0720\) exceeds 1 and is truncated to 1.00 (HRP, multi-asset). For BH, the products \(\tfrac{18}{j}\,p_{(j)}\) are 0.428, 0.273, 0.286 and 0.324. Taking the minimum from the bottom up makes the first two adjusted p-values both 0.273, so there is no rejection even at an FDR of 10%.

                                Case Studies: The Implementable Frontier and Volatility-Managed Portfolios

                                This section examines two recent papers that extend the mean–variance problem in directions the chapter's backtest covers only in part.                                 Which Frontier Can a Large Investor Reach? (1/2)

                                Slide 58 in the PDF

                                The formulas of the mean–variance section have no costs. Jensen, Kelly, Malamud & Pedersen (2026) ask which allocation rule stays efficient when costs grow with the size of the investor. The setting is the construction of portfolios from machine-learning (ML) return forecasts, on a market where every trade has a price impact.

                                The central object is the implementable efficient frontier (eq. 12 of the paper). For each volatility \(\sigma\), it gives the highest expected return net of trading costs:

                                \[k(\sigma) = \max_{\pi}\ E[r^{\text{net}}_{t+1}] \quad \text{s.t.} \quad E[\pi_t^\top\boldsymbol\Sigma\pi_t] = \sigma^2.\]
                                (4.46)

                                In (4.46), \(\pi_t\) is the vector of risky-asset weights at time \(t\), in the paper's notation; it is not to be confused with the implied returns \(\boldsymbol\pi\) of Black–Litterman. \(r^{\text{net}}_{t+1}\) is the portfolio's excess return net of trading costs, and \(k(\sigma)\) is the best net expected return at volatility \(\sigma\). Here \(\sigma^2\) measures the average conditional portfolio variance, which need not equal the unconditional variance of net returns.

                                Proposition 1 of the paper shows that the net Sharpe ratio \(k(\sigma)/\sigma\) falls along the frontier. Without costs and with a riskless asset, the Sharpe ratio is the same at every volatility: the slope \(\theta\) of (4.9). With costs, a higher volatility requires larger positions and larger trades, and these cost more than proportionally. For the same reason, a larger investor faces a lower frontier.

                                The trading cost, as a fraction of wealth \(W_t\) (eqs. 4 and 7 of the paper), is quadratic in the trade:

                                \[c_t = \tfrac{W_t}{2}(\pi_t - g_t\pi_{t-1})^\top\boldsymbol\Lambda(\pi_t - g_t\pi_{t-1}), \qquad \boldsymbol\Lambda = \mathrm{diag}(0.2/V_i).\]
                                (4.47)

The factor \(W_t\) makes the relative cost grow with wealth: the same trade, expressed in weights, moves the price more for a larger investor.

                                With quadratic costs, the optimal rule is that of Gârleanu & Pedersen (2013): the investor trades only part of the way towards an aim portfolio \(A_t\),

                                \[\pi_t = m\,g_t\pi_{t-1} + (\mathbf{I}-m)A_t.\]
                                (4.48)

                                The aim \(A_t\) is forward-looking: a weighted average of today's and expected future Markowitz portfolios. The weighting reflects the persistence of the signals and the frictions, so \(A_t\) is in general not the current Markowitz portfolio. In (4.48), \(m\) is an \(N\times N\) matrix of trade rates (eq. 14 of the paper), so \(\mathbf{I}-m\) is a matrix too; the symbol is unrelated to the target mean \(m\) of (4.1). In the scalar special case of Gârleanu & Pedersen (2013), \(m\) is the share of the old position kept each period.

                                The paper's Portfolio-ML rule learns the aim \(A_t\) directly from the signals by a ridge regression: least squares with a penalty on the size of the coefficients. The objective of the regression is not the forecast error of returns but the utility of the portfolio net of costs.

                                Volatility-Managed Portfolios: The Question

                                Slide 61 in the PDF

                                The second case study asks whether the Sharpe ratio rises when the investor cuts exposure in periods of high volatility. The answer can be yes only if measured volatility predicts next month's risk and expected returns do not rise in proportion to variance. If the conditional mean is constant, the optimal weight in a single risky asset, \(\alpha^*\) of (4.10), is inversely proportional to the conditional variance.

                                Moreira & Muir (2017) define the volatility-managed factor \(k\), written as in eq. 1 of DeMiguel, Martín-Utrera & Uppal (2024):

                                \[r^{\sigma}_{k,t+1} = \frac{c}{\sigma^2_{k,t}}\, r_{k,t+1}.\]
                                (4.49)

                                In (4.49), \(r_{k,t+1}\) is the excess return of factor \(k\) in month \(t+1\), and \(\sigma^2_{k,t}\) is the variance of the factor's daily returns in month \(t\), known at the time of the decision. The constant \(c\) gives \(r^{\sigma}_k\) the same volatility as \(r_k\); it is not the cost \(c_t\) of (4.47). The investor does not hold \(r^{\sigma}_k\) alone but the mean–variance combination of \(r_k\) and \(r^{\sigma}_k\).

                                Later evidence is mixed. Cederburg, O'Doherty, Wang & Yan (2020) show that, out of sample, the managed versions do not systematically beat the unmanaged strategies, and that estimation error in the combination weights matters. Barroso & Detzel (2021) show that, after trading costs, the gain is not robust for most of the factors studied.

                                DeMiguel, Martín-Utrera & Uppal (2024) ask the same question for a portfolio of many factors. The paper's claim is that the conditional mean–variance multifactor portfolio (CMV) beats its unconditional counterpart (UMV) out of sample and net of costs. In Table II, with nine factors over 1977–2020, CMV has a net Sharpe ratio of 1.062 against 0.940 for UMV, with \(p = 0.006\).

                                  The Paper's Method and the Course Replication

                                  Slide 63 in the PDF

                                  In CMV (eqs. 2–6 of DeMiguel, Martín-Utrera & Uppal (2024)), the weight of factor \(k\), set at the end of month \(t\) and applied to the return \(r_{k,t+1}\), is a linear function of the inverse of market volatility:

                                  \[\theta_{k,t} = a_k + \frac{b_k}{\sigma_t}.\]
                                  (4.50)

                                  In (4.50), \(\sigma_t\) is the realised volatility of the market in month \(t\), computed from daily returns. A coefficient \(b_k > 0\) means less exposure to factor \(k\) when the market is volatile. The return of the position is \(\theta_{k,t}\,r_{k,t+1} = a_k\,r_{k,t+1} + b_k\,r_{k,t+1}/\sigma_t\), so CMV is a static portfolio in the extended returns \((r_{k,t+1},\ r_{k,t+1}/\sigma_t)\). The coefficients \(\eta = (a, b)\), with \(a_k, b_k \ge 0\), solve a mean–variance problem with costs:

                                  \[\max_{\eta}\ \boldsymbol\mu^\top\eta - \widehat{TC}(\eta) - \frac{\gamma}{2}\,\eta^\top\boldsymbol\Sigma\,\eta, \qquad \gamma = 5,\]
                                  (4.51)

                                  where \(\boldsymbol\mu\) and \(\boldsymbol\Sigma\) are the mean and covariance of the extended returns and \(\widehat{TC}\) is the mean trading cost. UMV is the same problem (4.51) with \(b_k = 0\). CFW is the mean–variance combination of UMV and UMV divided by its variance last month, that is, the rule (4.49) applied to the whole portfolio (Moreira & Muir, 2017).

                                  In the paper, the cost of each stock comes from its monthly estimated spread, and trades net out across factors (trading diversification). The estimation window is expanding, the first one 120 months long.

                                  The course replication keeps the structure and changes the data. The "factors" are the excess returns of the ETFs, that is, positions financed at the T-bill rate; for BVB, returns are in RON with \(R_f = 0\), as in the rest of the chapter. The market is SPY for the US universes and BET-TR for BVB. The window is expanding, the first one 60 months long (36 for BVB), so the out-of-sample periods are those of the chapter's backtest. The chapter's liquidation rule does not bind: no rule loses 100% in a month.

                                  Slide 67 in the PDF

                                  A backtest written with the help of an assistant has the same pitfalls as any backtest, and checking them remains the researcher's task. The first is look-ahead: weights for month \(t\) built with returns of the same month. The test is an explicit assertion that the last estimation date is before \(t\).

                                  The second is survivorship bias and, more generally, selection: an ETF list chosen today contains only the funds that survived. The test is to use the universe available at each date, including ETFs delisted later, or to disclose the limitation.

                                  The third is multiple testing: 6 rules on 3 universes already give 18 tests. The test is to report Holm adjusted \(p\)-values, which control the FWER, or Benjamini–Hochberg adjusted \(p\)-values, which control the FDR (Benjamini & Hochberg, 1995). The fourth is power: for MV-LO minus 1/N on the multi-asset universe, \(\hat\Delta\) has a HAC standard error of 0.18 on 171 months. The minimum detectable effect must be computed before the tests are run.

                                  The fifth concerns the return type: portfolios aggregate simple returns, while Sharpe ratios use excess returns. One month recomputed step by step checks both conventions. The last concerns invented references: an unresolvable DOI or a title that differs from the paper's. A claim resting on such a reference is dropped, and the error goes into AI_ERRORS.md.

                                  \[p = 1 - \Phi\big(\hat\Delta/\text{SE}\big),\]
                                  (4.52)

                                  where \(\Phi\) is the distribution function of the standard Normal distribution and SE the standard error of \(\hat\Delta\).

                                  The replication gives \(\hat\Delta = +0.29\), with a HAC standard error of 0.18. From (4.52), \(z = 0.2863/0.1836 = 1.56\) and \(p = 1 - \Phi(1.56) = 0.06\). The two-sided HAC \(p\)-value is 0.12 and the bootstrap one 0.16. Even without a correction for multiple testing, the difference is not significant at 5%.

                                  Power shows how much data would be needed. Assuming that the standard error scales as \(1/\sqrt{T}\) and that the effect and the long-run variance remain stable, the number of months for which a one-sided test at level \(\alpha\) has power 0.8 against \(\Delta = 0.29\) is

                                  \[T = T_{\text{obs}}\Big[\frac{(z_{1-\alpha} + z_{0.8})\,\text{SE}}{\Delta}\Big]^2,\]
                                  (4.53)

                                  with \(T_{\text{obs}} = 171\) observed months. At \(\alpha = 5\%\), \(171\,\big[(1.645 + 0.842) \times 0.1836/0.2863\big]^2 \approx 435\) months, about 36 years. At the Bonferroni threshold \(5\%/18\), which is also the first Holm threshold, \(z_{1-\alpha} = 2.77\), and (4.53) gives about 919 months, or 77 years. These are power scenarios based on the current estimates.

                                  Slide 78 in the PDF

                                  The HAC Test Step by Step

                                  Slide 79 in the PDF
                                  • Gradient of \(f\) with respect to \(\mathbf{v} = (\mu_1, \mu_2, \mathbb{E}[r_1^2], \mathbb{E}[r_2^2])\)

                                  The Frontier in Closed Form, Step by Step

                                  Slide 83 in the PDF
                                  • Two constraints, two multipliers: \(\mathcal{L} = \tfrac12\mathbf{w}^\top\boldsymbol\Sigma\mathbf{w} - \lambda_1(\mathbf{w}^\top\boldsymbol\mu - m) - \lambda_2(\mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{1} - 1)\)
                                  • First-order condition: \(\boldsymbol\Sigma\mathbf{w} = \lambda_1\boldsymbol\mu + \lambda_2\mathbf{1}\), so \(\mathbf{w} = \lambda_1\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}\boldsymbol\mu + \lambda_2\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf{1}\)
                                  • The constraints, with \(A = \mathbf{1}^\top\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf{1}\), \(B = \mathbf{1}^\top\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}\boldsymbol\mu\), \(C = \boldsymbol\mu^\top\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}\boldsymbol\mu\), \(D = AC - B^2\)
                                    \[ \boldsymbol\mu^\top\mathbf{w} = \lambda_1 C + \lambda_2 B = m, \qquad \mathbf{1}^\top\mathbf{w} = \lambda_1 B + \lambda_2 A = 1 \]
                                  • Solving the \(2\times 2\) system: \(\lambda_1 = (Am - B)/D\), \(\lambda_2 = (C - Bm)/D\)
                                  • Variance: \(\mathbf{w}^\top\boldsymbol\Sigma\mathbf{w} = \mathbf{w}^\top(\lambda_1\boldsymbol\mu + \lambda_2\mathbf{1}) = \lambda_1 m + \lambda_2 = \dfrac{Am^2 - 2Bm + C}{D}\)
                                  • Check: at \(m = B/A\) the variance is \((C - B^2/A)/D = 1/A\), the GMV
                                  • Shapes: \(\sigma^2\) is quadratic in \(m\) (a parabola in the \((\sigma^2, m)\) plane)
                                    • \(\sigma^2 - A(m - B/A)^2/D = 1/A\) is a hyperbola in the \((\sigma, m)\) plane

                                  Wishart Moments and the Distribution of Weights

                                  Slide 84 in the PDF
                                  • i.i.d. \(N(\boldsymbol\mu, \boldsymbol\Sigma)\) returns, maximum-likelihood estimators
                                    • \(\hat{\boldsymbol\mu} \sim N(\boldsymbol\mu, \boldsymbol\Sigma/T)\), \(T\hat{\boldsymbol\Sigma} \sim W_N(T-1, \boldsymbol\Sigma)\), independent
                                    • Wishart: the matrix analogue of \(\chi^2\), \(T-1\) degrees of freedom
                                  • Inverse Wishart moment, finite for \(T > N + 2\): \(\mathbb{E}[\hat{\boldsymbol\Sigma}^{-1}] = \frac{T}{T-N-2}\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}\)
                                    • an upward bias that grows with \(N/T\)
                                  • Conditioning on \(\hat{\boldsymbol\mu}\) and using \(\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{x}^\top\mathbf{K}\mathbf{x}] = \operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{K}\operatorname{Var}(\mathbf{x})) + \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{x}]^\top\mathbf{K}\,\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{x}]\)
                                    • any random vector \(\mathbf{x}\), fixed matrix \(\mathbf{K}\), \(\operatorname{tr}\): trace
                                    • gives \(\mathbb{E}[\hat\theta^2]\) of the lecture (Seminar 4, A8)
                                  • GMV weights with Wishart \(\hat{\boldsymbol\Sigma}\) and \(T\) large enough relative to \(N\)
                                    • \(\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{\text{GMV}}\) is unbiased and its exact distribution and covariance are known (Okhrin & Schmid (2006))

                                  Bootstrap Details and the Block Length

                                  Slide 97 in the PDF
                                  • Studentization: \(s(\hat\Delta^*)\) from the block sums of each resample
                                    • \(s(\hat\Delta)\) on the data from a prewhitened quadratic-spectral kernel (Andrews & Monahan (1992)) with automatic bandwidth (Andrews (1991))
                                  • Ledoit & Wolf (2008), Algorithm 3.1 (grid and \(K\) of Sections 4–5, applied to each of the 18 pairs)
                                    • choose the block \(b\) whose bootstrap interval has actual coverage closest to \(95\%\)
                                    • Fit a first-order vector autoregression to the pair of returns; resample its residuals by a stationary bootstrap (Politis & Romano (1994)), mean block 5
                                    • Generate \(K = 5000\) pseudo-samples of length \(T\); in each, build the 95% interval for \(b \in \{1, 2, 4, 6, 8, 10\}\)
                                    • \(\hat g(b)\): share of intervals that contain the pseudo-true \(\hat\Delta\); pick \(\tilde b = \arg\min_b |\hat g(b) - 0.95|\)
                                  • A non-studentized bootstrap with a fixed 6-month block is not this test
                                    • its block is not calibrated for coverage

                                  The Deflated Sharpe Ratio (1/2)

                                  Slide 98 in the PDF
                                  • Bailey & López de Prado (2014): the best of \(N\) Sharpe ratios is biased upwards even if every true Sharpe ratio is zero
                                  • Expected maximum under \(H_0\): all true Sharpe ratios zero, \(N\) independent trials
                                    • monthly units as \(T\)
                                    • on this slide \(N\) counts trials (configurations), not assets
                                    • \(\gamma_E = 0.5772\): Euler's constant; \(\hat V\): the variance of the \(N\) estimates
                                    • \(\Phi\): standard Normal distribution function; \(e\): Euler's number
                                    \[ SR_0 = \sqrt{\hat V}\,\Big[(1-\gamma_E)\,\Phi^{-1}\big(1 - \tfrac1N\big) + \gamma_E\,\Phi^{-1}\big(1 - \tfrac{1}{Ne}\big)\Big] \]
                                  • Deflated Sharpe ratio: the probability that the true Sharpe of the winner exceeds \(SR_0\)
                                    • with skewness \(\hat\gamma_3\) and kurtosis \(\hat\gamma_4\) of the winner
                                    \[ \text{DSR} = \Phi\Big(\frac{(\widehat{SR} - SR_0)\sqrt{T-1}}{\sqrt{1 - \hat\gamma_3\widehat{SR} + \frac{\hat\gamma_4 - 1}{4}\widehat{SR}^2}}\Big) \]

                                  Case Study, Method in Detail (1/2)

                                  Slide 101 in the PDF
                                  • Cost (eqs. 4, 7)
                                    • \(\pi_t\): risky-asset weights; \(1 - \mathbf{1}^\top\pi_t\): the weight of the risk-free asset
                                    • \(g_t = \mathrm{diag}\big((1 + r^f_t + r_t)/(1 + g^w_t)\big)\) (eq. 6); \(r^f_t\): risk-free rate, \(r_t\): asset returns, \(g^w_t\): growth of wealth
                                    • \(\boldsymbol\Lambda = \mathrm{diag}(0.2/V_i)\); \(V_i\): average daily dollar volume over 6 months; trading \(1\%\) of \(V_i\) moves the price by \(0.1\%\) (eq. 35, Frazzini, Israel & Moskowitz (2018))
                                  • Optimal rule (Proposition 3): \(\pi_t = m\,g_t\pi_{t-1} + (\mathbf{I}-m)A_t\), generalising Gârleanu & Pedersen (2013)
                                    • \(A_t\): a weighted sum of today's and expected future Markowitz portfolios; \(m\) solves eq. (14)
                                  • Portfolio-ML (Proposition 4, eq. 40): \(A_t = \mathrm{diag}(1/\sigma_{i,t})\,f(s_t)\beta\)
                                    • \(\sigma_{i,t}\): volatility of asset \(i\); \(f\): \(p\) random Fourier features of the signals \(s_t\)
                                    • \(\hat\beta = (E_T[\tilde{\boldsymbol\Sigma}_t] + \lambda\mathbf{I})^{-1}E_T[\tilde r_{t+1}]\): a ridge regression (\(\lambda\): penalty) that maximises utility net of costs in closed form (eqs. 24–26)
                                    • it learns the weights directly, as Brandt, Santa-Clara & Valkanov (2009) do without costs
