Minsky (1992) classifies borrowers by whether their cash flows can service their debt: hedge finance covers interest and principal, speculative finance only the interest, and Ponzi finance not even the interest, so that repayment depends on a rising asset price. A long period of stability shifts the weight of borrowers towards speculative and Ponzi finance. Kindleberger & Aliber (2005) describe the same dynamics as a sequence: displacement, boom, euphoria, distress and panic. The tulip mania, the South Sea and Mississippi bubbles of 1720 and the crash of 1929 show, however, why a bubble is hard to call: to call a price a bubble we need a model of fundamental value, and the test must work on a single time series, in real time, before the panic.

Explosiveness tests answer the second requirement. On the S&P Composite price-dividend ratio, monthly from 1871 to 2026, the GSADF (Generalised Sup Augmented Dickey–Fuller) statistic of Phillips, Shi & Yu (2015a) is 3.41, against a 95% critical value of 2.39, while the full-sample ADF (Augmented Dickey–Fuller) test, at −1.11, sees nothing. Date-stamping finds the episodes Nov 1928–Sep 1929 and May 1997–Aug 2001. The dot-com peak of the ratio is 90.2, in Aug 2000, against a long-run mean of 29.8, but the sample maximum, 91.2 in May 2026, falls in no episode: a high ratio is not necessarily explosive.

The second question is whether a sharp run-up announces a crash. In the industry data of Greenwood, Shleifer & You (2019), replicated in the course, a run-up above 100% was followed by a 40% crash in 45% of cases, with a 95% confidence interval (CI) of [27%, 62%], against 11% for a random industry-month. The mean return over the next two years, however, is not significantly different from zero, so run-ups raise the risk of a crash, not the predictable loss. The credit that finances the boom, Minsky's mechanism, appears in the study of Greenwood, Hanson, Shleifer & Sørensen (2022): in the course replication, after rapid growth of credit and asset prices (the R-zone), a household-sector crisis begins within three years in 34% of cases, against 4% in normal periods, based on only 25 crises.

What Is a Bubble?

Fundamental Value and the Rational Bubble

Slide 5 in the PDF
  • Question: which part of the price cannot be justified by dividends, and what can we test about it?
  • No-arbitrage pricing: \(P_t = \E_t[M_{t+1}(P_{t+1} + D_{t+1})]\)
    • \(P_t\): price at the end of period \(t\); \(D_{t+1}\): dividend paid in period \(t + 1\)
    • \(M_{t+1}\): stochastic discount factor; \(\E_t\): expectation under the physical measure, given the information at \(t\)
  • Simplifying assumption: \(M_{t+1} \equiv 1/(1 + r)\), so \(P_t = \E_t[P_{t+1} + D_{t+1}]/(1 + r)\)
    • A constant risk premium does not make \(M_{t+1}\) constant
    • In general, the bubble satisfies \(B_t = \E_t[M_{t+1}B_{t+1}]\)
  • Solving forward gives the fundamental value \(F_t = \sum_{i \ge 1} \E_t[D_{t+i}]/(1 + r)^i\)
    • The present value of expected dividends
    • Gordon case, dividends growing at \(g < r\): \(F_t = D_{t+1}/(r - g)\)
  • But \(P_t = F_t + B_t\) also solves the pricing equation, for any \(B_t\) with \(\E_t[B_{t+1}] = (1 + r)\,B_t\)
    • \(B_t\) is the rational bubble: investors pay for it because they expect to resell at a higher price Blanchard & Watson (1982)

Why a Rational Bubble Must Explode

Slide 6 in the PDF
  • From \(\E_t[B_{t+1}] = (1 + r)B_t\): \(\E_t[B_{t+h}] = (1 + r)^h B_t\) for any horizon of \(h\) periods
    • An explosive conditional mean
    • Individual paths can crash
    • The bubble grows faster than dividends (\(r > g\)), so its share of the price rises over time
  • Rational bubbles are positive and exist from the first trade Diba & Grossman (1988)
    • A negative bubble would eventually push the expected price below zero
    • A bubble that is zero today must stay zero
  • A bubble is the failure of the transversality condition \(\lim_{h \to \infty} \E_t[P_{t+h}]/(1 + r)^h = 0\)
    • The price contains a value never paid out as dividends (derivation in the Appendix)
  • Testable implication
    • If dividends are at most I(1), then without a bubble the price and the price-dividend ratio are not explosive

Bubbles That Burst: Blanchard and Watson

Slide 7 in the PDF
  • Each period the bubble survives with probability \(\pi\) and collapses with probability \(1 - \pi\) Blanchard & Watson (1982)
    • \(B_{t+1} = \dfrac{1 + r}{\pi}\,B_t + \varepsilon_{t+1}\) with probability \(\pi\), \(\ B_{t+1} = \varepsilon_{t+1}\) otherwise
    • \(\pi \in (0, 1)\): survival probability; \(\varepsilon_{t+1}\): a shock with mean zero given the information at \(t\)
  • Expected growth is still \(r\)
    • While it survives the bubble grows faster, at \((1 + r)/\pi - 1\), to pay for the crash risk
  • Expected lifetime: \(1/(1 - \pi)\) periods, a geometric waiting time

The Evans Form: Bubbles That Restart

Slide 8 in the PDF
  • The problem: after a Blanchard–Watson collapse, \(B = \varepsilon\) can be negative, which Diba & Grossman (1988) rule out
  • Evans form: the bubble starts and restarts from a value \(\delta > 0\) Evans (1991)
    • Survival (probability \(\pi\)): \(B_{t+1} = \big[\delta + \frac{1 + r}{\pi}(B_t - \frac{\delta}{1 + r})\big]u_{t+1}\)
    • Collapse (probability \(1 - \pi\)): \(B_{t+1} = \delta\,u_{t+1}\)
    • \(u_{t+1}\): positive noise with mean 1, drawn independently of survival
  • The bubble condition still holds: \(\E_t[B_{t+1}] = \pi\big[\delta + \frac{1 + r}{\pi}B_t - \frac{\delta}{\pi}\big] + (1 - \pi)\delta = (1 + r)B_t\)
  • Small-bubble branch: if \(B_t \le \alpha\), then \(B_{t+1} = (1 + r)B_t u_{t+1}\)
    • The two branches above apply only for \(B_t > \alpha\)
    • Without the threshold, the survival branch turns negative for \(B_t < \delta(1 - \pi)/(1 + r)\)
    • With \(0 < \delta < (1 + r)\alpha\) the bubble stays positive; the simulation uses \(\alpha = \delta\)

A Simulated Rational Bubble (1/2)

Slide 9 in the PDF
    Question for the room For an Evans bubble much larger than its restart value δ, with r = 1%, how does the mean growth rate conditional on survival change when ▶ raises π from 0.97 to 0.995?
    Answer

    It falls from about 4.1% to 1.5% per period. For Bₜ > δ, the mean growth rate conditional on survival is (1 + r)/π − 1 − (δ/Bₜ)(1 − π)/π; when Bₜ is much larger than δ, the last term is small. The unconditional mean growth stays at r = 1%; it is not the growth rate conditional on survival. The values 33.3 and 200 periods, 1/(1 − π), are geometric waiting times under a constant collapse risk, not the exact duration of Evans episodes. With π = 0.97, the simulated bubble collapsed visibly 4 times, and at its peak the price was 1.46 times the fundamental value.

      Bubbles for Fama: The Design

      Slide 15 in the PDF
      • Fama's objection: a “bubble” is only a bubble if its end is predictable Fama (2014)
        • Otherwise it may be a rational reaction to news
      • Greenwood, Shleifer and You answer with all US industries since 1926 Greenwood, Shleifer & You (2019)
        • Not with famous episodes only
        • Do sharp run-ups predict crashes, or low returns?
      • Run-up: value-weighted industry return over the past 2 years above a threshold (e.g. \(100\%\))
        • Each firm weighted by its market capitalisation
        • Both conditions at once: the raw return and the return net of the market exceed the threshold
        • And a raw return of at least \(50\%\) over the past 5 years (from 1931), so that recoveries from slumps are not counted
        • First month of the run-up; a new episode in the same industry only two years later
      • Crash
        • A drawdown of at least \(40\%\) from a previous high within the next 2 years
        • Each crash frequency comes with a 95% CI (Confidence Interval)
      • The sample: the first 48 of the 49 industries of the Kenneth French Data Library (without “Other”)
        • Months with at least ten firms: 41 721 industry-months, 1926–2026

      What Do Sharp Run-Ups Predict? (1/2)

      Slide 16 in the PDF
        Question for the room Among the 53 episodes selected by the Greenwood, Shleifer and You rule, a crash of at least 40% follows in 45% of the cases. The mean return over the next two years is +2%, with a 95% CI of [−13%, 20%]. Which conclusion about the mean return do the data support?
        Answer

        There is no clear evidence that it is negative. The frequency of a crash and the return at the end of the two years measure different things. The interval contains negative and positive values; it does not establish that the mean is zero. A crash follows in 45% of the cases (53 episodes in 27 industries, 95% CI [27%, 62%]), against 11% for an arbitrary industry-month. Selection requires the two-year return to exceed the threshold both raw and in excess of the market, a raw five-year return of at least 50%, and at least two years between episodes of the same industry.

        Testing for Explosive Behaviour

        Right-Tailed ADF (1/2): Regression and Hypothesis

        Slide 18 in the PDF
        • Question: how do we show, on a single price series, that there was an explosive phase and when it started?
        • Regression on the log price \(y_t\): \(\Delta y_t = a + b\,y_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t\)
          • \(\Delta y_t = y_t - y_{t-1} = \ln(P_t/P_{t-1})\): the log return; \(\varepsilon_t\): shock; here no lagged \(\Delta y\)
          • \(\mathrm{ADF} = \hat b / \mathrm{SE}(\hat b)\): the Augmented Dickey–Fuller (ADF) \(t\)-statistic; \(\hat b\): the OLS (Ordinary Least Squares) estimate; SE: its Standard Error
          • \(b < 0\): mean reversion; \(b = 0\): random walk (unit root); \(b > 0\): each rise feeds the next, an explosive path
        • Bubble test: \(H_0: b = 0\) (unit root) vs \(H_1: b > 0\) (explosive root), the right tail Phillips, Wu & Yu (2011)
          • The left-tail Dickey–Fuller table does not apply

        Right-Tailed ADF (2/2): Collapsing Bubbles

        Slide 19 in the PDF
        • Evans: a bubble that collapses periodically leaves the full-sample test with low power Evans (1991)
          • The whole series looks stationary or like a random walk
          • Collapses give the full-sample slope a negative sign (Appendix)
        • Solution: run the ADF regression on many windows and take the largest statistic
          • SADF (Sup Augmented Dickey–Fuller) Phillips, Wu & Yu (2011) and GSADF (Generalised Sup Augmented Dickey–Fuller) Phillips, Shi & Yu (2015a) differ in the windows they search

        Three Ways to Choose Windows (1/2)

        Slide 20 in the PDF
          Question for the room With T = 440 months, how many ADF regressions does GSADF run with the minimum window of the PSY rule?
          Answer

          The rule gives r₀ = 0.01 + 1.8/√440 = 0.096, i.e. w₀ = 42 months. SADF runs 399 windows (one per end point), GSADF 79,800 (all start–end pairs), hence the supremum over about 80,000 windows.

          • \(\mathrm{ADF}_{r_1}^{r_2}\): the ADF statistic on the window from fraction \(r_1\) to fraction \(r_2\) of the sample
            • Each bar in the chart is one ADF regression
            • \(r_0\): the minimum window, as a fraction of the \(T\) changes \(\Delta y_t\); \(\sup\): the largest value over the windows allowed
          • Minimum window rule: \(r_0 = 0.01 + 1.8/\sqrt{T}\) Phillips, Shi & Yu (2015a)
            • E.g. \(T = 440\) months: \(r_0 = 0.096\), i.e. 42 months
            • Too short a window: an imprecise ADF and more false alarms; too long: short bubbles are missed
            • \(w_0 = r_0 T = 0.01T + 1.8\sqrt{T}\): the minimum share \(r_0\) falls towards \(1\%\), but the minimum number of observations grows
            • An episode of fixed length can become too short for the minimum window of a very long sample

          Three Ways to Choose Windows (2/2)

          Slide 21 in the PDF
          • SADF Phillips, Wu & Yu (2011)
            • \(\mathrm{SADF}(r_0) = \sup_{r_2 \in [r_0, 1]} \mathrm{ADF}_0^{r_2}\)
            • Windows start at the first observation and end anywhere after \(r_0\)
          • GSADF Phillips, Shi & Yu (2015a)
            • \(\mathrm{GSADF}(r_0) = \sup_{r_2 \in [r_0, 1]}\ \sup_{r_1 \in [0, r_2 - r_0]} \mathrm{ADF}_{r_1}^{r_2}\)
            • Windows start and end anywhere, with length at least \(r_0\)
          • BSADF (Backward Sup Augmented Dickey–Fuller): backward SADF at the end point \(r_2\) Phillips, Shi & Yu (2015a)
            • \(\mathrm{BSADF}_{r_2} = \sup_{r_1 \in [0, r_2 - r_0]} \mathrm{ADF}_{r_1}^{r_2}\), \(\ \mathrm{GSADF} = \sup_{r_2} \mathrm{BSADF}_{r_2}\)
            • \(\mathrm{BSADF}_{r_2}\) uses only data up to \(r_2\): it can be computed in real time
          • A larger search needs a higher bar: the critical value is simulated for each \(T\), \(r_0\) and lag order

          Date-Stamping an Episode (1/2): the PSY Rules

          Slide 22 in the PDF
          • Origination, in the PSY (Phillips, Shi and Yu) procedure Phillips, Shi & Yu (2015a)
            • The first date at which \(\mathrm{BSADF}_{r_2}\) exceeds its critical value \(cv_{r_2}\)
            • \(cv_{r_2}\): the 95% quantile of \(\mathrm{BSADF}_{r_2}\) under the null, date by date
          • Termination (PSY, eq. 8) Phillips, Shi & Yu (2015a)
            • The first date after \(\hat r_e + \delta \ln(T)/T\) at which \(\mathrm{BSADF}\) falls below \(cv_{r_2}\)
            • \(\hat r_e\): the estimated start, as a fraction of the sample; \(T\): number of changes \(\Delta y_t\)
            • \(\delta \ln(T)/T\): a minimum duration, with \(\delta\) chosen for the data frequency
            • Why \(\ln T\): an isolated exceedance often occurs by chance; a duration that grows slowly with \(T\) removes short alarms without losing long bubbles
          • PWY (Phillips, Wu and Yu) date-stamping uses the recursive \(\mathrm{ADF}_0^{r_2}\) instead Phillips, Wu & Yu (2011)
            • It may miss a second bubble after a collapse

                  The Log-Periodic Power Law (LPPLS)

                  Crashes as Critical Points

                  Slide 36 in the PDF
                  Didier Sornette, ETH Zurich Photo: Didier Sornette (2012); CC BY-SA 3.0 (Germany); Wikimedia Commons
                  • Question: can the shape of the run-up tell when the bubble ends?
                  • Johansen, Ledoit and Sornette (JLS) Johansen, Ledoit & Sornette (2000)
                    • Imitation among traders makes the hazard rate of a crash rise as a critical time \(t_c\) approaches
                    • Hazard rate: the crash probability per unit time, given no crash yet
                  • Rational investors stay in only if the price grows fast enough
                    • The extra growth pays for the rising crash risk
                  • Result: faster-than-exponential growth
                    • Decorated by oscillations that speed up near \(t_c\)
                  • \(t_c\) is the most probable end of the bubble, not a certain crash date Sornette (2017)

                  The LPPLS Equation

                  Slide 37 in the PDF
                  • \(\ln p(t) = A + B\,(t_c - t)^m + C\,(t_c - t)^m \cos\!\left(\omega \ln(t_c - t) - \phi\right)\), \(\ t < t_c\)
                    • In words: the log price is a power-law trend towards the critical time plus oscillations whose frequency rises as \(t_c\) approaches; \(p(t)\): price at time \(t\); \(t_c\): critical time
                  • \(A\): log price at \(t_c\)
                  • \(B < 0\): the super-exponential trend for a rising bubble
                  • \(0 < m < 1\): power-law exponent
                    • The growth rate of the power-law trend, \(-B m (t_c - t)^{m - 1}\), rises without bound as \(t \to t_c\)
                  • \(C\), \(\omega\), \(\phi\): amplitude, angular log-frequency and phase of the log-periodic oscillations
                  • Seven parameters: \(A, B, C, \phi\) and the nonlinear \(t_c, m, \omega\)
                  • LPPL: the log-periodic power law pattern
                    • LPPLS: the model with the finite-time singularity at \(t_c\)

                  Qualified Fits: Conditions on the Parameters and the Residuals

                  Slide 38 in the PDF
                  • A fit on the estimation window \([t_1, t_2]\) is qualified
                    • If it passes all conditions of Shu & Zhu (2020a, eq. 12)
                    • And \(B < 0\) for a rising bubble
                    • \(m \in [0.01, 0.99]\), \(\omega \in [2, 25]\), \(t_c \in [t_2, t_2 + (t_2 - t_1)/5]\)
                    • Damping \(\frac{m|B|}{\omega|C|} \ge 1\) (already imposed in the search)
                    • Number of oscillations (half-periods) \(O = \frac{\omega}{\pi}\ln\frac{t_c - t_1}{t_c - t_2} \ge 2.5\), so the oscillations are not noise
                    • Largest relative error of the fitted price \(\max_t |\hat p_t - p_t|/p_t \le 0.15\)
                  • Lomb periodogram of the detrended residual \((t_c - t)^{-m}(\ln p_t - A - B(t_c - t)^m)\)
                    • A heuristic filter with nominal level \(0.10\): the trend is fitted on the same data, so the p-value is not calibrated
                    • Lomb periodogram: spectral power for unevenly spaced points, here the values of \(\ln(t_c - t)\)
                  • The residual \(\ln \hat p_t - \ln p_t\) must be stationary
                    • Dickey–Fuller and Phillips–Perron reject a unit root at the nominal level \(0.10\), again a heuristic filter

                          The Markov-Switching Model

                          Slide 49 in the PDF
                          • Question: can run-ups and crashes be described as alternating risk states, without dating a bubble?
                          • Weekly log return \(r_t = \mu_{s_t} + \sigma_{s_t}\varepsilon_t\), \(\varepsilon_t \sim N(0, 1)\), regime \(s_t \in \{1, 2\}\) unobserved Hamilton (1989)
                            • \(r_t = 100\ln(P_t/P_{t-1})\), in %
                            • Each regime has its own mean \(\mu\) and volatility \(\sigma\): a calm and a turbulent regime
                          • Regimes follow a Markov chain: \(p_{ij} = P(s_t = j \mid s_{t-1} = i)\)
                            • Expected duration of regime \(i\): \(1/(1 - p_{ii})\)
                          • Estimation by maximum likelihood with the Hamilton filter
                            • The filter updates \(P(s_t = j \mid r_1, \dots, r_t)\) week by week
                          • Filtered probability: returns up to \(t\) for given parameters
                          • Smoothed probability: the whole sample (hindsight)
                            • Our parameters are estimated on the full sample, so our filtered probabilities are only pseudo real time; genuine real time needs parameters re-estimated with data up to \(t\)

                            • Non-standard null distribution (Appendix) Hansen (1992)
                              • \(p_{11}, p_{22}\) are not identified under \(H_0\), so LR is not \(\chi^2\)
                            • Parametric bootstrap
                              • Simulate from the fitted \(H_0\) model, refit both models, recompute LR; \(B = 199\) replications, so the smallest attainable \(p\) is \(1/(B + 1) = 0.005\)
                              • \(p = (1 + \#\{\mathrm{LR}^* \ge \mathrm{LR}\})/(B + 1)\); \(\#\{\cdot\}\): number of bootstrap values \(\mathrm{LR}^*\) at least as large as the observed LR

                            How Many Regimes? A Bootstrap LR Test (2/3)

                            Slide 53 in the PDF
                              Question for the room Bitcoin, one against two regimes: none of the first 99 bootstrap replications reaches the observed LR, 123.2. What p-value does the rule p = (1 + number of exceedances)/(B + 1) report?
                              Answer

                              p = (1 + 0)/(99 + 1) = 0.010. The option 0 ignores the correction; 0.005 is the smallest possible value with 199 replications. Observing no exceedances does not establish that the tail probability is zero. Under H₀ the transition probabilities p₁₁ and p₂₂ are not identified, so LR does not follow a χ² distribution: with 199 replications, the 95% bootstrap critical value is 11.8, against 9.5 from χ²₄.

                                    Levels Explode, Log Prices Accelerate

                                    Slide 78 in the PDF
                                    • Theory: the bubble is explosive in levels, \(\E_t[B_{t+1}] = (1 + r)B_t\)
                                      • The test regresses the log price
                                      • A pure bubble path \(B_t = B_0(1 + r)^t\) has a constant log growth \(\ln(1 + r)\): \(\Delta y_t\) does not depend on \(y_{t-1}\), so \(b = 0\)
                                      • What \(b > 0\) detects: a rising growth rate of \(\ln P_t\), which occurs while the bubble share of \(P_t = F_t + B_t\) grows Appendix
                                    • Toy example (\(1 + r = 2\)): \(F = 100\) constant, \(B = 1, 2, 4, 8\), so \(P = 101, 102, 104, 108\)
                                      • Bubble share of the price: \(1.0\%\), \(2.0\%\), \(3.8\%\), \(7.4\%\)
                                      • Log increments: \(\ln(102/101) = 0.0099\), \(\ln(104/102) = 0.0194\), \(\ln(108/104) = 0.0377\)
                                      • They rise with \(y_{t-1} = \ln P_{t-1}\), so the fitted \(b > 0\); the bubble alone, \(B_t = 2^t\), has every increment equal to \(\ln 2 = 0.693\), so \(b = 0\)
                                    • Check: add \(B = 16\), so \(P = 116\)
                                      • Is the next increment larger?
                                      • Yes, \(\ln(116/108) = 0.0715\); once \(B \gg F\) the increments approach \(\ln 2\) and \(b\) falls back towards 0: windows that catch the transition have the power

                                    Transversality: Where the Bubble Term Comes From

                                    Slide 99 in the PDF
                                    • Substitute the pricing equation into itself \(h\) times (law of iterated expectations)
                                      • \(P_t = \sum_{i=1}^{h} \dfrac{\E_t[D_{t+i}]}{(1 + r)^i} + \dfrac{\E_t[P_{t+h}]}{(1 + r)^h}\)

                                      • The condition that the discounted bubble is a martingale
                                    • The condition \(B_t \ge 0\) rules out a bubble that starts from zero Diba & Grossman (1988)
                                      • A bubble that is zero today stays zero

                                    Limit Theory (1): the Null Distribution

                                    Slide 103 in the PDF
                                    • Null model: \(y_t = d\,T^{-\eta} + y_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t\), \(\eta > 1/2\), \(\varepsilon_t\) iid \((0, \sigma^2)\) Phillips, Shi & Yu (2015a)
                                      • A random walk with an asymptotically negligible drift \(d\,T^{-\eta}\) (\(d\): a constant)
                                    • Limit (PSY, Theorem 1, eq. 5), by the functional central limit theorem and continuous mapping
                                      • \(\mathrm{ADF}_{r_1}^{r_2} \Rightarrow \dfrac{\frac12 r_w\left[W(r_2)^2 - W(r_1)^2 - r_w\right] - \int_{r_1}^{r_2} W\,dr\,\left[W(r_2) - W(r_1)\right]}{r_w^{1/2}\left\{r_w \int_{r_1}^{r_2} W^2 dr - \left(\int_{r_1}^{r_2} W\,dr\right)^2\right\}^{1/2}}\)

                                      • SADF and GSADF are suprema of the same functional: their critical values depend only on \(r_0\) and the deterministic terms
                                    • \(r_1 = 0\), \(r_2 = 1\): the Dickey–Fuller limit with a constant
                                      • Its 95% quantile is negative (simulated: \(-0.06\)), far from \(1.645\)
                                      • The drift and intercept specification changes the limit and the size Phillips, Shi & Yu (2014)

                                    Limit Theory (2): Mildly Explosive Alternatives

                                    Slide 104 in the PDF
                                    • Alternative: \(y_t = \rho_T\,y_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t\) with \(\rho_T = 1 + c/k_T\), \(c > 0\), \(k_T \to \infty\), \(k_T = o(T)\)
                                      • PSY use \(k_T = T^{\alpha}\), \(\alpha \in (0, 1)\)
                                      • \(c\): how explosive; \(k_T\): the speed at which the root approaches 1; \(k_T = o(T)\): \(k_T/T \to 0\)
                                      • Closer to 1 than any fixed explosive root, farther than a local-to-unity root \(1 + c/T\): the “mild” speed of real bubbles

                                    Estimation: Hamilton Filter, Kim Smoother, EM

                                    Slide 111 in the PDF
                                    • Log-likelihood of \(\theta\) (all \(\mu_j\), \(\sigma_j\), \(p_{ij}\)), by prediction and update Hamilton (1989)
                                      • \(\ell(\theta) = \sum_t \ln \sum_j P(s_t = j \mid \mathcal F_{t-1})\,\varphi(r_t; \mu_j, \sigma_j^2)\)
                                      • \(\mathcal F_t\): returns up to \(t\); \(\varphi(x; \mu, \sigma^2)\): the Normal density with mean \(\mu\) and variance \(\sigma^2\)
                                    • Kim smoother, backwards Kim (1994)
                                      • \(P(s_t = j \mid \mathcal F_T) = P(s_t = j \mid \mathcal F_t) \sum_k p_{jk}\,\dfrac{P(s_{t+1} = k \mid \mathcal F_T)}{P(s_{t+1} = k \mid \mathcal F_t)}\)
                                    • EM algorithm: the M-step is a weighted average Hamilton (1990)
                                      • Smoothed \(\xi_{tj} = P(s_t = j \mid \mathcal F_T)\), \(\xi_{t,ij} = P(s_{t-1} = i, s_t = j \mid \mathcal F_T)\)
                                      • \(\hat\mu_j = \dfrac{\sum_t \xi_{tj} r_t}{\sum_t \xi_{tj}}\), \(\ \hat\sigma_j^2 = \dfrac{\sum_t \xi_{tj}(r_t - \hat\mu_j)^2}{\sum_t \xi_{tj}}\), \(\ \hat p_{ij} = \dfrac{\sum_{t \ge 2} \xi_{t,ij}}{\sum_{t \ge 2} \xi_{t-1,i}}\)
                                    • Unbounded likelihood (\(\sigma_j \to 0\) on one return) and several local maxima
                                      • 20 random sets of starting values, then the full optimisation; if LR \(< 0\), a refit with more starts
                                      • No penalty: a \(\hat p_{ij} < 10^{-4}\) or a \(\hat\sigma_j\) below 5% of the sample volatility flags a boundary maximum

                                    What the Researcher Must Check

                                    Slide 120 in the PDF
                                    • Full-sample information in date-stamping
                                      • Critical values, lag order or detrending fitted on the whole sample
                                      • Test: cut the sample at \(t\) and recompute; the flag at \(t\) must not change
                                    • Wrong tail
                                      • Left-tail ADF critical values (about \(-2.86\)) used against an explosive alternative
                                      • Test: on simulated random walks the right-tailed test must reject in about 5% of the paths
                                    • Changing volatility
                                      • Test: wild bootstrap critical values; for Bitcoin \(2.82\) instead of \(2.27\)
                                    • Multiplicity: many dates, assets and windows
                                      • Test: run the rule on simulated random walks and count paths with at least one alarm
                                    • Overlapping outcomes: Fisher's exact or \(\chi^2\) tests overstate significance
                                      • Test: block bootstrap or circular rotations of the alarm series; drop windows cut by the end of the data

                                    Mini-Case: What the Result Does and Does Not Say

                                    Slide 121 in the PDF
                                    • A large one-sided \(p\) is not evidence of no information
                                      • It only fails to support a higher crash probability after alarms
                                      • Both point estimates are negative: one-sided rotation \(p\) for \(\Delta < 0\), a lower crash rate after alarms, \(0.07\) (Bitcoin), \(0.02\) (Nasdaq 100)
                                      • Bitcoin: this \(0.07\) and the Newey–West one-sided \(0.024\) answer the same question under different null assumptions (stationary rotations vs a regression with HAC (Heteroskedasticity and Autocorrelation Consistent) errors)
                                      • Post-hoc, two-sided reading: alarms come in steady rises that did not end in a crash within 90 days
                                    • Assumption behind the rotation \(p\)-values
                                      • Under \(H_0\) every circular shift of the alarm series (at least 26 steps, ends joined) is as likely as the observed alignment
                                      • Stronger than equal hit rates: it needs stationary series and alarms independent of the crash process
                                      • Hence exploratory \(p\)-values; check their size by simulation under plausible null processes
