LeCun, Bengio & Hinton (2015) describe deep learning as learning representations of data through successive processing layers. The chapter asks whether such models forecast financial series better than the classical models of Chapters 5–9. It also tests time-series foundation models, available since 2024: networks pretrained on billions of observations from other series and used zero-shot, that is, with no estimation on the target series. Their pretraining corpora are dominated by non-financial series (energy, transport, weather, web).

A flexible model can only learn the signal that is in the data. In S&P 500 daily returns since 2000 (6,718 days), the lag-1 autocorrelation of returns is −0.099, and the 15 of 50 lags that fall outside the 95% band are small and of changing sign. The autocorrelation of absolute returns is 0.29 at lag 1 and 0.13 at lag 50, and all 50 lags fall outside the band. The signal is in the volatility, not in the mean.

The three tests follow this pattern. For returns, after the correction for multiple testing, no model beats the zero forecast on the S&P 500 and Bitcoin; on the S&P 500, TimesFM-2.5 is significantly worse than it (out-of-sample \(R^2\) −3.35%, Diebold–Mariano test, DM, \(p\) = 0.001). For the realised variance of SPY, over 979 test days, Chronos-2 has a mean QLIKE loss of 0.223 and log-HAR 0.226, a difference that is not significant (DM \(p\) = 0.539). In the left tail, raw Chronos-2 has too many VaR 1% breaches on the S&P 500 (1.67% of days, Kupiec \(p\) = 0.001), and filtered historical simulation (FHS) has the lowest FZ0 loss. The chapter therefore shows how to evaluate a new model: against the right benchmark for each target, after the release of its weights, and with the inference that fits the type of comparison.

Three Forecasting Targets

Slide 4 in the PDF
  • Returns \(r_t = 100\ln(P_t/P_{t-1})\) (conditional mean), \(P_t\) the close on day \(t\)
    • Very low signal-to-noise ratio: predictable patterns are traded away (Chapter 2)
    • Benchmark: the zero forecast
  • Volatility (conditional variance): persistent and clustered (Chapters 1 and 5), a signal to learn
    • Benchmark: log-HAR, log realised variance regressed on its daily, weekly and monthly averages (heterogeneous autoregressive, Test 2)
  • Tail risk: the shape of the lower tail, not only the centre (Chapter 7)
    • VaR 1% (value-at-risk): the daily loss exceeded with probability 1%
    • ES 2.5% (expected shortfall): the average loss on the worst 2.5% of days
    • Benchmark: FHS (filtered historical simulation)
    • FHS: the empirical quantile of returns standardised by a GARCH volatility (generalised autoregressive conditional heteroskedasticity, Chapter 5), times the forecast volatility

    Neural Networks for Sequences

    From the Multilayer Perceptron to Deep Learning (1/3)

    Slide 6 in the PDF
    • Question of the section: how can a network use information from distant days?
      • And why does a simple recurrent network fail?
    • MLP (multilayer perceptron, Chapter 13)
      \[ h^{(\ell)} = \varphi\left(W^{(\ell)} h^{(\ell-1)} + b^{(\ell)}\right), \qquad h^{(0)} = x \]
      • Each layer applies a non-linear function to a linear combination of the previous layer
      • \(x\): input vector; \(h^{(\ell)}\): output of layer \(\ell\)
      • \(W^{(\ell)}\), \(b^{(\ell)}\): weight matrix and bias vector of layer \(\ell\), estimated from the data
      • \(\varphi\): activation, applied element by element: ReLU (rectified linear unit) \(\max(0, z)\), \(\tanh\), or the sigmoid \(\sigma(z) = 1/(1+e^{-z}) \in (0, 1)\)
    • An MLP takes a fixed window of inputs
      • A sequence network must carry information from one step to the next

    From the Multilayer Perceptron to Deep Learning (2/3)

    Slide 7 in the PDF
    • Training: backpropagation (Rumelhart, Hinton & Williams (1986)) computes the gradient of the loss with respect to every weight
    • Update rule: Adam (adaptive moment estimation, Kingma & Ba (2015))
      • A gradient step normalised by exponential averages of past gradients and of their squares
    • Loss for point forecasts: the mean squared error (MSE), minimised by the conditional mean
    • Loss for a quantile of level \(u\): the pinball loss
      \[ \rho_u(y, q) = \left(u - \mathbf{1}\{y < q\}\right)(y - q) \]
      • \(y\): realised value; \(q\): quantile forecast
      • Asymmetric: values above \(q\) cost \(u\) per unit, values below cost \(1 - u\)
      • The expected loss is minimised by the true \(u\)-quantile

    From the Multilayer Perceptron to Deep Learning (3/3)

    Slide 8 in the PDF
    • Universal approximation (Hornik, Stinchcombe & White (1989))
      • A wide enough network approximates any continuous function
      • Silent on the sample size needed, the binding constraint with one financial series
      • Regularisation: early stopping, weight decay (a penalty on the size of the weights), ensembles over seeds
      • The validation block must follow the training block
    • 2024 Nobel Prize in Physics to Hopfield and Hinton
      • The methods: LeCun, Bengio & Hinton (2015)
      • In finance, Gu, Kelly & Xiu (2020) gain on a cross-section of thousands of stocks, not on one index
      • Fischer & Krauss (2018) apply LSTMs to S&P 500 constituents, forecasting which stocks beat the next-day cross-sectional median

    RNN and LSTM (2/2)

    Slide 10 in the PDF
    • Long short-term memory (LSTM, Hochreiter & Schmidhuber (1997)): gates decide what to forget, what to write and what to show
      \[ \begin{aligned} f_t, i_t, o_t &= \sigma(W_\bullet x_t + U_\bullet h_{t-1} + b_\bullet), & g_t &= \tanh(W_g x_t + U_g h_{t-1} + b_g)\\ c_t &= f_t \odot c_{t-1} + i_t \odot g_t, & h_t &= o_t \odot \tanh(c_t) \end{aligned} \]
    • Notation
      • \(W_\bullet\) weights the input and \(U_\bullet\) the previous state here, reversing the RNN letters
      • \(f_t\): forget gate, \(i_t\): input gate, \(o_t\): output gate; each has its own weights \(W_\bullet\), \(U_\bullet\), \(b_\bullet\), \(\bullet \in \{f, i, o\}\)
      • \(\sigma\): sigmoid; a gate near 0 blocks, near 1 lets through
      • \(g_t\): candidate new content; \(c_t\): cell state, the long-term memory
      • \(h_t\): output; \(\odot\): element-by-element product
    • GRU (gated recurrent unit): a lighter variant with two gates

    Vanishing Gradients: a Bound and a VAR(1) (1/2)

    Slide 11 in the PDF
    • Training needs the effect of old states on today's state
    • Backpropagation through time multiplies one Jacobian per step
      \[ \frac{\partial h_T}{\partial h_{T-k}} = \prod_{j=0}^{k-1} D_{T-j}\, W, \qquad D_t = \operatorname{diag}\!\left(\varphi^\prime(W h_{t-1} + U x_t + b)\right) \]
      • \(\partial h_T / \partial h_{T-k}\): the matrix of changes in \(h_T\) per unit change in the state \(k\) steps earlier
      • \(D_t\): diagonal matrix of the slopes \(\varphi^\prime\) of the activation at step \(t\)
    • Bound, by submultiplicativity \(\lVert AB\rVert_2 \le \lVert A\rVert_2\lVert B\rVert_2\) (Pascanu, Mikolov & Bengio (2013))
      \[ \Big\lVert \frac{\partial h_T}{\partial h_{T-k}} \Big\rVert_2 \le \left(\gamma_\varphi \lVert W\rVert_2\right)^k, \qquad \gamma_\varphi = \sup_z |\varphi^\prime(z)| \]
      • \(\lVert W\rVert_2\): spectral norm, the largest factor by which \(W\) stretches a vector
      • \(\gamma_\varphi\): the largest slope of the activation (\(= 1\) for \(\tanh\))
      • \(\gamma_\varphi \lVert W\rVert_2 < 1\): the gradient shrinks geometrically with \(k\), i.e. it vanishes
    • Scalar case: with \(|w| = 0.9\) the gradient falls to 0.12 after 20 steps and to 0.005 after 50 steps (Bengio, Simard & Frasconi (1994))
      • Remedies: clipping the gradient norm (explosion), gating as in the LSTM (vanishing)

    Vanishing Gradients: a Bound and a VAR(1) (2/2)

    Slide 12 in the PDF
    • Linearise around a fixed point \(h^*\) of the network
      \[ h_t - h^* \approx A\,(h_{t-1} - h^*) + D U x_t, \qquad A = D W \]
      • Fixed point: a state that reproduces itself when the input is zero
      • \(D\): the slopes of the activation at \(h^*\)
      • The hidden state follows a VAR(1) (vector autoregression) with coefficient matrix \(A\)
    • \(\partial h_T / \partial h_{T-k} = A^k\) is the impulse response of this VAR at horizon \(k\)
      • \(\rho(A)\): spectral radius, the largest absolute eigenvalue of \(A\)
      • \(\rho(A) < 1\): stable VAR, \(\lVert A^k\rVert \to 0\) geometrically as \(k \to \infty\)
      • At finite horizons \(\lVert A^k\rVert\) can grow transiently if \(A\) is not normal (\(A A^\top \ne A^\top A\))
      • \(\rho(A) > 1\): they explode
    • A stable RNN near its fixed point is a short-memory model, like any stationary VAR
      • Seminar 14, exercise A4
      • Details in the Appendix

    Why the LSTM Remembers

    Slide 13 in the PDF
    • Along the cell state the only multiplier is the forget gate
      • \(\partial c_T / \partial c_{T-k} = \prod_{j=0}^{k-1} f_{T-j}\) on the direct path
      • \(c_t = f_t \odot c_{t-1} + i_t \odot g_t\): an AR(1) with a random coefficient
    • Simplified scalar cell with a constant forget gate \(f\): an AR(1) with coefficient \(f\)
      • \(f = \sigma(b_f)\) when the gate inputs are zero; \(b_f\): its bias
      • With a white-noise input \(i_t g_t\) and a stationary start, \(\operatorname{Corr}(c_t, c_{t-k}) = f^k\) decays geometrically
      • With \(b_f = 0\), the forget gate is \(f = 0.5\), and after 50 steps \(0.5^{50} \approx 9 \times 10^{-16}\) of the memory is left
      • \(b_f = 3\): \(f = 0.953\), and \(0.953^{50} \approx 0.09\) of the memory is left after 50 steps
      • Half-life 14 steps (the \(k\) with \(f^k = \frac12\), \(k = \ln 0.5/\ln f\))
    • Long memory decays hyperbolically: \(\rho_k \sim C k^{2d-1}\)
      • With \(\rho_k\) the autocorrelation at lag \(k\)
      • \(d \in (0, \frac12)\) the memory parameter, \(C > 0\) a constant
      • S&P 500 absolute returns: the fitted geometric decay gives \(\rho_{150}\) = 0.027, against 0.059 observed (Appendix)

    Attention and Transformers

    Attention: Queries, Keys and Values (1/2)

    Slide 14 in the PDF
    • Question of the section: how can a network link today directly to a day 500 days back?
      • Without the product of Jacobians that makes the RNN gradient vanish
    • Each time step asks a question (query) and compares it with the key of every step it is allowed to see
      \[ \operatorname{Attention}(Q, K, V) = \operatorname{softmax}\!\left(\frac{Q K^\top}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right) V, \qquad Q = XW_Q,\; K = XW_K,\; V = XW_V \]
      • The answer is a weighted average of their values (Vaswani et al. (2017))
      • \(X\): the inputs, one row per time step
      • \(W_Q\), \(W_K\), \(W_V\): learned weight matrices
      • \(Q\), \(K\), \(V\): queries, keys and values

    Attention: Queries, Keys and Values (2/2)

    Slide 15 in the PDF
    • \(QK^\top\): the similarity score of every pair of steps
    • \(d_k\): length of a key vector
      • Dividing by \(\sqrt{d_k}\) keeps the scores from saturating the softmax
    • softmax turns each row of scores \(s_1, \dots, s_n\) into positive weights that sum to one
      \[ \operatorname{softmax}(s)_j = \frac{e^{s_j}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n} e^{s_i}} \]
    • No product of Jacobians over time: any past step is one hop away
    • Cost \(O(n^2)\): the work grows with the square of the number of inputs \(n\)

      Positions and Patches

      Slide 17 in the PDF
      • Positional encoding: attention treats its inputs as a set, so order is added
        • Sinusoids in the original Transformer; rotary embeddings in Chronos-2

      Time-Series Foundation Models

      What Is a Foundation Model?

      Slide 18 in the PDF
      • Question of the section: can a model pretrained on other series forecast a financial series it never saw in training?
      • Foundation model: a large network pretrained once on a very broad corpus, then used on new tasks
        • An idea borrowed from large language models (LLM)
      • Appeal for finance: one model for thousands of series, no estimation per series
      • Three families of models
        • Chronos (Ansari et al. (2024)): values become the tokens of a language model
        • TimesFM (Das et al. (2024)): a decoder-only Transformer that reads the series in patches
        • Moirai (Woo et al. (2024)): any number of variables and a mixture-distribution output (Appendix)
        • Moirai is not tested here because of its non-commercial licence

      Zero-Shot Forecasting: What Goes In and What Comes Out

      Slide 19 in the PDF
      • Zero-shot forecasting: the parameters \(\hat\theta\) are fixed in pretraining
        \[ \big(\hat q_{u_1, T+h}, \dots, \hat q_{u_K, T+h}\big)_{h = 1, \dots, H} = F_{\hat\theta}\big(y_{T-C+1}, \dots, y_T\big) \]
        • At time \(T\) the model receives only the recent history of the series (the context)
        • \(F_{\hat\theta}\): the pretrained network, the same for every series
        • \(C\): context length (512 days here); \(H\): forecast horizon
        • \(\hat q_{u_k, T+h}\): the \(u_k\)-quantile of the value at \(T+h\), so the output is a quantile grid, not only a point
      • Fine-tuning is possible but not used
        • Fine-tuning: further training on the target series
        • We test the "no estimation" promise itself
      • The specific risk: the target series may be part of the pretraining corpus (slide on leakage)

      Chronos: Time Series as a Language

      Slide 20 in the PDF
      • Ansari et al. (2024): turn values into tokens and train a language model with cross-entropy
        • The language model: T5, Text-to-Text Transfer Transformer
      • Why tokens: the language-model architecture stays unchanged
        • A distribution over tokens imposes no parametric shape (it can be skewed or multimodal)
      • Mean scaling and quantisation
        \[ \tilde x_t = \frac{x_t}{s}, \quad s = \frac{1}{C}\sum_{i=1}^{C} |x_i|, \qquad z_t = \arg\min_{j} |\tilde x_t - c_j|, \quad c_j = -15 + \frac{30\,(j-1)}{4{,}092} \]
        • \(s\): mean absolute value of the \(C\) values in the context
        • \(c_j\), \(j = 1, \dots, 4{,}093\): equally spaced bin centres in \([-15, 15]\), \(30/4{,}092 = 0.0073\) apart, plus special tokens
        • \(z_t\): the token of \(x_t\), the index of the nearest centre
        • Forecast: sample future tokens \(z\), map them back to values \(s\,c_z\), read quantiles from the samples
      • Pretraining corpus: public datasets (energy, transport, retail, weather, web, finance and economics)
        • Plus synthetic TSMixup series: random convex combinations of real series
        • And KernelSynth series: paths of Gaussian processes with randomly combined covariance kernels
      • Chronos (T5): 8 to 710 million parameters
        • Chronos-Bolt, tested here: 9 to 205 million

      From a Quantile Grid to VaR and ES (1/2)

      Slide 22 in the PDF
      • The model returns quantiles \(q_u\) at levels \(u_1 < \dots < u_K\): \(P(Y \le q_u) = u\)
        • \(Y\): the future value; \(K\): the number of levels
      • Why a grid: the same quantiles give the point forecast, the volatility and the tail risk
        • So one model serves all three tests
      • Point forecasts from the grid
        • the median \(q_{0.5}\), or the mean \(\int_0^1 q_u\,du\), the average of all quantiles
        • for any function \(g\) with a finite mean: \(E[g(Y)] = \int_0^1 g(q_u)\,du\), because \(Y\) has the same distribution as \(q_U\) with \(U\) uniform (e.g. \(g = \exp\) when \(Y\) is a log variance)
        • If \(g\) is increasing, \(g(q_u)\) is also the \(u\)-quantile of \(g(Y)\)
        • Check: \(Y\) uniform on \((-1, 1)\), \(q_u = 2u - 1\), \(g(y) = y^2\): \(\int_0^1 (2u-1)^2\,du = 1/3 = E[Y^2]\), although \((2u-1)^2\) is not a quantile of \(Y^2\) (it falls, then rises in \(u\))

      From a Quantile Grid to VaR and ES (2/2)

      Slide 23 in the PDF
      • Tail risk at level \(\alpha\) (Chapter 7), for a return \(Y\)
        \[ \mathrm{VaR}_\alpha = -q_\alpha, \qquad \mathrm{ES}_\alpha = -\frac{1}{\alpha}\int_0^\alpha q_u\,du \]
      • Below the lowest level the grid is silent
        • Holding \(q_u\) at \(q_{0.01}\) understates ES
        • Interpolation can err either way (Seminar 14, exercises A1 and A7)
      • Chronos-Bolt and TimesFM stop at 10%: a "VaR 1%" request returns the 10% quantile
      • The chapter's rule: linear interpolation between levels
        • \(q_u\) held flat below the lowest level
        • The worked example is in Further Topics

      Pretraining Leakage and Contamination

      Slide 24 in the PDF
      • A test is out of sample only if the test period was never seen during training
      • It can be “seen” in four ways
        • (i) the same series is in the pretraining corpus
        • (ii) correlated series carry the same shocks

      Judging the Return Forecasts (1/2)

      Slide 26 in the PDF
      • Benchmark: the zero forecast; out-of-sample
        \[ R^2 = 1 - \frac{\sum_t (r_t - \hat r_t)^2}{\sum_t r_t^2} \]
        • \(r_t\): realised return; \(\hat r_t\): forecast from data up to \(t-1\)
        • \(R^2 > 0\): smaller squared errors than the zero forecast
      • Why zero rather than the historical mean: the estimated mean adds estimation noise
        • Zero is the benchmark of Gu, Kelly & Xiu (2020), the historical mean that of Campbell & Thompson (2008)
      • Grid means (\(\int_0^1 q_u\,du\)) do worse still
        • On the S&P 500, \(R^2\) is \(-\)0.77% (Chronos-2) and \(-\)3.42% (TimesFM-2.5)

      Judging the Return Forecasts (2/2)

      Slide 27 in the PDF
      • Block-bootstrap confidence interval (CI) for \(R^2\)
        • Rebuild a test period of the same length from blocks of 20 consecutive days drawn with replacement, 1,000 times
        • Keep the 2.5% and 97.5% percentiles
        • The blocks keep volatility clusters together
      • Diebold–Mariano (DM, Diebold & Mariano (1995)) test on squared errors
        \[ \mathrm{DM} = \frac{\bar d}{\sqrt{\hat V_{NW}/n}}, \qquad d_t = (r_t - \hat r_t)^2 - r_t^2 \]
        • \(\bar d\): mean of \(d_t\) over the \(n\) forecasts
        • \(\mathrm{DM} > 0\): the model has larger errors than the zero forecast
        • \(\hat V_{NW}\): long-run variance of \(d_t\), estimated by Newey–West, a HAC (heteroskedasticity and autocorrelation consistent) estimator
        • Under \(H_0\colon E[d_t] = 0\), DM is approximately standard Normal
        • The approximation needs a finite, non-zero long-run variance of \(d_t\) and weak dependence
        • Here \(d_t = \hat r_t^2 - 2 r_t \hat r_t\): the moments of \(r_t \hat r_t\) matter, not necessarily \(E[r_t^4]\)

        Nested Models: Clark–West against DM

        Slide 29 in the PDF
        • The problem: zero is nested in the historical mean and AR(1)
          • Under \(H_0\) the larger model only adds estimation noise, so DM rejects too rarely
        • Clark–West (Clark & West (2007)): add back the noise term and test its mean, one-sided HAC \(t\)-test
          \[ \tilde d_t = e_{0,t}^2 - \left[e_{1,t}^2 - (\hat r_{0,t} - \hat r_{1,t})^2\right] \]
          • \(\hat r_{0,t}\): zero forecast (small model); \(\hat r_{1,t}\): forecast of the large model; \(e_{0,t}\), \(e_{1,t}\): their errors \(r_t - \hat r_{\cdot,t}\)
          • \((\hat r_{0,t} - \hat r_{1,t})^2\): the cost of estimating coefficients that are zero under \(H_0\)
          • \(H_0\): \(E[\tilde d_t] = 0\); a large positive \(t\): the large model adds information
        • One-sided \(p\)-values, DM against Clark–West
          • both tests need a finite, non-zero long-run variance of the differential (for CW against the zero forecast, \(\tilde d_t = 2 r_t \hat r_t\))
          • historical mean, S&P 500: DM 0.048, Clark–West 0.007
          • historical mean, BET: DM 0.041, Clark–West 0.003
          • AR(1), S&P 500: DM 0.320, Clark–West 0.055 (the BET AR(1) and rolling windows: Appendix)

        Slide 30 in the PDF

        Realised Variance, HAR and the QLIKE Loss (1/2)

        Slide 33 in the PDF
        • Realised variance (RV, Andersen et al. (2003)): sum of squared 5-minute log returns within the trading day
          \[ \mathrm{RV}_t = \sum_{j=1}^{78} r_{t,j}^2 \]
          • \(r_{t,j}\): log return of the \(j\)-th 5-minute interval of day \(t\)
          • \(r_{t,1} = \ln(C_{t,1}/O_{t,1})\): open and close of the 09:30 bar; then \(r_{t,j} = \ln(C_{t,j}/C_{t,j-1})\)
          • 78 intervals in the regular SPY session (09:30–16:00, New York time); days without the full grid are dropped
          • Average volatility 12.1% p.a.
        • HAR (Corsi (2009)): today's RV regressed on yesterday's, the last week's and the last month's averages
          \[ \mathrm{RV}_t = \beta_0 + \beta_d \mathrm{RV}_{t-1} + \beta_w \overline{\mathrm{RV}}_{t-5:t-1} + \beta_m \overline{\mathrm{RV}}_{t-22:t-1} + \varepsilon_t \]
          • \(\overline{\mathrm{RV}}_{t-5:t-1}\), \(\overline{\mathrm{RV}}_{t-22:t-1}\): averages over the last 5 and 22 trading days
          • \(\beta_d\), \(\beta_w\), \(\beta_m\): daily, weekly and monthly weights

        Realised Variance, HAR and the QLIKE Loss (2/2)

        Slide 34 in the PDF
        • log-HAR: the same regression on \(\log \mathrm{RV}\); forecast \(\exp(\hat y + \hat s^2/2)\)
          • \(\hat y\): fitted log RV; \(\hat s^2\): variance of the log residuals
          • The factor \(e^{\hat s^2/2}\) turns a log forecast into the mean of RV if the log error is conditionally Normal
        • QLIKE (quasi-likelihood) loss (Patton (2011)) of a variance forecast \(\hat h_t\)
          \[ L = \frac{\mathrm{RV}_t}{\hat h_t} - \log\frac{\mathrm{RV}_t}{\hat h_t} - 1 \]
          • \(L \ge 0\), and \(L = 0\) when \(\hat h_t = \mathrm{RV}_t\)
          • Under-forecasts cost more than over-forecasts of the same size
          • Minimised by the conditional mean
          • Ranks forecasts as the true variance \(\sigma_t^2\) would when RV is a conditionally unbiased proxy, \(E[\mathrm{RV}_t \mid \mathcal{F}_{t-1}] = \sigma_t^2\), with \(\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\) the information up to \(t-1\) (so does MSE)

            QLIKE Losses of Eight Volatility Models (3/3)

            Slide 39 in the PDF
            • Giacomini–White test (Giacomini & White (2006)): can the loss differential \(d_t\) be predicted from yesterday's information?
              \[ \mathrm{GW} = n\, \bar Z^\top \hat\Omega^{-1} \bar Z \;\to\; \chi^2_2, \qquad Z_t = (1,\ d_{t-1})^\top d_t \]
              • \(d_t\): QLIKE loss of the model minus that of log-HAR
              • Instruments 1 and \(d_{t-1}\), known at \(t-1\)
              • \(H_0\): \(E[d_t] = 0\) and \(E[d_{t-1} d_t] = 0\) jointly
              • \(\bar Z\): mean of \(Z_t\); \(n\): number of forecasts
              • \(\hat\Omega\): long-run variance of \(Z_t\) (HAC, Bartlett kernel, 6 lags), since \(E[Z_t] = 0\) does not rule out autocorrelation in \(Z_t\)
            • Result: \(p\) = 0.828 (Chronos-2) and 0.812 (TimesFM-2.5): no conditional difference
              • The test needs a finite, non-singular long-run variance of \(Z_t\)
            • No difference detected is not proof of equality (power is discussed in the AI section)

            Scoring (VaR, ES) Pairs: the FZ0 Loss

            Slide 43 in the PDF
            • A functional is elicitable if some loss is minimised, in expectation, by its true value
              • ES alone is not, the pair (VaR, ES) is (Fissler & Ziegel (2016))
            • FZ0 loss of a forecast pair, with the daily loss \(L_t = -r_t\)
              \[ \mathrm{FZ0} = \frac{\mathbf{1}\{L_t > v\}(L_t - v)}{\alpha\, e} + \frac{v}{e} + \log e - 1 \]
              • FZ0: the zero-homogeneous Fissler–Ziegel loss, Patton, Ziegel & Chen (2019)
              • \(v\): VaR forecast; \(e > 0\): ES forecast; \(\alpha\): tail level; \(\mathbf{1}\{L_t > v\}\): 1 on a breach day
              • Lower is better: the first term penalises breaches, \(\log e\) penalises wide forecasts

                  • Berkowitz test (Berkowitz (2001)): transform \(z_t = \Phi^{-1}(u_t)\), standard Normal if the forecast is correct

                    Recalibrating Quantiles: Adaptive Conformal Inference

                    Slide 51 in the PDF
                    • Idea: shift the model's quantile by the error it made recently
                      • And adapt the level after each breach
                    • Score \(E_t = \hat q_{\alpha,t} - r_t\)
                      • Corrected quantile \(\hat q_{\alpha,t} - \hat Q_{1-\alpha_t}\)
                      • \(\hat q_{\alpha,t}\): the model's \(\alpha\)-quantile for day \(t\); \(E_t > 0\): the return fell below it
                      • \(\hat Q_{1-\alpha_t}\): the empirical \((1-\alpha_t)\) quantile of the last 250 scores
                    • Adaptive level of adaptive conformal inference (ACI, Gibbs & Candès (2021))
                      \[ \alpha_{t+1} = \alpha_t + \gamma(\alpha - \mathrm{err}_t), \qquad \mathrm{err}_t = \mathbf{1}\{r_t < \hat q_{\alpha,t} - \hat Q_{1-\alpha_t}\}, \qquad \alpha_1 = \alpha \]
                      • \(\alpha\): target level (1%); \(\mathrm{err}_t = 1\) on a breach
                      • \(\gamma > 0\): step size, here 0.001; \(\gamma = 0\): rolling calibration at a fixed level (the correction is recomputed daily from the last 250 scores), not standard split conformal
                      • After a breach \(\alpha_t\) falls (the next VaR is wider); after a calm day it rises slowly

                      Slide 56 in the PDF

                        \[ S_{i,t} = \big(\sin(\gamma\,\omega_i' G_t),\ \cos(\gamma\,\omega_i' G_t)\big)', \qquad \omega_i \sim N(0, I), \quad \gamma = 2 \]
                        • Each feature is a sine or cosine of a random combination of the predictors

                        • A wide two-layer network whose first-layer weights are random; only the output weights \(\beta\) are estimated
                        • \(P\) counts columns: \(P/2\) vectors \(\omega_i\), each giving a sine and a cosine (so \(P = 12\,000\) uses 6 000 vectors \(\omega_i\))

                            Attention in Numbers: Three Steps and a Causal Mask

                            Slide 75 in the PDF
                            • Three time steps, \(d_k = 1\): queries \(q = (1, 0, 2)\), keys \(k = (0.5, 1, -1)\), values \(v = (1, 2, 3)\)
                            • Step 3: scores \(q_3 k_j = (1, 2, -2)\)
                              • Softmax weights \(e^{s_j}/\sum_i e^{s_i} = (2.718, 7.389, 0.135)/10.243 = (0.265, 0.721, 0.013)\)
                              • Output \(0.265 \cdot 1 + 0.721 \cdot 2 + 0.013 \cdot 3 = 1.75\): step 3 draws mostly on step 2, whose key matches its query best
                            • Causal mask: in a forecaster that predicts every next step, step \(t\) may use only steps \(j \le t\)
                              \[ \operatorname{softmax}\!\left(\frac{Q K^\top}{\sqrt{d_k}} + M\right) V \]
                              • Equivalently add \(M_{tj} = 0\) for \(j \le t\), \(M_{tj} = -\infty\) for \(j > t\)
                              • Step 1 then puts weight 1 on itself (output 1)
                              • Without the mask its scores \((0.5, 1, -1)\) give weights \((0.35, 0.57, 0.08)\): 57% from step 2, a later day
                              • With the mask, step 2 (\(q_2 = 0\), equal scores on steps 1 and 2) outputs \((1 + 2)/2 = 1.5\)
                            • Every value in a 512-day context is already observed at the forecast origin
                              • So a model reading only the context may attend in both directions
                              • The mask matters where the model is trained to predict later positions from earlier ones

                            Forecasting Transformers: the Transformer Block

                            Slide 78 in the PDF
                            • Transformer block (Vaswani et al. (2017)): multi-head attention, then a small MLP applied to each position separately
                              \[ Z = \mathrm{LN}\big(X + \mathrm{MHA}(X)\big), \qquad H = \mathrm{LN}\big(Z + \mathrm{MLP}(Z)\big) \]
                              • Each part has a skip connection and a normalisation
                              • \(\mathrm{MHA}(X) = [\mathrm{head}_1, \dots, \mathrm{head}_m]\,W_O\) (multi-head attention): \(m\) attentions in parallel, each with its own \(W_Q\), \(W_K\), \(W_V\), concatenated and projected by \(W_O\)
                              • \(\mathrm{LN}\) (layer normalisation): each row is standardised, then rescaled by learned parameters
                              • \(X + \mathrm{MHA}(X)\): the skip connection, which lets the gradient pass straight through the block
                              • A model stacks several blocks: \(H\) is the input of the next block

                            Forecasting Transformers: Architectures and Losses

                            Slide 79 in the PDF
                            • Three ways to assemble the blocks
                              • Encoder: attention in both directions inside the context; Chronos-2 is encoder-only and returns the quantile grid in one pass (Ansari et al. (2025))
                              • Decoder-only: causal mask, trained to predict the next patch at every position: TimesFM (Das et al. (2024))
                              • Encoder–decoder (T5, used by the original Chronos): the encoder reads the context; the decoder writes the forecast one token at a time
                            • Cross-entropy (original Chronos): predicting the next token is a classification over 4,093 bins
                              \[ \mathcal{L}(\theta) = -\sum_{h=1}^{H} \log \hat p_\theta\big(z_{C+h} \mid z_1, \dots, z_{C+h-1}\big) \]

                              • \(\hat\mu\), \(\hat\sigma\): the mean and standard deviation of the context
                              • \(\sinh^{-1}\) grows like a logarithm for large values and so damps outliers

                            Norms, Spectral Radius and Explosion

                            Slide 96 in the PDF
                            • \(\gamma_\varphi \lVert W\rVert_2 < 1\) is sufficient for geometric vanishing, so \(\gamma_\varphi \lVert W\rVert_2 \ge 1\) is necessary for explosion (Pascanu, Mikolov & Bengio (2013))
                            • Spectral radius \(\rho\) (largest absolute eigenvalue)
                              • In a linear RNN, \(\rho(W) > 1\) is necessary for exponential explosion
                              • \(\rho(W) = 1\) with a Jordan block still gives polynomial growth
                              • With \(\varphi\) the local Jacobians \(D_t W\) matter: \(\rho(W) = 0\) yet \(\rho(DW) > 1\) is possible
                            • Stable VAR: impulse responses and gradients decay asymptotically like \(\rho(A)^k\)
                              • At finite horizons \(\lVert A^k\rVert_2\) can exceed 1: \(A = \left(\begin{smallmatrix} 0.5 & 4 \\ 0 & 0.5 \end{smallmatrix}\right)\) has \(\rho(A) = 0.5\), yet \(\lVert A^2\rVert_2 \approx 4.0\)
                              • Up to a factor \(k^{m-1}\) if \(A\) has a Jordan block of size \(m\)
                              • Jordan block: a repeated eigenvalue without a full set of eigenvectors
                            • \(DUx_t\) is an innovation only if the inputs \(x_t\) are centred white noise
                              • Long-memory inputs would pass through the network

                            Multiple Testing (1/2): Holm

                            Slide 104 in the PDF
                            • Holm: order the \(M\) \(p\)-values, \(p_{(1)} \le \dots \le p_{(M)}\)
                              • Reject while \(p_{(j)} < 0.05/(M - j + 1)\), stop at the first non-rejection
                            • The adjusted \(p_{(j)}\) is \(\max_{i \le j} \min\{1, (M - i + 1)\,p_{(i)}\}\)
                              • Toy example, \(M = 3\), \(p = 0.01, 0.02, 0.04\)
                              • Thresholds \(0.05/3 = 0.0167\), \(0.05/2 = 0.025\), \(0.05\): all three rejected
                              • Bonferroni (\(0.0167\) for all) rejects only the first
                              • Adjusted \(p\): 0.03, 0.04, 0.04
                              • Chapter: the BET LSTM has the smallest of the 18 one-sided DM \(p\)-values, 0.0072
                              • So its adjusted \(p\) is 18 \(\times\) 0.0072 = 0.130

                            Multiple Testing (2/2): Romano–Wolf and SPA

                            Slide 105 in the PDF
                            • Romano–Wolf: statistics \(t_k = \sqrt{T}\,\bar d_k/\hat\omega_k\), ordered \(t_{(1)} \ge \dots \ge t_{(K)}\)
                              \[ p^{RW}_{(j)} = \max_{i \le j}\, P^*\Big(\max_{k \in \mathcal{A}_i} t_k^* \ge t_{(i)}\Big), \qquad t_k^* = \frac{\sqrt{T}\,(\bar d_k^* - \bar d_k)}{\hat\omega_k} \]
                              • With \(\bar d_k\) the mean loss gain of model \(k\) over the zero forecast and \(\hat\omega_k\) its HAC standard deviation
                              • \(\mathcal{A}_i\): the hypotheses not yet rejected at step \(i\), i.e. \((i), \dots, (K)\)
                              • \(P^*\), \(\bar d_k^*\): probability and mean under the stationary bootstrap
                              • The threshold comes from the maximum of correlated statistics, so the procedure is less conservative than Holm for forecasts of one market
                            • SPA: one joint test of \(H_0\): no model has a lower expected loss than the benchmark
                              • Statistic \(\max\big(0, \max_k t_k\big)\)
                              • \(p\)-value from a stationary bootstrap
                              • Much worse models (\(t_k \le -\sqrt{2\ln\ln T}\)) are not recentred at zero, so they do not dilute the test
                            • Which question each answers
                              • Holm and Romano–Wolf: which comparisons are significant with at most a 5% chance of any false rejection
                              • SPA: whether any model beats the benchmark
