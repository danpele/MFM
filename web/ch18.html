Systemic risk is the risk that the distress of one or more institutions affects the financial system as a whole and, through it, the real economy. It is an externality: a bank does not pay for the losses its failure imposes on others. The chapter uses daily prices of thirteen banks from the US, Europe and Romania, from January 2010 to 18 September 2026. The declines of the three banking systems are simultaneous, in the euro-area crisis, the COVID-19 pandemic and the 2023 bank failures, and the maximum drawdown of the bank portfolio is 51% in the US, 61% in Europe and 47% in Romania.

The usual risk measure of a bank, Value-at-Risk (VaR), looks at the bank in isolation. Adrian & Brunnermeier (2016) measure instead its contribution to the system: ΔCoVaR 1% is the additional loss of the system when the bank moves from its median state to its own VaR. Across the eleven international banks, the Spearman rank correlation between VaR 1% and ΔCoVaR 1% is 0.13. Deutsche Bank and ING have the largest VaR, 7.19% and 7.16%, but only an average ΔCoVaR, while JPMorgan has one of the smallest VaR, 4.83%, but one of the largest ΔCoVaR. Regulating each bank by its own VaR therefore ignores its contribution to the system; with eleven banks and overlapping confidence intervals, however, the correlation remains very uncertain.

The systemic measures do not produce a single ranking either. The rank correlation between MES 5% (Marginal Expected Shortfall), the average loss of the bank on the worst 5% of market days, and ΔCoVaR 1% is −0.04, and the one between ΔCoVaR and the net volatility spillover is also −0.04; by contrast, VaR and MES have a rank correlation of 0.88. The chapter treats in turn the three families of measures, exposure (MES and SRISK), contribution (CoVaR) and connectedness (spillover networks and the Financial Risk Meter), and then stress tests and climate risk.

Systemic Risk: from One Bank to the System

Systemic risk concerns the financial system rather than a single institution. The chapter asks when the distress of one bank becomes a problem for the system, and measures the answer in three ways:

  1. The exposure of a bank to a market crash;
  2. Its contribution to the tail loss of the system;
  3. The network of links through which shocks travel.

The first section defines the concept, recalls two bank runs and the channels of contagion, and fixes the data and the sign conventions used throughout.

The Idea of the Chapter

Slide 2 in the PDF

Systemic risk is the risk that the distress of one or several institutions impairs the financial system as a whole and, through it, the real economy. It is an externality: a bank does not pay for the losses that its failure imposes on others, so its private choice of capital and risk does not account for them. A measure of systemic risk must therefore look beyond the bank's own loss distribution.

The survey of Benoit, Colliard, Hurlin & Pérignon (2017) groups the measures into three families, which organise the chapter:

  1. Exposure asks what the bank loses when the market crashes; the Marginal Expected Shortfall (MES) gives its average loss on crash days and SRISK its capital shortfall in a crisis.
  2. Contribution asks how much worse the system is when the bank is in distress; it is measured by ΔCoVaR, where CoVaR is the Value-at-Risk (VaR) of the system conditional on the bank.
  3. Connectedness asks through which links shocks travel, and is measured with spillover and tail networks.

Through Which Channels Does Contagion Travel?

Slide 5 in the PDF

The distress of one bank becomes systemic through three channels:

  1. In a funding run, depositors and lenders withdraw from any bank that looks similar to the one in trouble, so the shock spreads through beliefs rather than balance sheets.
  2. With common exposures and fire sales, banks that hold similar assets are hit together, and the forced selling of one bank lowers the prices for every holder.
  3. Through counterparty links, one bank's liability is another bank's asset, so a default passes losses directly to its creditors.

Market-based measures, such as those of the chapter, see the combined effect of the three channels in stock prices, without separating them.

In brief

  • One bank's distress becomes systemic through three channels
    • Funding runs: depositors and lenders withdraw from any bank that looks similar
    • Common exposures and fire sales: forced selling lowers prices for every holder
    • Counterparty links: one bank's liability is another bank's asset, so a default passes losses to its creditors

Losses as Positive Numbers and the System Portfolio

Slide 7 in the PDF

For a return \(X\) the loss is \(L = -X\), and the level \(\alpha\) is a tail probability, as in Chapter 7. The Value-at-Risk is

\[\mathrm{VaR}_\alpha(X) = -q_\alpha(X),\]
(18.1)

where \(q_\alpha\) is the \(\alpha\)-quantile of \(X\). The chapter writes VaR 1%, CoVaR 1% and MES 5%, all positive when they are losses.

The system return \(X^{sys}\) of a bank is the equal-weighted portfolio of the other banks of the same region, \(X^{sys} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_j X_j\). With log returns, this average is a first-order approximation of the log return of the daily rebalanced portfolio. CoVaR is stated for this aggregate, while MES conditions on the regional market index.

    Marginal Expected Shortfall and the Euler Property

    Slide 10 in the PDF

    The Marginal Expected Shortfall of bank \(i\) at level \(\alpha\) (Acharya, Pedersen, Philippon & Richardson, 2017) is

    \[\mathrm{MES}_{i,\alpha} = -\mathbb{E}\big[X_i \mid X_m \le q_\alpha(X_m)\big],\]
    (18.2)

    the average loss of the bank on the worst \(\alpha\) share of days for the market \(m\). The name "marginal" comes from the Euler property: if \(m = \sum_i w_i X_i\) is the portfolio itself, then the Expected Shortfall of the portfolio decomposes as \(\mathrm{ES}_\alpha(X_m) = \sum_i w_i\,\mathrm{MES}_{i,\alpha}\). This is the Euler allocation, proved in the Appendix.

    The market indices of the chapter are not portfolios of these 13 banks, so here MES measures exposure, not a share of the index ES. The historical estimate averages \(-X_i\) over the days with \(X_m \le \hat q_\alpha(X_m)\); with \(\alpha = 5\%\) and 3,961 days, these are about 200 days.

      From MES to a Crisis: LRMES

      Slide 13 in the PDF

      LRMES (Long-Run MES) is the expected fall of the bank's market equity in a crisis,

      \[\mathrm{LRMES}_{i,t} = -\mathbb{E}_t\big[R_{i,t+1:t+h} \mid R_{m,t+1:t+h} < C\big],\]
      (18.3)

      where \(R\) are the \(h\)-day arithmetic returns of the bank and the market. A crisis is a market fall below \(C = -40\%\) over six months (Acharya, Engle & Richardson, 2012), or below \(C = -10\%\) over \(h = 22\) days (Brownlees & Engle, 2017).

      Acharya, Engle & Richardson (2012) give a shortcut without simulation, \(\mathrm{LRMES}_i \approx 1 - \exp(-18 \times \mathrm{MES}_i^{2\%})\), where \(\mathrm{MES}_i^{2\%}\) is the average daily loss of the bank on days when the market falls by more than 2%. The factor 18 scales the daily loss to the six-month crisis, under a constant beta. For example, \(\mathrm{MES}^{2\%} = 3\%\) gives \(1 - e^{-0.54} = 41.7\%\). On the chapter's banks the shortcut LRMES ranges from 37% (HSBC) to 58% (ING), with 48% for JPMorgan and 52% for Banca Transilvania.

      SRISK and the Break-Even Leverage (1/2)

      Slide 14 in the PDF

      SRISK is the capital a bank would lack in a severe market crisis (Brownlees & Engle, 2017). Equity must be at least a share \(k\) of quasi-assets, the book debt \(D\) plus the market equity \(W\). Assuming that debt is not renegotiated in the crisis,

      \[\mathrm{SRISK}_i = k\,D_i - (1-k)(1 - \mathrm{LRMES}_i)\,W_i,\]
      (18.4)

      which can be written \(k[D_i + (1-\mathrm{LRMES}_i)W_i] - (1-\mathrm{LRMES}_i)W_i\): the capital required after the crisis minus the capital left.

      The prudential ratio is \(k = 8\%\) for US firms (Brownlees & Engle, 2017) and \(k = 5.5\%\) for European firms (Engle, Jondeau & Rockinger, 2015). The lower European ratio reflects accounting: IFRS (International Financial Reporting Standards) balance sheets allow less netting of derivatives than US GAAP (Generally Accepted Accounting Principles), so they report larger assets. A positive SRISK is a capital shortfall, and the system SRISK, the sum of the positive values, is published by the V-Lab of NYU Stern.

      In brief

      • SRISK (systemic risk): the capital a bank would lack in a severe market crisis

        • \(\mathrm{SRISK}_i = k\,D_i - (1 - k)(1 - \mathrm{LRMES}_i)\,W_i\), assuming debt is not renegotiated in the crisis
        • Equivalently, \(k[D_i + (1 - \mathrm{LRMES}_i)W_i] - (1 - \mathrm{LRMES}_i)W_i\)

      SRISK and the Break-Even Leverage (2/2)

      Slide 15 in the PDF

      Dividing (18.4) by \(W\) and writing the quasi-market leverage as \(\Lambda = (D+W)/W\) gives

      \[\frac{\mathrm{SRISK}}{W} = k(\Lambda - 1) - (1-k)(1 - \mathrm{LRMES}),\]

      \[\Lambda^* = 1 + \frac{(1-k)(1-\mathrm{LRMES})}{k}.\]

        Contribution: CoVaR

        Contribution reverses the direction of exposure: it asks how much worse the tail of the system becomes when a given bank is in distress. The section defines CoVaR and ΔCoVaR, estimates them by quantile regression, tests whether the contributions differ between banks and compares them with the bank's own VaR and with its exposure. It ends with the question of how far rankings of banks by these measures can be trusted.

        CoVaR: the VaR of the System Given a Bank in Distress

        Slide 19 in the PDF

        The CoVaR of the system conditional on bank \(i\) being at its VaR (Adrian & Brunnermeier, 2016) is defined by

        \[P\big(X^{sys} \le -\mathrm{CoVaR}_\alpha^{sys|i} \,\big|\, X_i = -\mathrm{VaR}_{i,\alpha}\big) = \alpha,\]
        (18.6)

        the loss of the system that is exceeded with probability \(\alpha\) on a day when bank \(i\) is at its own VaR.

        ΔCoVaR is the change from the median state of the bank, a normal day, to its distress state,

        \[\Delta\mathrm{CoVaR}_\alpha^{sys|i} = \mathrm{CoVaR}_\alpha^{sys|X_i = q_\alpha} - \mathrm{CoVaR}_\alpha^{sys|X_i = q_{50\%}},\]
        (18.7)

        with the bank return fixed at the quantile shown. ΔCoVaR measures the contribution of bank \(i\) to the tail risk of the system. Reversing the roles of bank and system gives the exposure CoVaR, the VaR of bank \(i\) when the system is in distress.

        Estimating CoVaR by Quantile Regression

        Slide 20 in the PDF

        Quantile regression (Koenker & Bassett, 1978) models the conditional quantile as \(q_\alpha(Y \mid x) = a_\alpha + b_\alpha x\) and estimates it by

        \[(\hat a_\alpha, \hat b_\alpha) = \arg\min_{a,b} \sum_t \rho_\alpha(y_t - a - b x_t), \qquad \rho_\alpha(u) = u\,(\alpha - \mathbf{1}\{u < 0\}),\]
        (18.8)

        where the check function \(\rho_\alpha\) weights negative residuals by \(1-\alpha\) and positive ones by \(\alpha\).

        CoVaR follows in three steps:

        1. The system is regressed on the bank, \(\hat q_\alpha(X^{sys} \mid X_i) = \hat a_\alpha + \hat b_\alpha X_i\);
        2. The bank's distress quantile is plugged in, \(\mathrm{CoVaR}_\alpha^{sys|i} = -(\hat a_\alpha + \hat b_\alpha \hat q_\alpha(X_i))\);
        3. The intercept cancels in the difference,
        \[\Delta\mathrm{CoVaR}_\alpha^{sys|i} = \hat b_\alpha\big(\hat q_{50\%}(X_i) - \hat q_\alpha(X_i)\big).\]
        (18.9)

        Uncertainty is measured by a moving-block bootstrap with 999 resamples, which keeps volatility clustering. The block length grows like \(n^{1/3}\) (Hall, Horowitz & Jing, 1995): \(3{,}961^{1/3} \approx 16\), rounded to 20 observations, about one trading month.

        In brief

        • Quantile regression Koenker & Bassett (1978): \(q_\alpha(Y \mid x) = a_\alpha + b_\alpha x\)

          • The check function \(\rho_\alpha(u) = u\,(\alpha - \mathbf{1}\{u < 0\})\) weights negative residuals by \(1 - \alpha\) and positive ones by \(\alpha\)

            Reading the Tests: Holm and the Joint Wald Test

            Slide 26 in the PDF

            With 25 pairs tested at 5%, more than one false "difference" is expected even if all contributions are equal, so the pairs are treated as one family. The procedure of Holm (1979) sorts the p-values, \(p_{(1)} \le \dots \le p_{(m)}\), and rejects while \(p_{(k)} \le 0.05/(m-k+1)\); the family-wise error stays below 5% for any dependence between the tests. In a toy check with \(p = 0.010, 0.030, 0.040\), the first is rejected because \(0.010 \le 0.05/3\), and the procedure stops at the second because \(0.030 > 0.05/2\): one rejection instead of three.

            The joint Wald test of equal contributions within a region uses \(W = \hat d^\top \hat V^{-1} \hat d\), which is \(\chi^2\) with the number of banks minus one degrees of freedom; \(\hat d\) collects the differences from one reference bank, any choice giving the same \(W\), and \(\hat V\) is their bootstrap covariance.

            For the US, the joint test rejects (\(p = 0.011\)) while none of the 25 pairs survives Holm. The two results are compatible: the joint test pools moderate differences, while Holm needs one very strong pair, \(p_{(1)} \le 0.05/25\). The contributions are therefore not all equal, but the data do not say which pair differs.

                From Pairs to Networks: the Variance Decomposition (2/2)

                Slide 34 in the PDF

                The \(H\)-day forecast error of \(y_i\) is a sum of shocks to all series, so its variance can be split by source. In the moving-average form \(y_t = \mu + \sum_{h\ge0}\Phi_h\,\varepsilon_{t-h}\), \(\Phi_h\) is the response of \(y\) after \(h\) days to a shock today, with \(\Phi_0 = I\). Orthogonal (Cholesky) shocks depend on the ordering of the series; the generalized decomposition of Koop, Pesaran & Potter (1996) and Pesaran & Shin (1998) does not.

                The generalized share of the forecast error variance of \(i\) due to \(j\) is

                \[\theta_{ij}(H) = \frac{\sigma_{jj}^{-1}\sum_{h=0}^{H-1}(e_i^\top \Phi_h \Sigma e_j)^2}{\sum_{h=0}^{H-1} e_i^\top \Phi_h \Sigma \Phi_h^\top e_i}, \qquad \tilde\theta_{ij} = \frac{\theta_{ij}}{\sum_j \theta_{ij}},\]
                (18.11)

                where the numerator is the part of the forecast error variance of \(i\) due to a shock in \(j\), the denominator the total forecast error variance of \(i\), \(e_i\) the \(i\)-th unit vector and \(\sigma_{jj}\) the variance of shock \(j\), the \(j\)-th diagonal element of \(\Sigma\).

                      The Financial Risk Meter and the GACV Penalty (1/2)

                      Slide 44 in the PDF

                      Each firm's tail equation is a LASSO (Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator) quantile regression (Belloni & Chernozhukov, 2011):

                      \[\hat\beta_j(\lambda) = \arg\min_{\beta}\ \frac{1}{n}\sum_t \rho_\tau\big(X_{j,t} - \beta_0 - \beta^\top Z_{-j,t}\big) + \lambda\|\beta\|_1,\]
                      (18.14)

                      over the \(n\) days of the window, where \(X_{j,t}\) is the return of firm \(j\), \(Z_{-j,t}\) collects the returns of the other firms and the lagged macro variables (the S&P 500 and Euro Stoxx 50 returns and the log change of the VIX), and \(\rho_\tau\) is the check loss of quantile regression. A larger \(\lambda\) leaves fewer active coefficients.

                      The penalty is chosen by GACV (Generalized Approximate Cross-Validation), which trades fit against the number of active coefficients:

                      \[\mathrm{GACV}(\lambda) = \frac{\sum_t \rho_\tau\big(X_{j,t} - \hat X_{j,t}(\lambda)\big)}{n - \mathrm{df}(\lambda)},\]
                      (18.15)

                      where \(\hat X_{j,t}(\lambda)\) is the fitted quantile and \(\mathrm{df}(\lambda)\) the number of interpolated observations, equal to one plus the number of nonzero slopes. The selected \(\lambda_j\) minimises (18.15) for each firm, and the FRM is the average of the \(\lambda_j\) across firms.

                          Limits of Stress Tests

                          Slide 54 in the PDF

                          Stress tests have three limits:

                          1. Scenarios are chosen by people, and the crisis that comes is rarely the one that was tested.
                          2. Second-round effects, such as fire sales, funding runs and counterparty losses, are hard to model and usually left out, as the ECB noted for its climate test.
                          3. Market-based measures, MES, CoVaR and connectedness, react quickly but only after prices move, whereas balance-sheet stress tests look forward but slowly.

                          Early-warning research therefore combines market measures with balance-sheet data, for example through entropy-based measures of market risk (Pele, Lazar & Dufour, 2017).

                          In brief

                          • Scenarios are chosen by people: the crisis that comes is rarely the one that was tested
                          • Second-round effects are hard to model and usually left out

                          • Market-based measures (MES, CoVaR, connectedness) react quickly but only after prices move

                          Slide 56 in the PDF

                          \[y_t = \mu + \sum_{j=1}^p \Phi_j y_{t-j} + \Gamma f_t + v_t,\]

                          Spillovers and Adverse Credit Shocks

                          Slide 57 in the PDF

                          (18.17)

                          where \(F_{i\leftarrow\bullet,\tau}^{(h)}\) is the share of the \(h\)-day forecast error variance of \(i\) that comes from the other series, the "from" column of the connectedness table, averaged over the \(m\) series.                               Common Mistakes

                              Slide 74 in the PDF

                              Six pitfalls recur:

                              1. ΔCoVaR or a spillover edge is read as causal, although a common shock creates the same pattern.
                              2. Overlapping intervals are read as "equal" and separate ones as "different", whereas the difference must be tested on joint draws and corrected for the number of pairs, with Holm and the joint Wald test.
                              3. The bank itself is included in the system portfolio, which adds a mechanical self-contribution.
                              4. Orthogonal variance decompositions are used although their results depend on the order of the banks.
                              5. SRISK is reported without balance sheets, or the \(1 - e^{-18\,\mathrm{MES}}\) shortcut is presented as the published measure.
                              6. Net labels are drawn without intervals.

                              LRMES via GJR–GARCH and DCC: the Model

                              Slide 76 in the PDF

                              The long-run marginal expected shortfall (LRMES) of Brownlees & Engle (2017) is simulated from a model of the zero-mean daily log returns of the bank and of the market, estimated by two-step quasi-maximum likelihood (QML). Volatility follows a GJR–GARCH(1,1) (Glosten, Jagannathan & Runkle, 1993), \(\sigma_t^2 = \omega + (\alpha + \gamma\mathbf{1}\{r_{t-1} < 0\})r_{t-1}^2 + \beta\sigma_{t-1}^2\), where \(\gamma > 0\) is the extra reaction after a fall and \(\alpha\) is not the tail level. The bank–market correlation follows a DCC(1,1) (dynamic conditional correlation) model (Engle, 2002), \(Q_t = (1-a-b)S + a\epsilon_{t-1}\epsilon_{t-1}^\top + bQ_{t-1}\) with \(\rho_t = Q_{12,t}/\sqrt{Q_{11,t}Q_{22,t}}\), where \(\epsilon_t\) are the standardised returns and \(S\) their sample correlation matrix.

                              In brief

                              • Model of Brownlees & Engle (2017): zero-mean daily log returns of the bank and of the market
                                • estimated by two-step quasi-maximum likelihood (QML)
                              • Volatility: GJR–GARCH(1,1) Glosten, Jagannathan & Runkle (1993)
                                • GARCH: generalized autoregressive conditional heteroskedasticity
                                • \(\sigma^2_{t} = \omega + (\alpha + \gamma\,\mathbf{1}\{r_{t-1} < 0\})\,r^2_{t-1} + \beta\,\sigma^2_{t-1}\)
                                • \(\gamma > 0\): extra reaction after a fall (leverage effect)
                                • Here \(\alpha\) is not the tail level
                              • Correlation: DCC(1,1) (dynamic conditional correlation) Engle (2002)
                                • \(Q_t = (1 - a - b)\,S + a\,\epsilon_{t-1}\epsilon_{t-1}^\top + b\,Q_{t-1}\), \(\rho_t = Q_{12,t}/\sqrt{Q_{11,t}Q_{22,t}}\)
                                • \(\epsilon_t = (r_{it}/\sigma_{it}, r_{mt}/\sigma_{mt})^\top\): standardised returns
                                • \(S\): their sample correlation matrix (\(\bar Q\) in Engle (2002))
                                • \(a\): reaction and \(b\): persistence of the correlation
                                • \(\rho_t\): today's bank–market correlation

                              A Normal Check: Which CoVaR Rises with Dependence?

                              Slide 84 in the PDF

                              For a bivariate Normal with zero means, \(\sigma_{sys} = 1.5\) and \(\alpha = 1\%\) (\(z = -2.326\)), the system given the bank at its VaR has mean \(\rho\sigma_{sys}z\) and standard deviation \(\sigma_{sys}\sqrt{1-\rho^2}\), so \(\mathrm{CoVaR}_\alpha = \sigma_{sys}|z|(\rho + \sqrt{1-\rho^2})\) and \(\Delta\mathrm{CoVaR}_\alpha = \rho\sigma_{sys}|z|\).                               MES as an Euler Contribution: Proof (1/2)

                              Slide 104 in the PDF

                              For a portfolio \(X_p = \sum_i w_i X_i\) with a continuous distribution, expected shortfall \(\mathrm{ES}_\alpha(X_p) = -\mathbb{E}[X_p \mid X_p \le q_\alpha(X_p)]\) is positively homogeneous of degree 1 in \(w\), so by Euler's theorem it equals \(\sum_i w_i\,\partial\mathrm{ES}_\alpha/\partial w_i\). Differentiating \(-\frac{1}{\alpha}\mathbb{E}[X_p\mathbf{1}\{X_p \le q_\alpha(w)\}]\) gives an interior and a boundary term, and the boundary term vanishes because \(\partial q_\alpha/\partial w_i = \mathbb{E}[X_i \mid X_p = q_\alpha]\) (Gouriéroux, Laurent & Scaillet, 2000). Hence \(\partial\mathrm{ES}_\alpha/\partial w_i = -\mathbb{E}[X_i \mid X_p \le q_\alpha(X_p)]\), the MES of bank \(i\) when the conditioning portfolio is the portfolio itself (Scaillet, 2004).

                              In brief

                              • Portfolio \(X_p = \sum_i w_i X_i\) with a continuous distribution
                                • \(\ES_\alpha(X_p) = -\E[X_p \mid X_p \le q_\alpha(X_p)]\)
                                • \(\ES_\alpha\) is positively homogeneous of degree 1 in \(w\), so by Euler's theorem \(\ES_\alpha(X_p) = \sum_i w_i\,\partial \ES_\alpha / \partial w_i\)
                                • Write \(\ES_\alpha = -\frac{1}{\alpha}\E\left[X_p \mathbf{1}\{X_p \le q_\alpha(w)\}\right]\), with \(f_p\) the density of \(X_p\)
                                • The derivative has an interior and a boundary term: \(\dfrac{\partial}{\partial w_i}\E[X_p \mathbf{1}\{X_p \le q_\alpha\}] = \E[X_i \mathbf{1}\{X_p \le q_\alpha\}] + q_\alpha f_p(q_\alpha)\left(\dfrac{\partial q_\alpha}{\partial w_i} - \E[X_i \mid X_p = q_\alpha]\right)\)
                                • The quantile derivative is \(\partial q_\alpha/\partial w_i = \E[X_i \mid X_p = q_\alpha]\) Gouriéroux, Laurent & Scaillet (2000), so the boundary term is zero
                                • Hence \(\partial \ES_\alpha / \partial w_i = -\E[X_i \mid X_p \le q_\alpha(X_p)] = \MES_{i,\alpha}\) with \(m = p\) Gouriéroux, Laurent & Scaillet (2000), Scaillet (2004)

                              LRMES Simulation: Innovations and Recursions

                              Slide 106 in the PDF

                              The simulation uses the standardised residuals \(\epsilon_{it}\) and \(\epsilon_{mt}\), whose sample correlation matrix \(S\) is the long-run target of \(Q_t\), and the bank innovation orthogonal to the market, \(\xi_{it} = (\epsilon_{it} - \rho_t\epsilon_{mt})/\sqrt{1-\rho_t^2}\) (Brownlees & Engle, 2017). Each simulated day draws a past date \(\tau\), takes the pair \((\xi_{i\tau}, \epsilon_{m\tau})\), rebuilds the bank shock with the simulated correlation and updates \(\sigma^2\) and \(Q\). The restrictions \(\omega > 0\), \(\alpha \ge 0\), \(\alpha + \gamma \ge 0\), \(\beta \ge 0\) and \(\alpha + \gamma/2 + \beta < 1\) (for symmetric innovations) keep the variance positive and finite, \(a, b > 0\) with \(a + b < 1\) keep \(Q_t\) positive definite, and the simulation starts from the variance and \(Q\) filtered to the last date.

                              In brief

                              • Standardised residuals \(\epsilon_{it} = r_{it}/\sigma_{it}\), \(\epsilon_{mt} = r_{mt}/\sigma_{mt}\)
                                • \(S\) is their sample correlation matrix, the long-run target of \(Q_t\)
                                • Bank innovation orthogonal to the market: \(\xi_{it} = (\epsilon_{it} - \rho_t\,\epsilon_{mt})/\sqrt{1 - \rho_t^2}\) Brownlees & Engle (2017)
                              • Each simulated day \(s\): draw a past date \(\tau\) and take the pair \((\xi_{i\tau}, \epsilon_{m\tau})\)
                                • rebuild the bank shock with the simulated correlation
                                • \(\epsilon_{i,t+s} = \rho_{t+s}\,\epsilon_{m\tau} + \sqrt{1 - \rho_{t+s}^2}\,\xi_{i\tau}\), then update \(\sigma^2\) and \(Q\) with the new returns
                              • Restrictions, so that \(\sigma_t^2 > 0\) and \(Q_t\) is positive definite
                                • GJR–GARCH: \(\omega > 0\), \(\alpha \ge 0\), \(\alpha + \gamma \ge 0\), \(\beta \ge 0\), \(\alpha + \gamma/2 + \beta < 1\) (finite variance for symmetric innovations)
                                • in general, finite variance needs \(\alpha + \gamma\,\E[\epsilon^2\mathbf{1}\{\epsilon < 0\}] + \beta < 1\)
                                • DCC: \(a, b > 0\), \(a + b < 1\)
                                • Initialisation: \(Q_1 = S\) in estimation; the simulation starts from \(\sigma^2_{T+1}\) and \(Q_{T+1}\) filtered to the last date

                              Quantile Regression: Asymptotics

                              Slide 107 in the PDF

                              With \(n\) days, regressors \(x_t = (1, X_{i,t})^\top\) and \(f_t\) the conditional density of \(Y\) at its \(\alpha\)-quantile, \(\sqrt n(\hat\theta_\alpha - \theta_\alpha)\) converges to \(N(0, \alpha(1-\alpha)D_1^{-1}D_0D_1^{-1})\) with \(D_0 = \mathbb{E}[x_tx_t^\top]\) and \(D_1 = \mathbb{E}[f_tx_tx_t^\top]\), assuming independent observations or martingale-difference scores. With serially dependent scores the middle matrix becomes the long-run covariance of \(x_t(\alpha - \mathbf{1}\{u_t < 0\})\), estimated by HAC or a block bootstrap. The sparsity \(1/f_t\) must also be estimated, and at \(\alpha = 1\%\) few observations lie near the quantile, so the estimate is noisy; the slope \(b_\alpha\) may also change with \(\alpha\).

                              In brief

                              • Asymptotics for a fixed \(\alpha\), with \(\hat\theta_\alpha = (\hat a_\alpha, \hat b_\alpha)^\top\)
                                • \(n\) days, \(x_t = (1, X_{i,t})^\top\), \(f_t\): the conditional density of \(Y\) at its \(\alpha\)-quantile
                                • \(\sqrt{n}(\hat\theta_\alpha - \theta_\alpha) \Rightarrow N\left(0,\; \alpha(1 - \alpha)\,D_1^{-1} D_0 D_1^{-1}\right)\), \(D_0 = \E[x_t x_t^\top]\), \(D_1 = \E[f_t\, x_t x_t^\top]\)
                                • Assumes independent observations (or scores \(x_t(\alpha - \mathbf{1}\{u_t < 0\})\) that are a martingale difference), \(f_t > 0\) near the quantile and \(D_0\), \(D_1\) positive definite
                                • With serially dependent scores the variance becomes \(D_1^{-1}\Omega D_1^{-1}\), with \(\Omega = \sum_h \mathrm{Cov}(x_t\psi_t,\, x_{t-h}\psi_{t-h})\) and \(\psi_t = \alpha - \mathbf{1}\{u_t < 0\}\): a HAC estimate or a block bootstrap
                                • The sparsity \(1/f_t\) must be estimated (kernel or bandwidth)
                                • At \(\alpha = 1\%\) few observations lie near the quantile, so this estimate is noisy
                              • The slope \(b_\alpha\) can change with \(\alpha\)
                                • the bank may matter more for the system in the tail than in the centre

                              Extremal Quantiles: Why \(\alpha = 1\%\) Is Hard

                              Slide 108 in the PDF

                              At \(\alpha = 1\%\) only \(\alpha n = 40\) days remain in the tail, and the Normal approximation, which assumes that \(\alpha n\) grows fast, can be poor; extremal-quantile theory is built for this case (Chernozhukov & Fernández-Val, 2011). For the same slope \(\hat b_{1\%}\), the block-bootstrap standard error is about 0.3 times the kernel-based one (median over banks, range 0.21–0.46), and the gap shows method uncertainty rather than which interval covers correctly. The practical rule is to report \(\alpha = 5\%\) and \(\alpha = 1\%\) with \(\alpha n\) and to expect the 1% ranking to be noisier; the Spearman correlation of 0.13 between the 1% and 5% rankings may reflect noise, genuine differences between the tail levels, or both.

                              In brief

                              • At \(\alpha = 1\%\) only \(\alpha n = 40\) days remain in the tail
                                • The Normal approximation above assumes \(\alpha n \to \infty\) fast; with \(\alpha n\) of a few dozen it can be poor
                                • Extremal-quantile theory (extreme-value limits, subsampling) is the alternative built for this case Chernozhukov & Fernández-Val (2011)
                              • For the same slope \(\hat b_{1\%}\), the block-bootstrap standard error is about 0.3 times the kernel-based one
                                • median over banks; the ratio ranges from 0.21 to 0.46

                                • The kernel-based standard error rests on a sparsity estimate from the few observations near the 1% quantile
                                • The bootstrap redraws the same few tail days: the gap shows method uncertainty, not which interval covers correctly

                              Testing \(\Delta\)CoVaR: Three Hypotheses

                              Slide 109 in the PDF

                              Three hypotheses are tested:

                              1. No contribution, \(H_0: b_\alpha = 0\), uses the variance of \(\hat b_\alpha\) from 999 block-bootstrap draws with a Holm correction over the 11 banks, since the joint asymptotics of Koenker & Bassett (1982) assume serially uncorrelated scores.
                              2. Equal tail and central links, \(H_0: b_\alpha = b_{50\%}\), uses the variance of the difference from 999 moving-block draws that keep the pairs of days together.
                              3. Equal contributions, \(H_0: \Delta\mathrm{CoVaR}_i = \Delta\mathrm{CoVaR}_j\), uses pairwise \(z\)-statistics with the bootstrap standard error of the difference, a correction of Holm (1979) over the pairs and a joint Wald test that all are equal.

                              In brief

                              • (1) \(H_0\colon b_\alpha = 0\) (no contribution)
                                • The joint asymptotics of \((\hat b_\alpha, \hat b_{50\%})\) Koenker & Bassett (1982) assume serially uncorrelated scores
                                • Here: the variance of \(\hat b_\alpha\) from the same 999 block-bootstrap draws, Holm correction over the 11 banks
                              • (2) \(H_0\colon b_\alpha = b_{50\%}\) (tail link equal to the central link)
                                • We estimate the variance of \(\hat b_\alpha - \hat b_{50\%}\) from 999 moving-block bootstrap draws that keep the pairs of days together
                              • (3) \(H_0\colon \Delta\CoVaR_i = \Delta\CoVaR_j\) (equal contributions)
                                • \(z_{ij} = (\widehat{\Delta\CoVaR}_i - \widehat{\Delta\CoVaR}_j)/\widehat{\mathrm{se}}^{*}\) for each pair, with \(\widehat{\mathrm{se}}^{*}\) the bootstrap standard error of the difference
                                • Holm (1979) correction over the pairs; a joint Wald test that all are equal

                              Backtesting CoVaR and MES (1/2)

                              Slide 112 in the PDF

                              Systemic measures are conditional risk measures and, as Banulescu-Radu, Hurlin, Leymarie & Scaillet (2021) show, need conditional backtests. For \(\mathrm{CoVaR}^{\le}\) the hit \(I_t\), a system loss beyond CoVaR on a day when the bank is beyond its VaR, has probability \(\alpha^2\) under \(H_0\) if both the bank VaR and the conditional quantile are calibrated. The Kupiec test of Chapter 8 checks that the hit frequency is \(\alpha^2\), with \(\mathrm{LR}_{\mathrm{POF}} \sim \chi^2_1\) under \(H_0\), the Christoffersen test also checks that hits are independent, and MES is backtested with the cumulative joint violation process of the same authors.

                              In brief

                              • Systemic measures are conditional risk measures, so they need conditional backtests Banulescu-Radu, Hurlin, Leymarie & Scaillet (2021)
                                • For \(\CoVaR^{\le}\): hit \(I_t = \mathbf{1}\{X^{sys}_t \le -\CoVaR^{\le}_t,\ X_{i,t} \le -\VaR_{i,t}\}\) with \(P(I_t = 1) = \alpha^2\) under \(H_0\)
                                • This holds if both the bank VaR and the conditional quantile are calibrated
                                • The Kupiec test checks that the hit frequency is \(\alpha^2\)
                                • With \(x\) hits in \(n\) days and \(\hat\pi = x/n\), \(\mathrm{LR}_{\mathrm{POF}} = -2\ln\dfrac{(1 - \alpha^2)^{n - x}(\alpha^2)^x}{(1 - \hat\pi)^{n - x}\hat\pi^{x}} \sim \chi^2_1\) under \(H_0\) (Chapter 8)
                                • The Christoffersen test also checks that hits are independent: a hit today must not make one tomorrow more likely
                                • The bank VaR is checked separately, against its own rate \(\alpha\)
                                • For MES: the cumulative joint violation process of Banulescu-Radu, Hurlin, Leymarie & Scaillet (2021)

                              What the Researcher Must Check (1/2)

                              Slide 125 in the PDF

                              Two errors must be checked first:

                              2. Cholesky decompositions change with the order of the banks, so the columns are permuted and the generalized table must not change.

                              What the Researcher Must Check (2/2)

                              Slide 126 in the PDF

                              Three more errors complete the list:

                              1. Look-ahead, a window labelled by its first date or a lag order chosen on the full sample, is caught by deleting data after \(t\) and checking that the index at \(t\) does not move.
                              2. Overlap between rolling indices and forward outcomes inflates OLS \(t\)-statistics, so Newey–West lags must be at least the overlap or a block bootstrap is used.
                              3. With 13 banks and \(p = 2\) each equation has 27 coefficients from 250 days, so more banks need shrinkage (Demirer et al., 2018), and edges that change with a LASSO VAR signal estimation noise, not structure.

                              In brief

                              • Look-ahead: a window labelled by its first date, or a lag order chosen on the full sample
                                • Test: the index at \(t\) must not move when data after \(t\) are deleted
                              • Overlap: rolling indices and forward outcomes overlap
                                • so OLS \(t\)-statistics are inflated
                                • Test: Newey–West lags at least equal to the overlap, or a block bootstrap
                              • Dimension: 13 banks and \(p = 2\) give 27 coefficients per equation from 250 days
                                • more banks need shrinkage Demirer et al. (2018)
                                • Test: compare with a LASSO VAR
                                • Unstable edges signal estimation noise, not structure
