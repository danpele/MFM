\(\blacktriangleright\) Central result: “VaR and \(\Delta\)CoVaR Measure Different Things”

Systemic risk is the risk that the distress of one or more institutions affects the financial system as a whole and, through it, the real economy. It is an externality: a bank does not pay for the losses its failure imposes on others. The chapter uses daily prices of thirteen banks from the US, Europe and Romania, from January 2010 to 18 September 2026. The declines of the three banking systems are simultaneous, in the euro-area crisis, the COVID-19 pandemic and the 2023 bank failures, and the maximum drawdown of the bank portfolio is 51% in the US, 61% in Europe and 47% in Romania.

The usual risk measure of a bank, Value-at-Risk (VaR), looks at the bank in isolation. Adrian & Brunnermeier (2016) measure instead its contribution to the system: ΔCoVaR 1% is the additional loss of the system when the bank moves from its median state to its own VaR. Across the eleven international banks, the Spearman rank correlation between VaR 1% and ΔCoVaR 1% is 0.13. Deutsche Bank and ING have the largest VaR, 7.19% and 7.16%, but only an average ΔCoVaR, while JPMorgan has one of the smallest VaR, 4.83%, but one of the largest ΔCoVaR. Regulating each bank by its own VaR therefore ignores its contribution to the system; with eleven banks and overlapping confidence intervals, however, the correlation remains very uncertain.

The systemic measures do not produce a single ranking either. The rank correlation between MES 5% (Marginal Expected Shortfall), the average loss of the bank on the worst 5% of market days, and ΔCoVaR 1% is −0.04, and the one between ΔCoVaR and the net volatility spillover is also −0.04; by contrast, VaR and MES have a rank correlation of 0.88. The chapter treats in turn the three families of measures, exposure (MES and SRISK), contribution (CoVaR) and connectedness (spillover networks and the Financial Risk Meter), and then stress tests and climate risk.

    Exposure: MES and SRISK

    How Much Does a Bank Lose When the Market Crashes?

    Slide 9 in the PDF
    • Exposure: the loss of the bank given a market crisis, not its stand-alone VaR
      • What matters are the banks that run out of capital exactly when the system needs it
    • Three steps, from one crash day to a crisis
      • MES: the average loss of the bank on the worst 5% of market days, the level used by Acharya, Pedersen, Philippon & Richardson (2017)
      • LRMES: the loss of the bank in a crisis lasting weeks or months
      • SRISK: the capital the bank would lack after that loss

    Marginal Expected Shortfall and the Euler Property

    Slide 10 in the PDF
    • MES (Marginal Expected Shortfall) of bank \(i\) at level \(\alpha\) Acharya, Pedersen, Philippon & Richardson (2017)
      • \(\MES_{i,\alpha} = -\E\left[X_i \mid X_m \le q_\alpha(X_m)\right]\): the average loss of the bank on the worst \(\alpha\) share of days for the market \(m\)
    • Why “marginal”: if \(m = \sum_i w_i X_i\) is the portfolio itself, then \(\ES_\alpha(X_m) = \sum_i w_i\,\MES_{i,\alpha}\)
      • this is the Euler allocation (proof in the Appendix)
      • Our market indices are not portfolios of these 13 banks
      • Here MES measures exposure, not a share of the index ES
    • Historical estimate: average \(-X_i\) over the days with \(X_m \le \hat q_\alpha(X_m)\)
      • With \(\alpha = 5\%\) and 3 961 days, about 200 days

      From MES to a Crisis: LRMES

      Slide 13 in the PDF
      • LRMES (Long-Run MES): the expected fall of the bank's market equity in a crisis
        • \(\mathrm{LRMES}_{i,t} = -\E_t\left[R_{i,t+1:t+h} \mid R_{m,t+1:t+h} < C\right]\), with \(R\) the \(h\)-day arithmetic returns of bank and market
        • Crisis: the market falls below \(C = -40\%\) over six months Acharya, Engle & Richardson (2012), or below \(C = -10\%\) over \(h = 22\) days Brownlees & Engle (2017)
      • Shortcut of Acharya, Engle & Richardson (2012), without simulation: \(\mathrm{LRMES}_i \approx 1 - \exp(-18 \times \MES_i^{2\%})\)
        • \(\MES_i^{2\%}\): average daily loss of the bank on days when the market falls by more than 2%
        • The 18 scales it to the six-month crisis, with a constant beta
        • Example: \(\MES^{2\%} = 3\%\) gives \(1 - e^{-0.54} = 41.7\%\)
      • On our banks: \(37\%\) (HSBC) to \(58\%\) (ING)
        • JPMorgan \(48\%\), Banca Transilvania \(52\%\)

      SRISK and the Break-Even Leverage (1/2)

      Slide 14 in the PDF
      • SRISK (systemic risk): the capital a bank would lack in a severe market crisis
      • Equity must be at least a share \(k\) of quasi-assets, book debt \(D\) plus market equity \(W\) Brownlees & Engle (2017)
        • \(\mathrm{SRISK}_i = k\,D_i - (1 - k)(1 - \mathrm{LRMES}_i)\,W_i\), assuming debt is not renegotiated in the crisis
        • Equivalently, \(k[D_i + (1 - \mathrm{LRMES}_i)W_i] - (1 - \mathrm{LRMES}_i)W_i\)
        • That is, the capital required after the crisis minus the capital left
      • \(k = 8\%\) for US firms Brownlees & Engle (2017) and \(k = 5.5\%\) for European firms Engle, Jondeau & Rockinger (2015)
        • IFRS (International Financial Reporting Standards) balance sheets allow less netting of derivatives than US GAAP (Generally Accepted Accounting Principles)
        • So they report larger assets
      • Positive SRISK is a capital shortfall
        • the system SRISK, the sum of the positive values, is published by V-Lab, New York University Stern School of Business

      SRISK and the Break-Even Leverage (2/2)

      Slide 15 in the PDF
      • Divide by \(W\), with quasi-market leverage \(\Lambda = (D + W)/W\): \(\dfrac{\mathrm{SRISK}}{W} = k(\Lambda - 1) - (1 - k)(1 - \mathrm{LRMES})\)
      • SRISK is positive once \(\Lambda\) exceeds the break-even leverage \(\Lambda^* = 1 + \dfrac{(1 - k)(1 - \mathrm{LRMES})}{k}\)
      • Example: \(D = 900\), \(W = 100\) (so \(\Lambda = 10\)), \(\mathrm{LRMES} = 41.7\%\)
        • \(\mathrm{SRISK} = 0.08 \times 900 - 0.92 \times (1 - 41.7\%) \times 100 = 72 - 53.6 = 18.4\)
        • \(\Lambda^* = 7.7 < \Lambda = 10\): a shortfall
      • Our data contain prices, not balance sheets
        • we report \(\Lambda^*\) from the estimated LRMES
        • we treat leverage as an assumption

        How Much Worse Is the System When a Bank Is in Distress?

        Slide 18 in the PDF
        • MES looks from the market to the bank
          • contribution looks the other way, from the bank to the system
        • We need a quantile in the tail of the system, not its mean
          • Quantile regression estimates the conditional quantile directly Koenker & Bassett (1978)
        • Levels: \(\alpha = 1\%\) and \(\alpha = 5\%\), those reported by Adrian & Brunnermeier (2016)
          • we compare the rankings at the two levels
        • Questions of this section
          • How much does each bank add to the tail loss of the system?
          • Are the differences between banks statistically significant?

        CoVaR: the VaR of the System Given a Bank in Distress

        Slide 19 in the PDF
        • CoVaR of the system conditional on bank \(i\) at its VaR Adrian & Brunnermeier (2016)
          • \(P\left(X^{sys} \le -\CoVaR_\alpha^{sys|i} \;\middle|\; X_i = -\VaR_{i,\alpha}\right) = \alpha\)
          • The loss of the system that is exceeded with probability \(\alpha\) on a day when bank \(i\) is at its own VaR
        • \(\Delta\)CoVaR: the change from the median state of the bank, a normal day, to its distress state
          • \(\Delta\CoVaR_\alpha^{sys|i} = \CoVaR_\alpha^{sys|X_i = q_\alpha} - \CoVaR_\alpha^{sys|X_i = q_{50\%}}\), with the bank return fixed at the quantile shown
        • \(\Delta\)CoVaR measures the contribution of bank \(i\) to the tail risk of the system
        • Reversing the roles gives the exposure CoVaR
          • the VaR of bank \(i\) when the system is in distress

        Estimating CoVaR by Quantile Regression

        Slide 20 in the PDF
        • Quantile regression Koenker & Bassett (1978): \(q_\alpha(Y \mid x) = a_\alpha + b_\alpha x\)
          • \((\hat a_\alpha, \hat b_\alpha) = \arg\min_{a,b} \sum_t \rho_\alpha(y_t - a - b x_t)\)
          • The check function \(\rho_\alpha(u) = u\,(\alpha - \mathbf{1}\{u < 0\})\) weights negative residuals by \(1 - \alpha\) and positive ones by \(\alpha\)
        • 1. Regress the system on the bank: \(\hat q_\alpha(X^{sys} \mid X_i) = \hat a_\alpha + \hat b_\alpha X_i\)
        • 2. Plug in the bank's distress quantile: \(\CoVaR_\alpha^{sys|i} = -\left(\hat a_\alpha + \hat b_\alpha \hat q_\alpha(X_i)\right)\)
        • 3. \(\Delta\CoVaR_\alpha^{sys|i} = \hat b_\alpha\left(\hat q_{50\%}(X_i) - \hat q_\alpha(X_i)\right)\)
          • the intercept cancels
        • Uncertainty: moving-block bootstrap with 999 resamples
          • it keeps volatility clustering
          • The block length grows like \(n^{1/3}\) Hall, Horowitz & Jing (1995): \(3\,961^{1/3} \approx 16\), rounded to 20 observations, about one trading month

            Testing \(\Delta\)CoVaR 1%

            Slide 25 in the PDF
            • All tests use the same 999 block-bootstrap draws for every bank
              • so the estimates keep their dependence (details: Appendix)
            • (1) No contribution, \(H_0\colon b_\alpha = 0\)
              • Standard error of the slope \(\hat b_{1\%}\) from the same bootstrap draws, Holm (1979) correction over the 11 banks
              • \(z\)-ratios from \(8.7\) (HSBC) to \(22.6\) (Citigroup): the slope is positive for all 11 banks, also after Holm (Quantile Regression: Asymptotics)
              • With the kernel sandwich standard error Hendricks & Koenker (1992), valid only for serially uncorrelated scores: \(t\) from \(4.0\) to \(6.9\)
            • (2) \(H_0\colon b_\alpha = b_{50\%}\), rejected for 2 of 11 banks (Citigroup, ING, also after Holm)
              • not rejecting for the other 9 does not prove equality
            • (3) Equal contributions, \(H_0\colon \Delta\CoVaR_i = \Delta\CoVaR_j\)
              • dominance tests: Castro & Ferrari (2014); results by bank: Appendix
              • At \(\alpha = 1\%\): 4 of 25 within-region pairs differ at 5% (0 after Holm); joint Wald \(p = 0.011\) (US), \(0.28\) (Europe)
              • At \(\alpha = 5\%\): 3 of 25 pairs differ (0 after Holm); joint Wald \(p = 0.017\) (US), \(0.038\) (Europe)

                From Pairs to Networks: the Variance Decomposition (1/2)

                Slide 33 in the PDF
                • A network looks at all pairs of banks at once
                  • Nodes: banks; directed edges: how much of the future uncertainty of bank \(i\) comes from shocks to bank \(j\)
                • Vector autoregression (VAR) of order \(p\) for \(N\) series \(y_t\)
                  • \(y_t = c + \sum_{l=1}^p A_l\,y_{t-l} + \varepsilon_t\), \(\mathrm{Cov}(\varepsilon_t) = \Sigma\)
                  • Each series is regressed on \(p\) lags of all series
                  • \(c\): constants; \(A_l\): \(N \times N\) coefficient matrices
                  • \(\varepsilon_t\): shocks; \(\Sigma\): their covariance matrix (assumptions: Appendix)
                • Variance-decomposition networks Diebold & Yilmaz (2009), Diebold & Yilmaz (2012), Diebold & Yilmaz (2014): the approach of this section
                • Other networks
                  • Granger causality Billio, Getmansky, Lo & Pelizzon (2012) (Appendix)
                  • tail networks TENET (Tail-Event driven NETwork risk) Härdle, Wang & Yu (2016)
                  • the Financial Risk Meter (next section)

                From Pairs to Networks: the Variance Decomposition (2/2)

                Slide 34 in the PDF
                • The \(H\)-day forecast error of \(y_i\) is a sum of shocks to all series
                  • so its variance can be split by source
                  • Moving-average form \(y_t = \mu + \sum_{h \ge 0}\Phi_h\,\varepsilon_{t-h}\): \(\Phi_h\) is the response of \(y\) after \(h\) days to a shock today, \(\Phi_0 = I\)
                • Orthogonal (Cholesky) shocks depend on the ordering of the series
                  • the generalized decomposition does not Koop, Pesaran & Potter (1996), Pesaran & Shin (1998)
                  • \(\theta_{ij}(H) = \dfrac{\sigma_{jj}^{-1}\sum_{h=0}^{H-1}(e_i^\top \Phi_h \Sigma e_j)^2}{\sum_{h=0}^{H-1} e_i^\top \Phi_h \Sigma \Phi_h^\top e_i}\), normalised: \(\tilde\theta_{ij} = \theta_{ij}/\sum_j \theta_{ij}\)
                  • Numerator: the part of the forecast error variance of \(i\) due to a shock in \(j\)
                  • Denominator: the total forecast error variance of \(i\)
                  • \(e_i\): the \(i\)-th unit vector (it picks series \(i\))
                  • \(\sigma_{jj}\): the variance of shock \(j\), the \(j\)-th diagonal element of \(\Sigma\)

                      How Tight Are the Links between Banks in the Tail?

                      The Financial Risk Meter and the GACV Penalty (1/2)

                      Slide 44 in the PDF
                      • LASSO (Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator) quantile regression Belloni & Chernozhukov (2011)
                        • \(\hat\beta_j(\lambda) = \arg\min_{\beta} \dfrac{1}{n}\sum_t \rho_\tau\left(X_{j,t} - \beta_0 - \beta^\top Z_{-j,t}\right) + \lambda\|\beta\|_1\), over the \(n\) days of the window
                        • \(X_{j,t}\): return of firm \(j\)
                        • \(Z_{-j,t}\): returns of the other firms and the lagged macro variables (S&P 500 and Euro Stoxx 50 returns, log change of the VIX)
                        • A larger \(\lambda\) leaves fewer active coefficients
                      • GACV (Generalized Approximate Cross-Validation) trades fit against the number of active coefficients
                        • \(\mathrm{GACV}(\lambda) = \dfrac{\sum_t \rho_\tau\left(X_{j,t} - \hat X_{j,t}(\lambda)\right)}{n - \mathrm{df}(\lambda)}\), \(\hat X_{j,t}(\lambda)\): fitted quantile; \(\mathrm{df}\): number of interpolated observations, equal to 1 + the number of nonzero slopes (intercept included)
                        • The selected \(\lambda_j\) minimises GACV
                        • The FRM is the average of the \(\lambda_j\)

                              Does Connectedness Warn of Crises, or Only Track Volatility?

                              Slide 65 in the PDF
                              An open research question: systemic risk or only volatility?
                              • Connectedness indices are monitored as early-warning tools
                                • but an index from a VAR on volatilities may co-move with volatility without adding predictive information
                                • Live research: regularised VAR networks Demirer et al. (2018); tail connectedness differs from mean connectedness Ando, Greenwood-Nimmo & Shin (2022)

                              LRMES via GJR–GARCH and DCC: the Model

                              Slide 76 in the PDF
                              • Model of Brownlees & Engle (2017): zero-mean daily log returns of the bank and of the market
                                • estimated by two-step quasi-maximum likelihood (QML)
                              • Volatility: GJR–GARCH(1,1) Glosten, Jagannathan & Runkle (1993)
                                • GARCH: generalized autoregressive conditional heteroskedasticity
                                • \(\sigma^2_{t} = \omega + (\alpha + \gamma\,\mathbf{1}\{r_{t-1} < 0\})\,r^2_{t-1} + \beta\,\sigma^2_{t-1}\)
                                • \(\gamma > 0\): extra reaction after a fall (leverage effect)
                                • Here \(\alpha\) is not the tail level
                              • Correlation: DCC(1,1) (dynamic conditional correlation) Engle (2002)
                                • \(Q_t = (1 - a - b)\,S + a\,\epsilon_{t-1}\epsilon_{t-1}^\top + b\,Q_{t-1}\), \(\rho_t = Q_{12,t}/\sqrt{Q_{11,t}Q_{22,t}}\)
                                • \(\epsilon_t = (r_{it}/\sigma_{it}, r_{mt}/\sigma_{mt})^\top\): standardised returns
                                • \(S\): their sample correlation matrix (\(\bar Q\) in Engle (2002))
                                • \(a\): reaction and \(b\): persistence of the correlation
                                • \(\rho_t\): today's bank–market correlation

                              A Normal Check: Which CoVaR Rises with Dependence?

                              Slide 84 in the PDF
                              • Bivariate Normal with zero means, \(\sigma_{sys} = 1.5\), \(\alpha = 1\%\), \(z = -2.326\)
                                • the bank sits at its VaR, \(X_i = -\VaR_{i,\alpha}\)
                                • Given the bank, the system has mean \(\rho\,\sigma_{sys} z\) and standard deviation \(\sigma_{sys}\sqrt{1 - \rho^2}\)
                                • Hence \(\CoVaR_\alpha = \sigma_{sys}|z|\left(\rho + \sqrt{1 - \rho^2}\right)\) and, with the median state \(X_i = 0\), \(\Delta\CoVaR_\alpha = \rho\,\sigma_{sys}|z|\)
                              • From \(\rho = 0.6\) to \(\rho = 0.9\): \(\CoVaR_{1\%}\) falls from \(4.89\%\) to \(4.66\%\)
                                • because \(\rho + \sqrt{1 - \rho^2}\) peaks at \(\rho = 0.71\)

                              MES as an Euler Contribution: Proof (1/2)

                              Slide 104 in the PDF
                              • Portfolio \(X_p = \sum_i w_i X_i\) with a continuous distribution
                                • \(\ES_\alpha(X_p) = -\E[X_p \mid X_p \le q_\alpha(X_p)]\)
                                • \(\ES_\alpha\) is positively homogeneous of degree 1 in \(w\), so by Euler's theorem \(\ES_\alpha(X_p) = \sum_i w_i\,\partial \ES_\alpha / \partial w_i\)
                                • Write \(\ES_\alpha = -\frac{1}{\alpha}\E\left[X_p \mathbf{1}\{X_p \le q_\alpha(w)\}\right]\), with \(f_p\) the density of \(X_p\)
                                • The derivative has an interior and a boundary term: \(\dfrac{\partial}{\partial w_i}\E[X_p \mathbf{1}\{X_p \le q_\alpha\}] = \E[X_i \mathbf{1}\{X_p \le q_\alpha\}] + q_\alpha f_p(q_\alpha)\left(\dfrac{\partial q_\alpha}{\partial w_i} - \E[X_i \mid X_p = q_\alpha]\right)\)
                                • The quantile derivative is \(\partial q_\alpha/\partial w_i = \E[X_i \mid X_p = q_\alpha]\) Gouriéroux, Laurent & Scaillet (2000), so the boundary term is zero
                                • Hence \(\partial \ES_\alpha / \partial w_i = -\E[X_i \mid X_p \le q_\alpha(X_p)] = \MES_{i,\alpha}\) with \(m = p\) Gouriéroux, Laurent & Scaillet (2000), Scaillet (2004)

                              LRMES Simulation: Innovations and Recursions

                              Slide 106 in the PDF
                              • Standardised residuals \(\epsilon_{it} = r_{it}/\sigma_{it}\), \(\epsilon_{mt} = r_{mt}/\sigma_{mt}\)
                                • \(S\) is their sample correlation matrix, the long-run target of \(Q_t\)
                                • Bank innovation orthogonal to the market: \(\xi_{it} = (\epsilon_{it} - \rho_t\,\epsilon_{mt})/\sqrt{1 - \rho_t^2}\) Brownlees & Engle (2017)
                              • Each simulated day \(s\): draw a past date \(\tau\) and take the pair \((\xi_{i\tau}, \epsilon_{m\tau})\)
                                • rebuild the bank shock with the simulated correlation
                                • \(\epsilon_{i,t+s} = \rho_{t+s}\,\epsilon_{m\tau} + \sqrt{1 - \rho_{t+s}^2}\,\xi_{i\tau}\), then update \(\sigma^2\) and \(Q\) with the new returns

                                • in general, finite variance needs \(\alpha + \gamma\,\E[\epsilon^2\mathbf{1}\{\epsilon < 0\}] + \beta < 1\)
                                • DCC: \(a, b > 0\), \(a + b < 1\)
                                • Initialisation: \(Q_1 = S\) in estimation; the simulation starts from \(\sigma^2_{T+1}\) and \(Q_{T+1}\) filtered to the last date

                              Quantile Regression: Asymptotics

                              Slide 107 in the PDF
                              • Asymptotics for a fixed \(\alpha\), with \(\hat\theta_\alpha = (\hat a_\alpha, \hat b_\alpha)^\top\)
                                • \(n\) days, \(x_t = (1, X_{i,t})^\top\), \(f_t\): the conditional density of \(Y\) at its \(\alpha\)-quantile
                                • \(\sqrt{n}(\hat\theta_\alpha - \theta_\alpha) \Rightarrow N\left(0,\; \alpha(1 - \alpha)\,D_1^{-1} D_0 D_1^{-1}\right)\), \(D_0 = \E[x_t x_t^\top]\), \(D_1 = \E[f_t\, x_t x_t^\top]\)

                                • The sparsity \(1/f_t\) must be estimated (kernel or bandwidth)
                                • At \(\alpha = 1\%\) few observations lie near the quantile, so this estimate is noisy
                              • The slope \(b_\alpha\) can change with \(\alpha\)
                                • the bank may matter more for the system in the tail than in the centre

                                • \(\hat\beta = \arg\min_\beta \sum_t \left(y_t - \sum_i \beta_i x_{it}\right)^2 + \lambda \sum_i w_i\left(\tfrac{1}{2}|\beta_i| + \tfrac{1}{2}\beta_i^2\right)\), \(w_i = 1/|\hat\beta_{i,\mathrm{OLS}}|\)
                                • \(w_i\): adaptive weights, larger for coefficients small in OLS, re-estimated in each training sample
                                • The \(|\beta_i|\) part sets coefficients to zero, the \(\beta_i^2\) part shrinks them
                                • Rolling 150-day windows: plain elastic net, \(w_i = 1\), with \(p = 1\) (the order BIC selects in \(100\%\) of the windows) and \(H = 10\)
                              • Why: selection and shrinkage in the VAR coefficients
                                • without forcing a sparse network (links also come through \(\Sigma\))
                              • On the full sample we keep the BIC order of our OLS VAR, \(p = 2\)
                              • With about 4 000 days the penalty hardly matters
                                • it matters in short windows

                              Granger-Causality Networks and Multiple Testing (1/2)

                              Slide 117 in the PDF

                                • \(j\) Granger-causes \(i\) if the past of \(j\) improves the forecast of \(i\)
                                • In \(X_{i,t} = c + a X_{i,t-1} + g X_{j,t-1} + u_t\) we test \(H_0\colon g = 0\)
                                • Even with no links at all, about \(7.8\) edges would appear by chance

                              Granger-Causality Networks and Multiple Testing (2/2)

                              Slide 118 in the PDF
                              • Our tests: bivariate regressions, one lag, HAC Wald statistics (Newey–West, 9 lags)
                                • Daily returns: 58 edges at 5%, 19 after the Holm (1979) correction, 57 with the BH procedure at the nominal 5% level Benjamini & Hochberg (1995)
                                • Holm with 5 or 20 HAC lags: 21 and 21 edges; with heteroskedasticity-robust errors only: 33
                                • The Benjamini & Yekutieli (2001) procedure, valid under any dependence: 38 edges
                                • Log volatility, highly persistent: one extra lag that is not tested Toda & Yamamoto (1995), as in the lag-augmented tests of Shi, Hurn & Phillips (2020), valid only with a correct order \(p\): 153, 149 and 153 edges
                              • Holm controls the chance of any false edge under arbitrary dependence
                              • The FDR is the expected share of false edges among those drawn
                                • the BH (Benjamini–Hochberg) procedure guarantees it for independent tests Benjamini & Hochberg (1995)
                                • but tests on overlapping bank pairs are dependent: the 5% level of BH is only nominal here
                              • Daily returns of banks in different time zones

                              What the Researcher Must Check (1/2)

                              Slide 125 in the PDF
                              • Non-stationary inputs: a VAR on price levels, or on trending series, produces spurious connectedness
                                • Test: unit-root tests on every input
                                • All eigenvalues of the companion matrix of the fitted VAR strictly inside the unit circle in every window
                              • Ordering: Cholesky decompositions change with the order of the banks
                                • Test: permute the columns; the generalized table must not change
                              ↩ Back

                              What the Researcher Must Check (2/2)

                              Slide 126 in the PDF
                              • Look-ahead: a window labelled by its first date, or a lag order chosen on the full sample
                                • Test: the index at \(t\) must not move when data after \(t\) are deleted
                              • Overlap: rolling indices and forward outcomes overlap
                                • so OLS \(t\)-statistics are inflated
                                • Test: Newey–West lags at least equal to the overlap, or a block bootstrap
                              • Dimension: 13 banks and \(p = 2\) give 27 coefficients per equation from 250 days
                                • more banks need shrinkage Demirer et al. (2018)
                                • Test: compare with a LASSO VAR
                                • Unstable edges signal estimation noise, not structure
