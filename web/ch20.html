Gu et al. (2024) describe a recent window of a series as a path and summarise it by its path signature. Signature features enter a two-step LASSO regression, and past periods whose path resembles the current one receive a larger weight through a signature kernel. For freight rates, the authors report a more than fivefold accuracy gain over an industry forecast. The chapter asks whether the same method improves forecasts of daily realised variance (RV), where the benchmark is the heterogeneous autoregressive (HAR) model of Corsi (2009) and, above all, its log version, log-HAR.

The transfer is pre-registered: four hypotheses are fixed before the results, and the data are 50 assets from the VOLARE archive, 40 of them stocks. Each component of the method is tested separately, so that any gain can be attributed either to the features or to the weights. The comparisons use the QLIKE (quasi-likelihood) loss, asset-by-asset Diebold–Mariano tests and the Holm and Benjamini–Hochberg corrections for multiple testing.

The result is null. At the one-day horizon, Sig-L, the model with signature features, has a mean QLIKE loss 0.4% higher than log-HAR. Sig-L is significantly better than log-HAR on 3 of 50 assets without correction and on a single one after the Holm correction, while about 2.5 raw rejections are expected by chance alone. At one week and one month, the full model, Sig-LK, has a QLIKE loss 28.0% and 92.9% higher than log-HAR. The strongest effect in the study is the oldest one: log-HAR is significantly better than HAR in levels on 21 of 50 assets at one day, without correction. The chapter shows, through ablations and the mechanism, why the method does not transfer.

Realised Measures: What We Forecast

Realised Variance and Quadratic Variation (1/2)

Slide 5 in the PDF
  • Question: what exactly do we forecast, and how do intraday data measure the variance of a day?
  • The price model: the log price moves by a drift, a diffusion term and jumps
    \begin{equation*} dp_t = \mu_t\,dt + \sigma_t\,dW_t + dJ_t \end{equation*}
    • \(p_t\): log price at time \(t\) (time in days); \(\mu_t\): drift; \(\sigma_t\): spot volatility; \(W_t\): Brownian motion; \(J_t\): pure-jump process, \(\Delta J_s\) = jump size at time \(s\)
  • Realised variance: the sum of squared intraday returns of day \(t\) Barndorff-Nielsen & Shephard (2002)
    \begin{equation*} \RV_t = \sum_{i=1}^{M} r_{t,i}^2 \;\xrightarrow{\;M\to\infty\;}\; \int_{t-1}^{t}\sigma_s^2\,ds + \sum_{t-1<s\le t} (\Delta J_s)^2 \end{equation*}
    • \(M\): number of intraday returns in day \(t\) (for a US stock, 78 five-minute returns in the 6.5-hour session); \(r_{t,i}\): the \(i\)-th intraday log return of day \(t\)
    • The limit is the quadratic variation of day \(t\): the integrated variance plus the sum of squared jumps

Realised Variance and Quadratic Variation (2/2)

Slide 6 in the PDF
  • Without jumps and noise, on an equally spaced grid, the limit is the integrated variance
    • the error is mixed Normal
    \begin{equation*} \sqrt{M}\,(\RV_t - \mathrm{IV}_t) \Rightarrow \mathrm{MN}\!\left(0,\; 2\,\mathrm{IQ}_t\right), \qquad \mathrm{IV}_t = \int_{t-1}^{t}\sigma_s^2\,ds, \quad \mathrm{IQ}_t = \int_{t-1}^{t}\sigma_s^4\,ds \end{equation*}
    • \(\mathrm{IV}_t\): integrated variance, the true variance of day \(t\); \(\mathrm{IQ}_t\): integrated quarticity
    • \(\mathrm{MN}(0, V)\): mixed Normal, a Normal with a random variance \(V\); given the volatility path, the error is Normal with variance \(2\,\mathrm{IQ}_t/M\)
  • Reading: RV is a noisy measurement of \(\mathrm{IV}_t\)
    • the noise is largest on volatile days (where \(\mathrm{IQ}_t\) is large)
    • This is the measurement error that HARQ, the HAR model with realised quarticity, corrects for (next section)
  • Microstructure noise makes very fine sampling biased
    • five-minute returns are the standard compromise (Chapter 9)

Semivariances and Quarticity in VOLARE

Slide 7 in the PDF
  • Semivariances Barndorff-Nielsen, Kinnebrock & Shephard (2010): RV split by the sign of the intraday returns
    \begin{equation*} \RV_t^{+} = \sum_{i=1}^{M} r_{t,i}^2\,\mathbf{1}\{r_{t,i}>0\}, \qquad \RV_t^{-} = \sum_{i=1}^{M} r_{t,i}^2\,\mathbf{1}\{r_{t,i}<0\} \end{equation*}
    • \(\RV_t^{+}\): variance from up-moves, \(\RV_t^{-}\): variance from down-moves
    • They add up: \(\RV_t = \RV_t^{+} + \RV_t^{-}\)
  • Realised quarticity: an estimate of \(\mathrm{IQ}_t\), the scale of the measurement error of RV
    \begin{equation*} \mathrm{RQ}_t = \frac{M}{3}\sum_{i=1}^{M} r_{t,i}^4 \;\to\; \mathrm{IQ}_t \end{equation*}
    • The factor \(M/3\): a Normal return with variance \(s^2\) has fourth moment \(3s^4\), and each five-minute variance is about \(\mathrm{IV}_t/M\)
    • The limit needs a continuous, noise-free price and an equally spaced grid; a jump \(\Delta J\) adds about \(M(\Delta J)^4/3\), so raw RQ is jump-sensitive

Targets and Losses (1/2): the Target

Slide 8 in the PDF
  • The target at horizon \(h\) is the average RV over the next \(h\) days
    \begin{equation*} \RV_{t+1:t+h} = \frac{1}{h}\sum_{j=1}^{h}\RV_{t+j}, \qquad h \in \{1, 5, 22\} \end{equation*}
    • \(t\): the forecast origin (the last day observed); \(h = 1, 5, 22\): one day, one week, one month of trading days
  • Log models forecast \(y = \ln\RV_{t+1:t+h}\) and return to levels with a variance correction
    \begin{equation*} F_t = \exp(\hat y_t + \hat s^2/2) \end{equation*}
    • \(F_t\): forecast of \(\RV_{t+1:t+h}\) made at \(t\); \(\hat y_t\): fitted log forecast; \(\hat s^2\): variance of the regression residuals
    • \(+\hat s^2/2\): since \(\E[e^Z] = e^{\mu + s^2/2}\) for \(Z \sim N(\mu, s^2)\); exact if the log error is conditionally Normal with constant variance, otherwise an approximation
  • For \(h > 1\) targets overlap: this induces serial dependence
    • through lag \(h - 1\) by construction, possibly longer
    • so every test uses a HAC (heteroskedasticity and autocorrelation consistent) variance

Matching a Forecast to Its Target

Slide 9 in the PDF
  • In the target definition, the subscript \(t\) is the forecast origin
  • In loss and test formulas, the subscript labels a matched evaluation row
    • the realised target
    • the forecast made before that target became observable
  • One-day horizon: information through Monday close forecasts Tuesday RV
    • stored next to the Tuesday realisation
  • Five-day horizon: the same origin forecasts the average from Tuesday to the following Monday
    • the next origin gives an overlapping target
  • Forecast comparison tests use the same realised target for both models in each row
  • The row label is not permission to use that target in estimation

Targets and Losses (2/2): Robust Losses

Slide 10 in the PDF
  • A loss is robust Patton (2011)
    • for a conditionally unbiased proxy, the expected-loss ranking with \(\RV_t\) equals that with the true variance
    • Conditionally unbiased: \(\E_{t-1}[\RV_t] = \sigma^2_t\), with \(\E_{t-1}\) the expectation given information up to \(t - 1\) and \(\sigma^2_t\) the true variance of day \(t\)
  • The two robust losses used here (written for \(h = 1\); for \(h > 1\) replace \(\RV_t\) by the target)
    \begin{equation*} \mathrm{MSE}_t = (\RV_t - F_t)^2, \qquad \QL_t = \frac{\RV_t}{F_t} - \ln\frac{\RV_t}{F_t} - 1 \;\ge\; 0 \end{equation*}
    • MSE (mean squared error): the squared error; QLIKE (quasi-likelihood loss) is zero only if \(F_t = \RV_t\) and grows faster when the forecast is too low than when it is too high
    • Example with \(\RV_t = 1\): \(F_t = 0.5\) gives \(\QL_t = 2 - \ln 2 - 1 = 0.307\), \(F_t = 2\) gives \(0.5 - \ln 0.5 - 1 = 0.193\)
  • QLIKE depends on the ratio \(\RV_t/F_t\)
    • the same proportional error has the same loss on a calm day as on a stormy day
  • MSE is dominated by a few crisis days
    • QLIKE is the primary loss, MSE the secondary one

    Benchmarks: the HAR Family

    HAR: Three Averages of the Past (1/2)

    Slide 15 in the PDF
    • Question: which simple benchmark must be beaten before we believe a new method?
    • The HAR model Corsi (2009): future RV is a linear function of the daily, weekly and monthly averages of past RV
      \begin{equation*} \RV_{t+1:t+h} = \beta_0 + \beta_d\,\RV_t + \beta_w\,\RV_t^{(w)} + \beta_m\,\RV_t^{(m)} + \varepsilon_{t+h} \end{equation*}
      \begin{equation*} \RV_t^{(w)} = \frac{1}{5}\sum_{j=0}^{4}\RV_{t-j}, \qquad \RV_t^{(m)} = \frac{1}{22}\sum_{j=0}^{21}\RV_{t-j} \end{equation*}
    • Notation
      • \(\RV_t^{(w)}\), \(\RV_t^{(m)}\): weekly (5-day) and monthly (22-day) averages of RV up to day \(t\); \(\varepsilon_{t+h}\): forecast error
      • \(\beta_0\): intercept; \(\beta_d, \beta_w, \beta_m \ge 0\) in practice: how much yesterday, last week and last month weigh; estimated by OLS for each horizon \(h\)
      • \(\beta_d + \beta_w + \beta_m\) close to 1: high persistence of volatility
    • Traders at daily, weekly and monthly frequencies
      • a cascade that mimics long memory with three parameters

    HAR: Three Averages of the Past (2/2)

    Slide 16 in the PDF
    • HAR is a restricted AR(22): the 22 lag coefficients take only three distinct values
      \begin{equation*} \phi_1 = \beta_d + \frac{\beta_w}{5} + \frac{\beta_m}{22}, \qquad \phi_{2..5} = \frac{\beta_w}{5} + \frac{\beta_m}{22}, \qquad \phi_{6..22} = \frac{\beta_m}{22} \end{equation*}
      • \(\phi_j\): the coefficient of \(\RV_{t+1-j}\) in an AR(22) (autoregression with 22 lags)
    • log-HAR: the same regression in logs
      \begin{equation*} \ln\RV_{t+1:t+h} = \beta_0 + \beta_d \ln\RV_t + \beta_w \ln\RV^{(w)}_t + \beta_m \ln\RV^{(m)}_t + \varepsilon_{t+h} \end{equation*}
      • No negative forecasts, less driven by spikes
      • Logs of averages are not averages of logs, so this log-HAR is not an exact restricted AR(22) in \(\ln\RV\) (Seminar 20, A7)

    Path Signatures

    A Window of Data Is a Path

    Slide 18 in the PDF
    • Question: how do we summarise a 22-day window so that the order of the moves is kept, not only their levels?
    • Take the last \(L\) days of \(d - 1\) series
      • add time as the first channel (time augmentation)
      • connect the points linearly
      \begin{equation*} X: [0, 1] \to \mathbb{R}^{d}, \qquad X_{u_i} = \Big(u_i,\; \ln \RV_{t-L+1+i},\; \textstyle\sum_{j \le t-L+1+i} r_j\Big), \qquad u_i = \frac{i}{L-1},\; i = 0, \dots, L - 1 \end{equation*}
      • \(L\): window length in days (here 22); \(d\): number of channels (here \(d = 3\): time, log RV, cumulative return); \(u_i \in [0, 1]\): rescaled time of the \(i\)-th point; \(r_j = \ln(C_j/O_j)\): open-to-close log return of day \(j\), with \(O_j\), \(C_j\) the open and close prices
    • A regression on the window sees 22 lags; a path summary sees their order, speed and co-movement
      • Example: RV rose while prices fell, or RV rose first and prices fell later

    The Signature: Definition (1/2)

    Slide 20 in the PDF
    • Why iterated integrals
      • level 1 keeps only the total increments
      • integrals over ordered times keep the order in which the channels move
    • One signature term is an iterated integral of the path along a word of channels
      \begin{equation*} S(X)^{i_1 \dots i_k}_{a,b} = \int_{a < u_1 < \dots < u_k < b} dX^{i_1}_{u_1}\cdots dX^{i_k}_{u_k} \end{equation*}
    • Notation
      • \(X: [a, b] \to \mathbb{R}^d\): a path of bounded variation (finite total length), e.g. piecewise linear; \(X^i_u\): its channel \(i\) at time \(u\)
      • Word \((i_1, \dots, i_k)\): an ordered list of \(k\) channels, letters in \(\{1, \dots, d\}\); \(k\) is the level of the term
      • \(u_1 < \dots < u_k\): the integral runs over ordered times; \(dX^{i}_{u}\): increment of channel \(i\) at time \(u\) (for a linear segment, slope times \(du\))

    The Signature: Definition (2/2)

    Slide 21 in the PDF
    • The signature is the whole sequence of levels
      \begin{equation*} \Sig(X) = \big(1,\; S^{(1)},\; S^{(2)},\; S^{(3)}, \dots\big), \qquad S^{(k)} \in (\mathbb{R}^d)^{\otimes k} \end{equation*}
      • \(S^{(k)}\): the \(d^k\) terms of level \(k\), one for each word of length \(k\), stored as a \(k\)-dimensional array (a tensor; \(\otimes\): tensor product)
      • \(\Sig^N(X)\): the truncated signature, levels \(0\) to \(N\); \(N\) is the depth
    • Level 1: the total increments of the channels
      \begin{equation*} S^{i} = X^{i}_b - X^{i}_a \end{equation*}
    • Level 2: increments of channel \(j\) weighted by how far channel \(i\) has already moved
      \begin{equation*} S^{ij} = \int_a^b (X^{i}_u - X^{i}_a)\,dX^{j}_u \end{equation*}
      • Each level adds one integral and records finer details of the order of the moves

    Linear Segments and Chen' s Identity (1/2)

    Slide 22 in the PDF
    • A linear segment with increment \(\Delta \in \mathbb{R}^d\) has the tensor exponential as signature
      \begin{equation*} \Sig(\text{segment}) = \exp_{\otimes}(\Delta) = \Big(1,\; \Delta,\; \tfrac{1}{2!}\Delta^{\otimes 2},\; \tfrac{1}{3!}\Delta^{\otimes 3}, \dots\Big) \end{equation*}
      • \(\Delta = X_b - X_a\): the increment of the segment; \(\Delta^{\otimes k}\): the array of all products \(\Delta^{i_1}\cdots\Delta^{i_k}\), so a level-\(k\) term is \(\Delta^{i_1}\cdots\Delta^{i_k}/k!\)
    • Chen' s identity Chen (1957)
      • the signature of two paths run one after the other is the tensor product of their signatures
      \begin{equation*} \Sig(X * Y) = \Sig(X) \otimes \Sig(Y), \qquad \big(A \otimes B\big)^{(k)} = \sum_{p=0}^{k} A^{(k-p)} \otimes B^{(p)} \end{equation*}
      • \(X * Y\): concatenation, \(Y\) starts where \(X\) ends; \(A^{(k)}\), \(B^{(k)}\): level \(k\) of the signatures \(A\), \(B\), with \(A^{(0)} = B^{(0)} = 1\)
      • Level \(k\) of the whole path collects every way of splitting a word of length \(k\) between the first and the second piece

    Linear Segments and Chen' s Identity (2/2)

    Slide 23 in the PDF
    • A piecewise-linear path with \(m\) segments, increments \(\Delta_1, \dots, \Delta_m\)
      \begin{equation*} \Sig = \exp_{\otimes}(\Delta_1) \otimes \cdots \otimes \exp_{\otimes}(\Delta_m) \end{equation*}
      • This is the whole algorithm: \(m\) updates, each costing \(O(d^N)\) at depth \(N\)
    • Rolling windows: Chen also removes an initial piece
      \begin{equation*} \Sig(X_{[s,t]}) = \Sig(X_{[a,s]})^{-1} \otimes \Sig(X_{[a,t]}) \end{equation*}
      • \(X_{[s,t]}\): the path restricted to \([s, t]\), with \(a \le s \le t\); \((\cdot)^{-1}\): inverse for \(\otimes\), the signature of the piece run backwards

    Worked Example: a Two-Segment Path

    Slide 24 in the PDF
    • The path \(X: (0,0) \to (1,0) \to (1,2)\), with increments \(\Delta_1 = (1, 0)\) and \(\Delta_2 = (0, 2)\)
    • Level 1: \(S^{(1)} = \Delta_1 + \Delta_2 = (1, 2)\)
    • Level 2 by Chen' s identity: \(S^{ij} = \tfrac12\Delta_1^i\Delta_1^j + \Delta_1^i\Delta_2^j + \tfrac12\Delta_2^i\Delta_2^j\)
      • \(\Delta_m^i\) is the component \(i\) of the increment of segment \(m\)
    • Result: \(S^{11} = \tfrac12\), \(\;S^{12} = 0 + 1\cdot 2 + 0 = 2\), \(\;S^{21} = 0\), \(\;S^{22} = \tfrac12\cdot 2^2 = 2\)
    • Check: \(S^{11} = (\Delta X^1)^2/2\) and \(S^{22} = (\Delta X^2)^2/2\)
      • shuffle identity: the product of two level-1 coordinates equals the sum of their two orderings at level 2
    • \(S^{12} \neq S^{21}\): the order matters, since channel 1 moves in the first segment and channel 2 in the second
    • The total displacement (level 1) cannot tell this path from the one that goes up first and right second
      • both have \(\Delta X = (1, 2)\); level 2 tells them apart

    The Lévy Area: Order as a Number

    Slide 25 in the PDF
    • The Lévy area \(A^{ij}\): the antisymmetric part of level 2
      \begin{equation*} A^{ij} = \tfrac12\big(S^{ij} - S^{ji}\big) \end{equation*}
      • Equals the signed area between the path, projected on channels \((i, j)\), and its chord (the straight line from start to end)
      • \(A^{ij} > 0\): the path turns anticlockwise in the \((i, j)\) plane
      • The lead-lag reading needs a positive association: if \(i\) falls and then \(j\) rises, \(i\) moves first, yet \(A^{ij} < 0\)
    • Symmetric part: \(\tfrac12(S^{ij} + S^{ji}) = \tfrac12\,\Delta X^i\,\Delta X^j\), no new information
      • Worked example: \(A^{12} = (2 - 0)/2 = 1\), the area of the triangle between the path and the chord
    • Channels (return, log RV): a price fall followed by a volatility rise gives \(A < 0\)
      • so does a price rise followed by a volatility fall
    • A lead–lag statistic
      • leverage-type sequences (Chapter 5) are one contributor to a negative area
      • the sign alone does not identify them

    The Signature Kernel

    Slide 26 in the PDF
    • Kernel = inner product of the truncated signatures Király & Oberhauser (2019): a similarity score between two paths
      \begin{equation*} k_N(X, Y) = \langle \Sig^N(X), \Sig^N(Y) \rangle = 1 + \sum_{k=1}^{N}\sum_{|w| = k} S(X)^{w} S(Y)^{w} \end{equation*}
      • \(X\), \(Y\): two paths with the same channels; \(w\): a word, \(|w|\): its length (level); \(S(X)^{w}\): the term of word \(w\); the leading 1 is level 0
    • Distance induced by the kernel (Gu et al., Eq. 9)
      • squared Euclidean distance between the truncated signatures
      \begin{equation*} d_{\Sig}(X, Y) = k_N(X, X) - 2\,k_N(X, Y) + k_N(Y, Y) = \|\Sig^N(X) - \Sig^N(Y)\|^2 \end{equation*}
      • \(d_{\Sig} = 0\): the two windows have the same signature up to depth \(N\); large \(d_{\Sig}\): different shapes
    • Gu et al. and this chapter use this truncated kernel, with \(N\) as for the features
      • The untruncated kernel solves a Goursat PDE (partial differential equation) Salvi et al. (2021); the paper cites it but does not use it
    • Channels are rescaled before the kernel (Appendix): otherwise the channel with the largest units dominates

    The Method: Two-Step LASSO

    Slide 29 in the PDF
    • Step 1, selection by a weighted LASSO Tibshirani (1996) (Eq. 14)
      \begin{equation*} \hat\theta_{\mathrm{L}} = \arg\min_\theta \sum_{\tau} w_\tau\big(y_{\tau+\Delta t} - \xi_\tau^\top\theta\big)^2 + \lambda\|\theta\|_1 \end{equation*}
      • \(\theta\): coefficient vector, \(\|\theta\|_1 = \sum_j |\theta_j|\); \(w_\tau \ge 0\): weight of week \(\tau\) (equal, or from the kernel, next slide)
      • \(\lambda \ge 0\): penalty; a larger \(\lambda\) generally favours sparser solutions; \(\lambda = 0\) is weighted OLS
    • Step 2, refit by weighted OLS on the selected support only, the nonzero coefficients of step 1 (Eq. 15)
      \begin{equation*} \hat\theta = \arg\min_{\mathrm{supp}(\theta) = \mathrm{supp}(\hat\theta_{\mathrm{L}})} \sum_{\tau} w_\tau\big(y_{\tau+\Delta t} - \xi_\tau^\top\theta\big)^2 \end{equation*}
    • Why refit: LASSO shrinks the kept coefficients towards zero
      • OLS after selection removes the penalty-induced shrinkage on the selected support Belloni & Chernozhukov (2013)
      • the refit does not remove selection or omitted-variable bias
      • The paper calls it “adaptive” because of the weights \(w_\tau\), not in the sense of the adaptive LASSO of Zou (2006)
    • Choice of \(\lambda\) in this chapter: the support with the smallest BIC Efron et al. (2004)
      • BIC: Bayesian Information Criterion
      • along the LARS (Least Angle Regression) path
      • in each training window (Appendix)

    The Method: Kernel Weights

    Slide 30 in the PDF
    • Distance of each past window to the last window with an observed target (Eq. 11)
      \begin{equation*} \delta_\tau = d_{\Sig, N}\big(z_{\tau - l : \tau},\; z_{t - \Delta t - l : t - \Delta t}\big) \end{equation*}
      • \(z_\tau = (x_\tau, y_{\tau+\Delta t})\): the pairs of predictors and later target, not the features \(\xi_\tau\) (Algorithm 1); \(z_{\tau - l : \tau}\): their path over \(l\) weeks
      • \(t\): the current week; \(t - \Delta t\): the last week whose target \(y_t\) is already observed; \(d_{\Sig, N}\): the signature distance at depth \(N\)
    • Softmax weights with temperature \(\gamma \ge 0\) (Eq. 12): similar windows (small \(\delta_\tau\)) get more weight
      \begin{equation*} w_\tau = \frac{\exp(-\gamma\,\delta_\tau)}{\sum_{s} \exp(-\gamma\,\delta_s)} \end{equation*}
      • \(s\): runs over all training weeks, so the weights sum to 1; \(\gamma = 0\): ordinary (equal-weight) regression; \(\gamma \to \infty\): only the most similar past window
    • A nearest-neighbour idea inside a regression: learn from the past periods that looked like today
      • Cost of the idea: fewer effective observations, \(n_{\mathrm{eff}} = 1 / \sum_\tau w_\tau^2\) (Kish); \(n_{\mathrm{eff}} = n\) for equal weights \(1/n\), \(n_{\mathrm{eff}} = 1\) if one week takes all the weight

    Kernel Temperature: One Choice per Class, on a Validation Block

    Slide 35 in the PDF
    • Scale-free temperature, set in every training window
      \begin{equation*} \gamma = \frac{c}{\mathrm{median}_\tau(\delta_\tau)} \end{equation*}

    • For the 40 stocks the smallest temperature, \(c = 0.5\), wins at every horizon for Sig-LK

    Evaluation Protocol (1/3): the DM Test

    Slide 37 in the PDF
    • Question: is the loss difference between two models larger than sampling noise?
    • Per asset and horizon: mean QLIKE and MSE Diebold & Mariano (1995)
      • compared by the DM (Diebold–Mariano) test of equal predictive accuracy
      • the paper' s relative error is reported descriptively only (Appendix)
      \begin{equation*} d_t = L(\RV_t, F^{A}_t) - L(\RV_t, F^{B}_t), \qquad \mathrm{DM} = \frac{\bar d}{\sqrt{\hat\Omega / T}} \end{equation*}
      • \(L\): the loss (QLIKE or MSE); \(F^{A}_t\), \(F^{B}_t\): forecasts of models \(A\) and \(B\); \(d_t\): their loss differential
      • \(\bar d\): the mean of \(d_t\) over the \(T\) evaluation days; \(\hat\Omega\): the long-run variance of \(d_t\) (Further Topics)
    • Under \(H_0\colon \E[d_t] = 0\), DM is approximately \(N(0, 1)\)
      • \(\mathrm{DM} < -1.645\) means model \(A\) is significantly more accurate at 5% (one-sided)
    • Rolling estimation, so the test is read as the unconditional GW (Giacomini–White) test
      • The conditional GW test also asks whether \(d_{t-h}\) predicts \(d_t\) (instruments \((1, d_{t-h})\)) Giacomini & White (2006)

    Evaluation Protocol (2/3): the Model Confidence Set

    Slide 38 in the PDF

                Case Study: The Signature as a Lead-Lag Metric

                The Question: The Signature as a Lead-Lag Metric

                Slide 60 in the PDF
                • Question: does one asset move before another, and can the signature of their joint path measure it?
                  • lead-lag relations show in which market information appears first (price discovery)
                • Bennett, Cucuringu & Reinert (2022), Machine Learning
                  • the pairwise lead-lag relations form a directed network, which is then split into clusters of leaders and followers
                  • data: the 434 NYSE (New York Stock Exchange) stocks with the highest volume, daily returns 2000–2019
                • One of the metrics compared: twice the Lévy area of the path of normalised log prices over \([t - m, t]\)
                  \begin{equation*} S^{ij} = 2A^{ij} = \iint_{t-m<u<v<t} \big(dX^i_u\, dX^j_v - dX^j_u\, dX^i_v\big) \end{equation*}
                  • \(S^{ij} > 0\): moves of \(i\) are followed by moves of \(j\) in the same direction
                  • unlike cross-correlations, it needs no choice of a maximum lag
                  • it assumes a positive association: it cannot tell “\(i\) leads, with negative association” from “\(j\) leads, with positive association”

                What the Paper Found, and with Which Procedure

                Slide 61 in the PDF
                • The empirical results of the paper come from a metric other than the signature (published version, Sections 5–6)
                  • the metric: ccf-auc with distance correlation, with lags of up to 5 days
                  • the network clustering: Hermitian RW (random walk)
                • The lead-lag clusters are significant in a permutation test that shuffles the order of the days
                  • the permutation destroys both the lagged dependence between assets and the autocorrelation of each asset: on its own, the test does not separate them
                • In the authors' comparisons the clusters do not match SIC (Standard Industrial Classification) sectors
                  • their correlations with volume and with market capitalisation are small
                • The paper's numbers (Section 6.3): the signal built on these clusters has an annualised Sharpe ratio of 0.62
                  • the S&P 500 has 0.40 over the same period
                  • both are before transaction costs

                  The Method in Brief and How the Replication Differs

                  Slide 63 in the PDF
                  • The path of the day: cumulative sums of the SPY and BTC returns, each return divided by the standard deviation of its asset on that day
                    • 78 returns: from the open of the 09:30 bar to the close of each bar 09:30–15:55
                    • the normalisation the authors recommend: otherwise the area grows with volatility
                  • The Lévy area of a window of \(m\) steps is a sum of lagged products of the returns \(x_i\) (SPY) and \(y_i\) (BTC)
                    \begin{equation*} A_m = \tfrac12 \sum_{l=1}^{m-1} \sum_{i} \big(x_i\, y_{i+l} - y_i\, x_{i+l}\big) \end{equation*}
                    • lag \(l\) appears \(m - l\) times: a window of 2 steps (10 minutes) keeps lag 1 only
                    • a long window adds many lags without effect, hence noise
                  • Daily statistic: the mean area over all windows of \(m\) steps in the day
                    • the test: NW \(t\) with 8 lags on the 489 daily values
                  • Differences from the paper
                    • two assets instead of 434, and five-minute bars instead of daily returns
                    • no network clustering and no trading signal

                  The Result: SPY Leads by Five Minutes, and the Window Matters

                  Slide 64 in the PDF
                  • SPY moves before Bitcoin by one five-minute step
                    • the correlation at lag \(+1\) is 0.088 (NW \(t\) = 9.8), and at lag \(-1\) it is \(-\)0.005 (\(t\) = \(-\)0.71)
                    • the direct test of direction: the difference between the correlations at lags \(+1\) and \(-1\) is 0.093 (NW \(t\) = 9.5)
                    • the contemporaneous correlation is 0.40; at lags \(+2\) to \(+6\) the correlations are small and negative (between \(-\)0.016 and \(-\)0.004)
                  • The signature metric finds the effect only in short windows
                    • 10 minutes: \(t\) = 9.6, and the area is positive on 70% of the days
                    • 2 hours: \(t\) = 3.0; the whole session (390 minutes): \(t\) = 1.51, area positive on 51% of the days
                  • The analysis is exploratory: 20 tests (12 non-zero lags and 8 windows)
                    • Holm-adjusted \(p\): 0.032 for the 2-hour window, 0.157 for 195 minutes

                  What the Lead-Lag Result Supports, and Its Limits

                  Slide 65 in the PDF
                  • What the result supports: a five-minute lead-lag association between two assets, not a causal relation and not a profitable signal
                    • no trading costs and no out-of-sample evaluation
                  • The sign of the area measures the orientation of the path; reading it as “SPY leads” assumes a positive association between the two assets
                  • Limits
                    • a one-step lag also arises from unsynchronised timestamps of the two sources; separating the two needs tick data
                    • the window length \(m\) plays the role of the maximum lag: the signature does not remove this choice
                    • the NW \(t\)-test needs finite moments and weak dependence of the daily statistic tested (bounded correlations, normalised areas), not of the raw returns

                  Two Refinements (1/2): HARQ

                  Slide 75 in the PDF
                  • RV measures the true variance with error
                    \begin{equation*} \RV_t = \mathrm{IV}_t + \eta_t, \qquad \Var(\eta_t) \approx 2\,\mathrm{IQ}_t / M \end{equation*}
                    • \(\eta_t\): measurement error; errors-in-variables attenuate (pull towards zero) \(\beta_d\), most on noisy days
                  • HARQ Bollerslev, Patton & Quaedvlieg (2016): the weight on yesterday' s RV falls when RV is measured less precisely
                    \begin{equation*} \RV_{t+1:t+h} = \beta_0 + \big(\beta_d + \beta_Q(\mathrm{RQ}_t^{1/2} - \overline{\mathrm{RQ}^{1/2}})\big)\RV_t + \beta_w\RV_t^{(w)} + \beta_m\RV_t^{(m)} + \varepsilon_{t+h} \end{equation*}
                    • \(\mathrm{RQ}_t^{1/2}\): square root of realised quarticity, a measure of the error of \(\RV_t\); \(\overline{\mathrm{RQ}^{1/2}}\): its in-sample mean, so \(\beta_d\) is the weight on an average day
                    • Expected \(\beta_Q < 0\): on a noisy day (\(\mathrm{RQ}_t\) above average) yesterday' s RV gets less weight
                  • BPQ (Bollerslev–Patton–Quaedvlieg) filter (“insanity filter”)
                    • a forecast outside the in-sample range of the target is replaced by the in-sample mean

                  Two Refinements (2/2): SHAR

                  Slide 76 in the PDF
                  • SHAR Patton & Sheppard (2015): split the daily term by the sign of the intraday returns
                    \begin{equation*} \RV_{t+1:t+h} = \beta_0 + \beta^{+}\RV_t^{+} + \beta^{-}\RV_t^{-} + \beta_w\RV_t^{(w)} + \beta_m\RV_t^{(m)} + \varepsilon_{t+h} \end{equation*}

                  Why Signatures Work (1/2): Uniqueness and Shuffles

                  Slide 79 in the PDF
                  • Uniqueness Hambly & Lyons (2010): the full signature identifies the path
                    • \(\Sig(X)\) determines \(X\) up to tree-like equivalence (adding pieces that are retraced backwards) and translation
                    • With a strictly increasing time channel nothing can be retraced, so only translation remains
                    • A finite truncation \(\Sig^N\) is not injective: two different paths can share levels \(1..N\)
                  • Shuffle identity: a product of signature terms is a sum of higher terms
                    \begin{equation*} S^{i}S^{j} = S^{ij} + S^{ji}, \qquad S^{ij}S^{k} = S^{ijk} + S^{ikj} + S^{kij} \end{equation*}
                    • The sum runs over all interleavings (shuffles) of the two words that keep the order inside each word
                    • So a linear model in \(\Sig^N(X)\) already contains polynomials of the increments

                  Why Signatures Work (2/2): Universality and Decay

                  Slide 80 in the PDF
                  • Universality: any continuous function of the path is approximately linear in the signature
                    • Stone–Weierstrass, via the shuffle identity
                    \begin{equation*} \forall \varepsilon > 0\;\; \exists\, \ell \text{ linear}: \quad \sup_{X \in K} \big|f(X) - \langle \ell, \Sig(X)\rangle\big| < \varepsilon \end{equation*}
                    • \(K\): a compact set of time-augmented paths with a common start (in the 1-variation topology); \(f: K \to \mathbb{R}\): a continuous function of the path
                    • \(\langle \ell, \Sig(X)\rangle\): a weighted sum of finitely many signature terms, i.e. a linear regression on them
                    • Sufficient depth permits nonlinear approximation; this does not guarantee accuracy at depth 3 or out of sample. The cost is \(d^N\) features, so sparsity is needed
                  • Factorial decay: high levels are small
                    \begin{equation*} \|S^{(k)}\| \le \frac{\|X\|_{1\text{-var}}^k}{k!} \end{equation*}
                    • \(\|X\|_{1\text{-var}}\): total length of the path (sum of the lengths of its segments); \(\|S^{(k)}\|\): size (Euclidean norm) of level \(k\)
                    • Truncating at a small depth \(N\) loses little for paths of moderate length, which is why channels are rescaled (Appendix)

                  Two Invariances and the Basepoint

                  Slide 82 in the PDF
                  • Translation invariance: \(\Sig(X + c) = \Sig(X)\) for any constant vector \(c \in \mathbb{R}^d\)
                    • only increments enter
                    • The signature does not see the level of volatility, so the level enters separately: the log-HAR terms play the role of the external factors \(x_\tau\) of Gu et al.
                  • Basepoint augmentation Morrill et al. (2020): the path starts at the origin, then runs through the points of the window
                    \begin{equation*} \hat X = \big(0,\; X_{u_0},\; X_{u_1}, \dots, X_{u_{L-1}}\big), \qquad \Sig(\hat X) = \exp_{\otimes}(X_{u_0}) \otimes \Sig(X) \end{equation*}
                    • Level 1 becomes \(X_{u_0} + (X_{u_{L-1}} - X_{u_0}) = X_{u_{L-1}}\): the starting point does not appear on its own, but the higher-order terms depend on the absolute level (for example \(\ln\RV\)), so the signature becomes level-aware
                    • Translating the path to start at 0 does not do this: it is itself a translation (Seminar 20, C2 uses the prepended segment)
                  • Reparametrisation invariance: \(X_{\psi(u)}\), with \(\psi\) increasing, has the same signature
                    • without a time channel, two moves with the same spatial shape, run at different speeds, have the same signature
                    • the time channel tells them apart by referring them to a fixed clock; the augmented path stays invariant to a reparametrisation of the whole augmented path
                    • \(u_i = i/(L-1)\) maps every window to \([0, 1]\): the absolute duration (22 vs 2 days) needs physical time or a separate regressor

                  Evaluation Protocol: the HAC Variance

                  Slide 84 in the PDF
                  • Why not the plain sample variance: \(d_t\) is autocorrelated
                    • overlapping targets up to lag \(h - 1\)
                    • volatility clustering in the losses
                    • so \(\hat\gamma_0/T\) understates the uncertainty of \(\bar d\) (and over-rejects) when the sum of the autocovariances is positive, the typical case with overlapping targets
                  • The Newey–West estimator Newey & West (1987), heteroskedasticity and autocorrelation consistent (HAC)
                    \begin{equation*} \hat\Omega = \hat\gamma_0 + 2\sum_{j=1}^{q}\Big(1 - \frac{j}{q+1}\Big)\hat\gamma_j, \qquad \hat\gamma_j = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=j+1}^{T}(d_t - \bar d)(d_{t-j} - \bar d) \end{equation*}
                    • \(\hat\gamma_j\): the sample autocovariance of \(d_t\) at lag \(j\)
                    • The Bartlett weights \(1 - j/(q+1)\) decline linearly with the lag and guarantee \(\hat\Omega \ge 0\)
                    • \(q = \max(h, \lceil T^{1/3}\rceil)\) lags: at least \(h\), for the dependence created by the overlap (\(\lceil\cdot\rceil\): rounding up)

                  Features and Scaling

                  Slide 92 in the PDF
                  Route Decay Kernel
                  • Regressors at day \(\tau\) for the signature models: 3 log-HAR terms + 39 signature terms
                    • \(\xi_\tau = \big[\ln\RV_\tau,\; \ln\RV^{(w)}_\tau,\; \ln\RV^{(m)}_\tau,\; \Sig^3(X_{\tau-21:\tau})\big]\), path \(X\) = (time, \(\ln\RV\), cumulative \(r\)), 22 points
                  • Each non-time channel \(q\) is multiplied by \(\kappa_q = 1/(s_q\sqrt{L - 1})\)
                    • so its typical total move over the window is about 1
                    • \(s_q\): standard deviation of the daily increments of channel \(q\) from the start of the training window up to the origin \(t\) (\(W + h\) days)
                    • \(L - 1 = 21\): the number of increments in one path, used only in the factor \(\sqrt{L - 1}\)
                    • Time is not rescaled (constant increments, \(s = 0\)): it runs from 0 to 1; a word \((i_1..i_k)\) is rescaled by \(\prod_j \kappa_{i_j}\)
                  • Before the LASSO every column is standardised with weighted moments of the training window
                    • constant columns (words of time only) are dropped
                  • Raw features at \(\tau\) use data up to \(\tau\)
                    • scales and standardisation use data up to the origin \(t\)
                    • the no-look-ahead cutoff is \(t\)

                  Estimation: Choosing \(\lambda\) Without Look-Ahead

                  Slide 93 in the PDF
                  • Inside each training window: the whole weighted LASSO path by LARS
                    • For every support on the path: weighted OLS refit (step 2 of Gu et al.)
                  • Pick the support that minimises a BIC-type criterion with the Kish effective sample size
                    • a heuristic of this design
                    • \(\mathrm{BIC}(S) = n_{\mathrm{eff}}\ln \hat s^2_S + (|S| + 1)\ln n_{\mathrm{eff}}\), \(\quad n_{\mathrm{eff}} = 1/\sum_\tau w_\tau^2\)
                    • \(S\): a support (set of kept regressors), \(|S|\): its size, \(+1\) for the intercept; \(\hat s^2_S\): weighted residual variance of the refit on \(S\)
                  • Why not cross-validation: 50 assets \(\times\) 3 horizons \(\times\) hundreds of re-estimations
                    • BIC uses only the window
                    • Kish size corrects for unequal weights, not for serial dependence (overlapping targets, \(h > 1\)): not a likelihood-based BIC for these data

                  Slide 98 in the PDF
