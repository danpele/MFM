Gu et al. (2024) describe a recent window of a series as a path and summarise it by its path signature. Signature features enter a two-step LASSO regression, and past periods whose path resembles the current one receive a larger weight through a signature kernel. For freight rates, the authors report a more than fivefold accuracy gain over an industry forecast. The chapter asks whether the same method improves forecasts of daily realised variance (RV), where the benchmark is the heterogeneous autoregressive (HAR) model of Corsi (2009) and, above all, its log version, log-HAR.

The transfer is pre-registered: four hypotheses are fixed before the results, and the data are 50 assets from the VOLARE archive, 40 of them stocks. Each component of the method is tested separately, so that any gain can be attributed either to the features or to the weights. The comparisons use the QLIKE (quasi-likelihood) loss, asset-by-asset Diebold–Mariano tests and the Holm and Benjamini–Hochberg corrections for multiple testing.

The result is null. At the one-day horizon, Sig-L, the model with signature features, has a mean QLIKE loss 0.4% higher than log-HAR. Sig-L is significantly better than log-HAR on 3 of 50 assets without correction and on a single one after the Holm correction, while about 2.5 raw rejections are expected by chance alone. At one week and one month, the full model, Sig-LK, has a QLIKE loss 28.0% and 92.9% higher than log-HAR. The strongest effect in the study is the oldest one: log-HAR is significantly better than HAR in levels on 21 of 50 assets at one day, without correction. The chapter shows, through ablations and the mechanism, why the method does not transfer.

Route and Motivation

The chapter tests whether a forecasting method published for freight rates improves forecasts of daily realised variance. The method has two ingredients, signature features of a recent data window and kernel weights that favour similar past windows, and each is tested on its own against the HAR family of benchmarks. The transfer is pre-registered: the hypotheses, the panel and the tests are fixed before the results are seen.

Why Forecast Realised Variance?

Slide 3 in the PDF

Volatility is an input to almost every risk measure of the course: tail-risk measures in Chapter 7, option prices in Chapter 12 and portfolio weights in Chapter 4. Intraday data make the daily variance almost observable through realised variance (RV), the measure of Chapter 9. Volatility forecasting therefore becomes a regression problem with a noisy but model-free target.

The benchmark is hard to beat. The HAR (heterogeneous autoregressive) model of Corsi (2009) uses three averages of past RV, is linear and is estimated by ordinary least squares (OLS) in a second. A new method has to beat HAR out of sample, on many assets and with a proper test, before it can be said to improve volatility forecasts.

Realised Measures: What We Forecast

Before any model is compared, the target must be defined. This section states the price model behind realised variance, the measurement error of RV and the auxiliary measures in the VOLARE data, and then fixes the forecast target, the alignment of forecasts with targets and the loss functions used to rank the models.

Realised Variance and Quadratic Variation (1/2)

Slide 5 in the PDF

The question is what exactly is forecast, and how intraday data measure the variance of a day. The log price \(p_t\), with time in days, moves by a drift, a diffusion term and jumps:

\[dp_t = \mu_t\,dt + \sigma_t\,dW_t + dJ_t,\]
(20.1)

where \(\mu_t\) is the drift, \(\sigma_t\) the spot volatility, \(W_t\) a Brownian motion and \(J_t\) a pure-jump process, with \(\Delta J_s\) the jump size at time \(s\).

Realised variance is the sum of squared intraday returns of day \(t\) (Barndorff-Nielsen & Shephard, 2002):

\[\mathrm{RV}_t = \sum_{i=1}^{M} r_{t,i}^2 \;\xrightarrow{\;M\to\infty\;}\; \int_{t-1}^{t}\sigma_s^2\,ds + \sum_{t-1<s\le t} (\Delta J_s)^2,\]
(20.2)

where \(M\) is the number of intraday returns in day \(t\) and \(r_{t,i}\) the \(i\)-th intraday log return; for a US stock, \(M = 78\) five-minute returns cover the 6.5-hour session. The limit in (20.2) is the quadratic variation of the day: the integrated variance plus the sum of squared jumps.

In brief

  • Question: what exactly do we forecast, and how do intraday data measure the variance of a day?

    • \(p_t\): log price at time \(t\) (time in days); \(\mu_t\): drift; \(\sigma_t\): spot volatility; \(W_t\): Brownian motion; \(J_t\): pure-jump process, \(\Delta J_s\) = jump size at time \(s\)
  • Realised variance: the sum of squared intraday returns of day \(t\) Barndorff-Nielsen & Shephard (2002)
    \begin{equation*} \RV_t = \sum_{i=1}^{M} r_{t,i}^2 \;\xrightarrow{\;M\to\infty\;}\; \int_{t-1}^{t}\sigma_s^2\,ds + \sum_{t-1<s\le t} (\Delta J_s)^2 \end{equation*}

Realised Variance and Quadratic Variation (2/2)

Slide 6 in the PDF

Without jumps and noise, on an equally spaced grid, the limit of RV is the integrated variance \(\mathrm{IV}_t = \int_{t-1}^{t}\sigma_s^2\,ds\), the true variance of day \(t\), and the error is mixed Normal:

\[\sqrt{M}\,(\mathrm{RV}_t - \mathrm{IV}_t) \Rightarrow \mathrm{MN}\big(0,\; 2\,\mathrm{IQ}_t\big), \qquad \mathrm{IQ}_t = \int_{t-1}^{t}\sigma_s^4\,ds,\]
(20.3)

where \(\mathrm{IQ}_t\) is the integrated quarticity and \(\mathrm{MN}(0, V)\) a Normal law with a random variance \(V\): given the volatility path, the error is Normal with variance \(2\,\mathrm{IQ}_t/M\).

RV is therefore a noisy measurement of \(\mathrm{IV}_t\), and the noise is largest on volatile days, where \(\mathrm{IQ}_t\) is large. This measurement error is what HARQ, the HAR model with realised quarticity, corrects for. In the other direction, microstructure noise makes very fine sampling biased, and five-minute returns are the standard compromise (Chapter 9).

In brief

  • Without jumps and noise, on an equally spaced grid, the limit is the integrated variance
    • the error is mixed Normal
    \begin{equation*} \sqrt{M}\,(\RV_t - \mathrm{IV}_t) \Rightarrow \mathrm{MN}\!\left(0,\; 2\,\mathrm{IQ}_t\right), \qquad \mathrm{IV}_t = \int_{t-1}^{t}\sigma_s^2\,ds, \quad \mathrm{IQ}_t = \int_{t-1}^{t}\sigma_s^4\,ds \end{equation*}
    • \(\mathrm{IV}_t\): integrated variance, the true variance of day \(t\); \(\mathrm{IQ}_t\): integrated quarticity
    • \(\mathrm{MN}(0, V)\): mixed Normal, a Normal with a random variance \(V\); given the volatility path, the error is Normal with variance \(2\,\mathrm{IQ}_t/M\)

Semivariances and Quarticity in VOLARE

Slide 7 in the PDF

Realised semivariances (Barndorff-Nielsen, Kinnebrock & Shephard, 2010) split RV by the sign of the intraday returns: \(\mathrm{RV}_t^{+} = \sum_i r_{t,i}^2\mathbf{1}\{r_{t,i}>0\}\) is the variance from up-moves and \(\mathrm{RV}_t^{-} = \sum_i r_{t,i}^2\mathbf{1}\{r_{t,i}<0\}\) the variance from down-moves, and they add up to \(\mathrm{RV}_t\).

Realised quarticity estimates \(\mathrm{IQ}_t\), the scale of the measurement error of RV:

\[\mathrm{RQ}_t = \frac{M}{3}\sum_{i=1}^{M} r_{t,i}^4 \;\to\; \mathrm{IQ}_t.\]
(20.4)

The factor \(M/3\) comes from the Normal law: a return with variance \(s^2\) has fourth moment \(3s^4\), and each five-minute variance is about \(\mathrm{IV}_t/M\). The limit requires a continuous, noise-free price and an equally spaced grid; a jump \(\Delta J\) adds about \(M(\Delta J)^4/3\), so raw RQ is sensitive to jumps.

In brief

  • Semivariances Barndorff-Nielsen, Kinnebrock & Shephard (2010): RV split by the sign of the intraday returns
    \begin{equation*} \RV_t^{+} = \sum_{i=1}^{M} r_{t,i}^2\,\mathbf{1}\{r_{t,i}>0\}, \qquad \RV_t^{-} = \sum_{i=1}^{M} r_{t,i}^2\,\mathbf{1}\{r_{t,i}<0\} \end{equation*}
    • \(\RV_t^{+}\): variance from up-moves, \(\RV_t^{-}\): variance from down-moves
    • They add up: \(\RV_t = \RV_t^{+} + \RV_t^{-}\)
  • Realised quarticity: an estimate of \(\mathrm{IQ}_t\), the scale of the measurement error of RV
    \begin{equation*} \mathrm{RQ}_t = \frac{M}{3}\sum_{i=1}^{M} r_{t,i}^4 \;\to\; \mathrm{IQ}_t \end{equation*}
    • The factor \(M/3\): a Normal return with variance \(s^2\) has fourth moment \(3s^4\), and each five-minute variance is about \(\mathrm{IV}_t/M\)
    • The limit needs a continuous, noise-free price and an equally spaced grid; a jump \(\Delta J\) adds about \(M(\Delta J)^4/3\), so raw RQ is jump-sensitive

Targets and Losses (1/2): the Target

Slide 8 in the PDF

The target at horizon \(h\) is the average RV over the next \(h\) days,

\[\mathrm{RV}_{t+1:t+h} = \frac{1}{h}\sum_{j=1}^{h}\mathrm{RV}_{t+j}, \qquad h \in \{1, 5, 22\},\]
(20.5)

where \(t\) is the forecast origin, the last day observed, and \(h = 1, 5, 22\) are one day, one week and one month of trading days.

Log models forecast \(y = \ln \mathrm{RV}_{t+1:t+h}\) and return to levels with a variance correction, \(F_t = \exp(\hat y_t + \hat s^2/2)\), where \(F_t\) is the forecast made at \(t\), \(\hat y_t\) the fitted log forecast and \(\hat s^2\) the variance of the regression residuals. The correction follows from \(\mathbb{E}[e^Z] = e^{\mu + s^2/2}\) for \(Z \sim N(\mu, s^2)\); it is exact if the log error is conditionally Normal with constant variance and an approximation otherwise.

For \(h > 1\) consecutive targets overlap, which induces serial dependence through lag \(h-1\) by construction and possibly longer. Every test therefore uses a HAC (heteroskedasticity and autocorrelation consistent) variance.

In brief

  • The target at horizon \(h\) is the average RV over the next \(h\) days
    \begin{equation*} \RV_{t+1:t+h} = \frac{1}{h}\sum_{j=1}^{h}\RV_{t+j}, \qquad h \in \{1, 5, 22\} \end{equation*}
    • \(t\): the forecast origin (the last day observed); \(h = 1, 5, 22\): one day, one week, one month of trading days
  • Log models forecast \(y = \ln\RV_{t+1:t+h}\) and return to levels with a variance correction
    \begin{equation*} F_t = \exp(\hat y_t + \hat s^2/2) \end{equation*}
    • \(F_t\): forecast of \(\RV_{t+1:t+h}\) made at \(t\); \(\hat y_t\): fitted log forecast; \(\hat s^2\): variance of the regression residuals
    • \(+\hat s^2/2\): since \(\E[e^Z] = e^{\mu + s^2/2}\) for \(Z \sim N(\mu, s^2)\); exact if the log error is conditionally Normal with constant variance, otherwise an approximation

Matching a Forecast to Its Target

Slide 9 in the PDF

The subscripts play two roles. In the definition of the target, \(t\) is the forecast origin; in the loss and test formulas, the subscript labels a matched evaluation row, which pairs the realised target with the forecast made before that target became observable.

At the one-day horizon, information through Monday's close forecasts Tuesday's RV, and the forecast is stored next to the Tuesday realisation. At the five-day horizon the same origin forecasts the average from Tuesday to the following Monday, and the next origin gives an overlapping target. The forecast comparison tests use the same realised target for both models in each row. The row label is not permission to use that target in estimation.

Targets and Losses (2/2): Robust Losses

Slide 10 in the PDF

A loss is robust in the sense of Patton (2011) if, for a conditionally unbiased proxy, the ranking of models by expected loss computed with \(\mathrm{RV}_t\) is the same as with the true variance. Conditional unbiasedness means \(\mathbb{E}_{t-1}[\mathrm{RV}_t] = \sigma_t^2\), where \(\mathbb{E}_{t-1}\) is the expectation given information up to \(t-1\) and \(\sigma_t^2\) the true variance of day \(t\).

The chapter uses two robust losses, written here for \(h = 1\) (for \(h > 1\), \(\mathrm{RV}_t\) is replaced by the target):

\[\mathrm{MSE}_t = (\mathrm{RV}_t - F_t)^2, \qquad \mathrm{QLIKE}_t = \frac{\mathrm{RV}_t}{F_t} - \ln\frac{\mathrm{RV}_t}{F_t} - 1 \;\ge\; 0.\]
(20.6)

MSE (mean squared error) is the squared error. QLIKE (quasi-likelihood loss) is zero only if \(F_t = \mathrm{RV}_t\) and grows faster when the forecast is too low than when it is too high: with \(\mathrm{RV}_t = 1\), \(F_t = 0.5\) gives \(2 - \ln 2 - 1 = 0.307\) and \(F_t = 2\) gives \(0.5 - \ln 0.5 - 1 = 0.193\).

QLIKE depends only on the ratio \(\mathrm{RV}_t/F_t\), so the same proportional error has the same loss on a calm day and on a stormy one, whereas MSE is dominated by a few crisis days. QLIKE is therefore the primary loss and MSE the secondary one.

In brief

  • A loss is robust Patton (2011)
    • for a conditionally unbiased proxy, the expected-loss ranking with \(\RV_t\) equals that with the true variance
    • Conditionally unbiased: \(\E_{t-1}[\RV_t] = \sigma^2_t\), with \(\E_{t-1}\) the expectation given information up to \(t - 1\) and \(\sigma^2_t\) the true variance of day \(t\)
  • The two robust losses used here (written for \(h = 1\); for \(h > 1\) replace \(\RV_t\) by the target)
    \begin{equation*} \mathrm{MSE}_t = (\RV_t - F_t)^2, \qquad \QL_t = \frac{\RV_t}{F_t} - \ln\frac{\RV_t}{F_t} - 1 \;\ge\; 0 \end{equation*}
    • MSE (mean squared error): the squared error; QLIKE (quasi-likelihood loss) is zero only if \(F_t = \RV_t\) and grows faster when the forecast is too low than when it is too high
    • Example with \(\RV_t = 1\): \(F_t = 0.5\) gives \(\QL_t = 2 - \ln 2 - 1 = 0.307\), \(F_t = 2\) gives \(0.5 - \ln 0.5 - 1 = 0.193\)
  • QLIKE depends on the ratio \(\RV_t/F_t\)
    • the same proportional error has the same loss on a calm day as on a stormy day
  • MSE is dominated by a few crisis days
    • QLIKE is the primary loss, MSE the secondary one

    Benchmarks: the HAR Family

    A new method is only interesting if it beats the models already in use. This section presents HAR, its log version and two extensions, and arranges the seven models of the chapter so that the contribution of each ingredient of the transferred method can be isolated.

    HAR: Three Averages of the Past (1/2)

    Slide 15 in the PDF

    The question is which simple benchmark must be beaten before a new method is believed. In the HAR model of Corsi (2009), future RV is a linear function of the daily, weekly and monthly averages of past RV:

    \[\mathrm{RV}_{t+1:t+h} = \beta_0 + \beta_d\,\mathrm{RV}_t + \beta_w\,\mathrm{RV}_t^{(w)} + \beta_m\,\mathrm{RV}_t^{(m)} + \varepsilon_{t+h},\]
    (20.7)

    with \(\mathrm{RV}_t^{(w)} = \frac{1}{5}\sum_{j=0}^{4}\mathrm{RV}_{t-j}\) and \(\mathrm{RV}_t^{(m)} = \frac{1}{22}\sum_{j=0}^{21}\mathrm{RV}_{t-j}\) the weekly and monthly averages up to day \(t\), and \(\varepsilon_{t+h}\) the forecast error.

    \(\beta_0\) is the intercept, and \(\beta_d\), \(\beta_w\) and \(\beta_m\), nonnegative in practice, measure how much yesterday, last week and last month weigh; they are estimated by OLS for each horizon \(h\). A sum \(\beta_d + \beta_w + \beta_m\) close to 1 means high persistence. The motivation is a cascade of traders at daily, weekly and monthly frequencies, which mimics long memory with three parameters.

    Path Signatures

    The transferred method summarises a window of data by its path signature. This section defines the object: the window as a path, the signature as the sequence of iterated integrals, its computation for piecewise-linear paths through Chen's identity, the Lévy area as a measure of order, and the signature kernel that measures the similarity of two windows.

    A Window of Data Is a Path

    Slide 18 in the PDF

    The question is how to summarise a 22-day window so that the order of the moves is kept, not only their levels. The last \(L\) days of \(d-1\) series are taken, time is added as the first channel (time augmentation) and the points are connected linearly:

    \[X_{u_i} = \Big(u_i,\; \ln \mathrm{RV}_{t-L+1+i},\; \textstyle\sum_{j \le t-L+1+i} r_j\Big), \qquad u_i = \frac{i}{L-1},\; i = 0, \dots, L-1,\]
    (20.9)

    a path \(X: [0,1] \to \mathbb{R}^d\) with \(L = 22\) days and \(d = 3\) channels: time, log RV and the cumulative return. Here \(u_i \in [0,1]\) is the rescaled time of the \(i\)-th point and \(r_j = \ln(C_j/O_j)\) the open-to-close log return of day \(j\), with \(O_j\) and \(C_j\) the open and close prices.

    A regression on the window sees 22 lags; a path summary sees their order, speed and co-movement. It can distinguish, for example, a window in which RV rose while prices fell from one in which RV rose first and prices fell later.

    Where Signatures Come From

    Slide 19 in the PDF

    Signatures come from the geometry of loop spaces. Chen (1957) showed that the iterated integrals of a path form an algebra, and Chen (1958) that they determine the path up to reparametrisation and retraced pieces. In the rough path theory of Lyons (1998), the signature is the natural feature set for controlled differential equations: a response to a path depends on it, to first order, only through its signature.

    Machine learning has used signatures as features in Levin, Lyons & Ni (2013) and Chevyrev & Kormilitzin (2016), and Lyons & McLeod (2022) survey the field; in finance, Cuchiero, Gazzani & Svaluto-Ferro (2023) build signature-based asset price models.

    The Signature: Definition (1/2)

    Slide 20 in the PDF

    Level 1 of the signature keeps only the total increments of the channels. Integrals over ordered times keep, in addition, the order in which the channels move, which is why the signature is built from iterated integrals. One signature term is an iterated integral of the path along a word of channels:

    \[S(X)^{i_1 \dots i_k}_{a,b} = \int_{a < u_1 < \dots < u_k < b} dX^{i_1}_{u_1}\cdots dX^{i_k}_{u_k}.\]
    (20.10)

    Here \(X: [a,b] \to \mathbb{R}^d\) is a path of bounded variation (finite total length), for example piecewise linear, and \(X^i_u\) its channel \(i\) at time \(u\). The word \((i_1, \dots, i_k)\) is an ordered list of \(k\) channels with letters in \(\{1, \dots, d\}\), and \(k\) is the level of the term. The integral runs over ordered times \(u_1 < \dots < u_k\), and \(dX^i_u\) is the increment of channel \(i\) at time \(u\), for a linear segment its slope times \(du\).

    The Signature: Definition (2/2)

    Slide 21 in the PDF

    The signature is the whole sequence of levels,

    \[\mathrm{Sig}(X) = \big(1,\; S^{(1)},\; S^{(2)},\; S^{(3)}, \dots\big), \qquad S^{(k)} \in (\mathbb{R}^d)^{\otimes k},\]
    (20.11)

    where \(S^{(k)}\) collects the \(d^k\) terms of level \(k\), one for each word of length \(k\), stored as a tensor (\(\otimes\) is the tensor product). The truncated signature \(\mathrm{Sig}^N(X)\) keeps levels 0 to \(N\); \(N\) is the depth.

    Level 1 contains the total increments, \(S^i = X^i_b - X^i_a\). Level 2 contains \(S^{ij} = \int_a^b (X^i_u - X^i_a)\,dX^j_u\), the increments of channel \(j\) weighted by how far channel \(i\) has already moved. Each further level adds one integral and records finer details of the order of the moves.

    Linear Segments and Chen’s Identity (1/2)

    Slide 22 in the PDF

    A linear segment with increment \(\Delta = X_b - X_a \in \mathbb{R}^d\) has the tensor exponential as signature, \(\mathrm{Sig} = \exp_\otimes(\Delta) = (1, \Delta, \Delta^{\otimes2}/2!, \Delta^{\otimes3}/3!, \dots)\), where \(\Delta^{\otimes k}\) is the array of all products \(\Delta^{i_1}\cdots\Delta^{i_k}\), so that a level-\(k\) term is \(\Delta^{i_1}\cdots\Delta^{i_k}/k!\).

    Chen's identity (Chen, 1957) states that the signature of two paths run one after the other is the tensor product of their signatures:

    \[\mathrm{Sig}(X * Y) = \mathrm{Sig}(X) \otimes \mathrm{Sig}(Y), \qquad (A \otimes B)^{(k)} = \sum_{p=0}^{k} A^{(k-p)} \otimes B^{(p)},\]
    (20.12)

    where \(X * Y\) is the concatenation, \(Y\) starting where \(X\) ends, and \(A^{(k)}\), \(B^{(k)}\) are level \(k\) of the signatures \(A\) and \(B\), with \(A^{(0)} = B^{(0)} = 1\). Level \(k\) of the whole path collects every way of splitting a word of length \(k\) between the first and the second piece.

    In brief

    • A linear segment with increment \(\Delta \in \mathbb{R}^d\) has the tensor exponential as signature
      \begin{equation*} \Sig(\text{segment}) = \exp_{\otimes}(\Delta) = \Big(1,\; \Delta,\; \tfrac{1}{2!}\Delta^{\otimes 2},\; \tfrac{1}{3!}\Delta^{\otimes 3}, \dots\Big) \end{equation*}
      • \(\Delta = X_b - X_a\): the increment of the segment; \(\Delta^{\otimes k}\): the array of all products \(\Delta^{i_1}\cdots\Delta^{i_k}\), so a level-\(k\) term is \(\Delta^{i_1}\cdots\Delta^{i_k}/k!\)

    Worked Example: a Two-Segment Path

    Slide 24 in the PDF

And \(S^{12} \neq S^{21}\), because channel 1 moves in the first segment and channel 2 in the second. Level 1 cannot tell this path from the one that goes up first and right second, since both have \(\Delta X = (1,2)\); level 2 tells them apart.

    The Lévy Area: Order as a Number

    Slide 25 in the PDF

    The Lévy area is the antisymmetric part of level 2,

    \[A^{ij} = \tfrac12\big(S^{ij} - S^{ji}\big),\]
    (20.13)

    equal to the signed area between the path, projected on channels \((i,j)\), and its chord, the straight line from start to end. \(A^{ij} > 0\) means that the path turns anticlockwise in the \((i,j)\) plane. The symmetric part, \(\tfrac12(S^{ij} + S^{ji}) = \tfrac12\Delta X^i\Delta X^j\), adds no new information. In the worked example, \(A^{12} = (2-0)/2 = 1\), the area of the triangle between the path and the chord.

    As a lead–lag statistic the area needs care. The lead–lag reading requires a positive association: if \(i\) falls and then \(j\) rises, \(i\) moves first, yet \(A^{ij} < 0\). For the channels (return, log RV), a price fall followed by a volatility rise gives \(A < 0\), and so does a price rise followed by a volatility fall. Leverage-type sequences (Chapter 5) are one contributor to a negative area, but the sign alone does not identify them.

    The Signature Kernel

    Slide 26 in the PDF

    The signature kernel is the inner product of the truncated signatures (Király & Oberhauser, 2019), a similarity score between two paths \(X\) and \(Y\) with the same channels:

    \[k_N(X,Y) = \langle \mathrm{Sig}^N(X), \mathrm{Sig}^N(Y)\rangle = 1 + \sum_{k=1}^{N}\sum_{|w|=k} S(X)^w S(Y)^w,\]
    (20.14)

    where \(w\) is a word, \(|w|\) its length and \(S(X)^w\) the term of word \(w\); the leading 1 is level 0. The distance induced by the kernel (Gu et al., Eq. 9) is the squared Euclidean distance between the truncated signatures, \(d_{\mathrm{Sig}}(X,Y) = k_N(X,X) - 2k_N(X,Y) + k_N(Y,Y) = \|\mathrm{Sig}^N(X) - \mathrm{Sig}^N(Y)\|^2\): zero when the two windows have the same signature up to depth \(N\), large when their shapes differ.

    Gu et al. (2024) and this chapter use this truncated kernel, with the same depth \(N\) as the features. The untruncated kernel solves a Goursat PDE (partial differential equation) (Salvi et al., 2021), which the paper cites but does not use. The channels are rescaled before the kernel is computed (Appendix); otherwise the channel with the largest units dominates.

    • Kernel = inner product of the truncated signatures Király & Oberhauser (2019): a similarity score between two paths

      • \(X\), \(Y\): two paths with the same channels; \(w\): a word, \(|w|\): its length (level); \(S(X)^{w}\): the term of word \(w\); the leading 1 is level 0
    • Distance induced by the kernel (Gu et al., Eq. 9)
      • squared Euclidean distance between the truncated signatures
      \begin{equation*} d_{\Sig}(X, Y) = k_N(X, X) - 2\,k_N(X, Y) + k_N(Y, Y) = \|\Sig^N(X) - \Sig^N(Y)\|^2 \end{equation*}
      • \(d_{\Sig} = 0\): the two windows have the same signature up to depth \(N\); large \(d_{\Sig}\): different shapes

      • The untruncated kernel solves a Goursat PDE (partial differential equation) Salvi et al. (2021); the paper cites it but does not use it
    • Channels are rescaled before the kernel (Appendix): otherwise the channel with the largest units dominates

    Section summary

    A window of data becomes a path, and its signature records the increments and the order of the moves level by level. Chen's identity makes the signature of a piecewise-linear path a product of tensor exponentials, the Lévy area turns order into a number, and the signature kernel compares windows by the distance between their truncated signatures.

    The Method: Two-Step LASSO

    Slide 29 in the PDF

    The first step selects features with a weighted LASSO (Tibshirani, 1996) (Eq. 14):

    \[\hat\theta_{\mathrm{L}} = \arg\min_\theta \sum_\tau w_\tau\big(y_{\tau+\Delta t} - \xi_\tau^\top\theta\big)^2 + \lambda\|\theta\|_1,\]
    (20.16)

    with \(\theta\) the coefficient vector, \(\|\theta\|_1 = \sum_j|\theta_j|\), \(w_\tau \ge 0\) the weight of week \(\tau\), equal or given by the kernel, and \(\lambda \ge 0\) the penalty: a larger \(\lambda\) generally favours sparser solutions, and \(\lambda = 0\) gives weighted OLS. The second step (Eq. 15) refits by weighted OLS on the selected support, the nonzero coefficients of the first step.

    The refit is needed because the LASSO shrinks the kept coefficients towards zero; OLS after selection removes this penalty-induced shrinkage on the selected support (Belloni & Chernozhukov, 2013), but not selection or omitted-variable bias. The paper calls the method "adaptive" because of the weights \(w_\tau\), not in the sense of the adaptive LASSO of Zou (2006). In this chapter \(\lambda\) is chosen in each training window as the support with the smallest BIC (Bayesian Information Criterion) along the LARS (Least Angle Regression) path (Efron et al., 2004); details are in the Appendix.

    The Method: Kernel Weights

    Slide 30 in the PDF

    The kernel weights start from the distance of each past window to the last window with an observed target (Eq. 11), \(\delta_\tau = d_{\mathrm{Sig},N}(z_{\tau-l:\tau},\, z_{t-\Delta t-l:t-\Delta t})\). Here \(z_\tau = (x_\tau, y_{\tau+\Delta t})\) are the pairs of predictors and later target, not the features \(\xi_\tau\) (Algorithm 1), \(t\) is the current week and \(t - \Delta t\) the last week whose target \(y_t\) is already observed. The weights are a softmax with temperature \(\gamma \ge 0\) (Eq. 12):

    \[w_\tau = \frac{\exp(-\gamma\,\delta_\tau)}{\sum_s \exp(-\gamma\,\delta_s)},\]
    (20.17)

    where \(s\) runs over all training weeks, so the weights sum to 1. Similar windows, with small \(\delta_\tau\), get more weight; \(\gamma = 0\) gives ordinary equal-weight regression and \(\gamma \to \infty\) uses only the most similar past window.

    The idea is a nearest-neighbour scheme inside a regression: learn from the past periods that looked like today. Its cost is a smaller effective sample, measured by Kish's \(n_{\mathrm{eff}} = 1/\sum_\tau w_\tau^2\), which equals \(n\) for equal weights \(1/n\) and 1 if one week takes all the weight.

    Kernel Temperature: One Choice per Class, on a Validation Block

    Slide 35 in the PDF

    The kernel temperature is made scale-free and set in every training window as

    \[\gamma = \frac{c}{\mathrm{median}_\tau(\delta_\tau)},\]
    (20.18)

    where \(\delta_\tau\) are the kernel distances of the training days to the current window and \(c > 0\) is a dimensionless constant, the only tuning parameter.     Slide 36 in the PDF

    The diagram shows the rolling scheme.     Evaluation Protocol (1/3): the DM Test

    Slide 37 in the PDF

    The question is whether the loss difference between two models exceeds sampling noise. For each asset and horizon the mean QLIKE and MSE of two models are compared by the DM (Diebold–Mariano) test of equal predictive accuracy (Diebold & Mariano, 1995); the relative error of the paper is reported only descriptively (Appendix). The statistic is

    \[d_t = L(\mathrm{RV}_t, F^A_t) - L(\mathrm{RV}_t, F^B_t), \qquad \mathrm{DM} = \frac{\bar d}{\sqrt{\hat\Omega/T}},\]
    (20.19)

    where \(L\) is the loss (QLIKE or MSE), \(F^A_t\) and \(F^B_t\) the forecasts of models \(A\) and \(B\), \(\bar d\) the mean loss differential over the \(T\) evaluation days and \(\hat\Omega\) the long-run variance of \(d_t\) (Further Topics).

    Under \(H_0: \mathbb{E}[d_t] = 0\), DM is approximately \(N(0,1)\), and \(\mathrm{DM} < -1.645\) means that model \(A\) is significantly more accurate at 5%, one-sided. Because estimation is rolling, the test is read as the unconditional GW (Giacomini–White) test; the conditional GW test also asks whether \(d_{t-h}\) predicts \(d_t\), with instruments \((1, d_{t-h})\) (Giacomini & White, 2006).

                Case Study: The Signature as a Lead-Lag Metric

                The case study uses the signature for a different question: not to forecast one series, but to measure whether one asset moves before another. The Lévy area, a level-2 signature term, is a natural candidate, and the study illustrates it on intraday SPY and Bitcoin data.

                The Question: The Signature as a Lead-Lag Metric

                Slide 60 in the PDF

                The question is whether one asset moves before another and whether the signature of their joint path can measure it. Lead–lag relations show in which market information appears first, a question of price discovery. Bennett, Cucuringu and Reinert (2022, Machine Learning) build a directed network from pairwise lead–lag relations and split it into clusters of leaders and followers, using the 434 New York Stock Exchange (NYSE) stocks with the highest volume and daily returns from 2000 to 2019.

                One of the metrics they compare is twice the Lévy area of the path of normalised log prices over \([t-m, t]\):

                \[S^{ij} = 2A^{ij} = \iint_{t-m<u<v<t} \big(dX^i_u\, dX^j_v - dX^j_u\, dX^i_v\big).\]
                (20.20)

                A positive \(S^{ij}\) means that moves of \(i\) are followed by moves of \(j\) in the same direction.                   Two Refinements (1/2): HARQ

                  Slide 75 in the PDF

                  RV measures the integrated variance with error, \(\mathrm{RV}_t = \mathrm{IV}_t + \eta_t\) with \(\mathrm{Var}(\eta_t) \approx 2\,\mathrm{IQ}_t/M\), and errors in variables pull the daily coefficient \(\beta_d\) towards zero, most on noisy days. HARQ (Bollerslev, Patton & Quaedvlieg, 2016) lets the weight on yesterday's RV depend on the square root of realised quarticity:

                  \[\mathrm{RV}_{t+1:t+h} = \beta_0 + \big(\beta_d + \beta_Q(\mathrm{RQ}_t^{1/2} - \overline{\mathrm{RQ}^{1/2}})\big)\mathrm{RV}_t + \beta_w\mathrm{RV}_t^{(w)} + \beta_m\mathrm{RV}_t^{(m)} + \varepsilon_{t+h},\]
                  (20.22)

                  with \(\beta_Q < 0\) expected, so that a noisy day gets less weight. The BPQ "insanity filter" replaces a forecast outside the in-sample range of the target by the in-sample mean.

                  In brief

                  • RV measures the true variance with error
                    \begin{equation*} \RV_t = \mathrm{IV}_t + \eta_t, \qquad \Var(\eta_t) \approx 2\,\mathrm{IQ}_t / M \end{equation*}
                    • \(\eta_t\): measurement error; errors-in-variables attenuate (pull towards zero) \(\beta_d\), most on noisy days
                  • HARQ Bollerslev, Patton & Quaedvlieg (2016): the weight on yesterday’s RV falls when RV is measured less precisely
                    \begin{equation*} \RV_{t+1:t+h} = \beta_0 + \big(\beta_d + \beta_Q(\mathrm{RQ}_t^{1/2} - \overline{\mathrm{RQ}^{1/2}})\big)\RV_t + \beta_w\RV_t^{(w)} + \beta_m\RV_t^{(m)} + \varepsilon_{t+h} \end{equation*}
                    • \(\mathrm{RQ}_t^{1/2}\): square root of realised quarticity, a measure of the error of \(\RV_t\); \(\overline{\mathrm{RQ}^{1/2}}\): its in-sample mean, so \(\beta_d\) is the weight on an average day
                    • Expected \(\beta_Q < 0\): on a noisy day (\(\mathrm{RQ}_t\) above average) yesterday’s RV gets less weight
                  • BPQ (Bollerslev–Patton–Quaedvlieg) filter (“insanity filter”)
                    • a forecast outside the in-sample range of the target is replaced by the in-sample mean

                  Two Refinements (2/2): SHAR

                  Slide 76 in the PDF

                  SHAR (Patton & Sheppard, 2015) splits the daily term by the sign of the intraday returns, \(\mathrm{RV}_{t+1:t+h} = \beta_0 + \beta^{+}\mathrm{RV}_t^{+} + \beta^{-}\mathrm{RV}_t^{-} + \beta_w\mathrm{RV}_t^{(w)} + \beta_m\mathrm{RV}_t^{(m)} + \varepsilon_{t+h}\), where \(\mathrm{RV}_t^{+}\) and \(\mathrm{RV}_t^{-}\) are the positive and negative semivariances; \(\beta^{+} = \beta^{-}\) gives back HAR. Patton and Sheppard find \(\beta^{-} > \beta^{+}\), an intraday version of the leverage effect (Chapter 5), and the signature can capture a related effect from daily data through the Lévy area between returns and log RV.

                  In brief

                  • SHAR Patton & Sheppard (2015): split the daily term by the sign of the intraday returns
                    \begin{equation*} \RV_{t+1:t+h} = \beta_0 + \beta^{+}\RV_t^{+} + \beta^{-}\RV_t^{-} + \beta_w\RV_t^{(w)} + \beta_m\RV_t^{(m)} + \varepsilon_{t+h} \end{equation*}
                    • \(\RV_t^{+}\), \(\RV_t^{-}\): the positive and negative semivariances of day \(t\)
                    • \(\beta^{+} = \beta^{-}\) gives back HAR, since \(\RV_t^{+} + \RV_t^{-} = \RV_t\)

                  Why Signatures Work (1/2): Uniqueness and Shuffles

                  Slide 79 in the PDF

                  By the uniqueness theorem of Hambly & Lyons (2010), the full signature determines the path up to tree-like equivalence and translation, and with a strictly increasing time channel only translation remains; a finite truncation is not injective. The shuffle identity, for example \(S^iS^j = S^{ij} + S^{ji}\), writes products of signature terms as sums of higher terms, so a linear model in the truncated signature already contains polynomials of the increments.

                  In brief

                  • Uniqueness Hambly & Lyons (2010): the full signature identifies the path
                    • \(\Sig(X)\) determines \(X\) up to tree-like equivalence (adding pieces that are retraced backwards) and translation
                    • With a strictly increasing time channel nothing can be retraced, so only translation remains
                    • A finite truncation \(\Sig^N\) is not injective: two different paths can share levels \(1..N\)
                  • Shuffle identity: a product of signature terms is a sum of higher terms
                    \begin{equation*} S^{i}S^{j} = S^{ij} + S^{ji}, \qquad S^{ij}S^{k} = S^{ijk} + S^{ikj} + S^{kij} \end{equation*}
                    • The sum runs over all interleavings (shuffles) of the two words that keep the order inside each word
                    • So a linear model in \(\Sig^N(X)\) already contains polynomials of the increments

                  Why Signatures Work (2/2): Universality and Decay

                  Slide 80 in the PDF

                  Universality states that any continuous function of the path on a compact set \(K\) of time-augmented paths is approximated uniformly by a linear functional \(\langle \ell, \mathrm{Sig}(X)\rangle\) of the signature, a consequence of the Stone–Weierstrass theorem through the shuffle identity. This allows nonlinear approximation at sufficient depth but guarantees nothing at depth 3 or out of sample. Factorial decay, \(\|S^{(k)}\| \le \|X\|_{1\text{-var}}^k/k!\), makes the high levels small for paths of moderate length.

                  In brief

                  • Universality: any continuous function of the path is approximately linear in the signature
                    • Stone–Weierstrass, via the shuffle identity
                    \begin{equation*} \forall \varepsilon > 0\;\; \exists\, \ell \text{ linear}: \quad \sup_{X \in K} \big|f(X) - \langle \ell, \Sig(X)\rangle\big| < \varepsilon \end{equation*}
                    • \(K\): a compact set of time-augmented paths with a common start (in the 1-variation topology); \(f: K \to \mathbb{R}\): a continuous function of the path
                    • \(\langle \ell, \Sig(X)\rangle\): a weighted sum of finitely many signature terms, i.e. a linear regression on them
                    • Sufficient depth permits nonlinear approximation; this does not guarantee accuracy at depth 3 or out of sample. The cost is \(d^N\) features, so sparsity is needed
                  • Factorial decay: high levels are small
                    \begin{equation*} \|S^{(k)}\| \le \frac{\|X\|_{1\text{-var}}^k}{k!} \end{equation*}
                    • \(\|X\|_{1\text{-var}}\): total length of the path (sum of the lengths of its segments); \(\|S^{(k)}\|\): size (Euclidean norm) of level \(k\)
                    • Truncating at a small depth \(N\) loses little for paths of moderate length, which is why channels are rescaled (Appendix)

                  Two Invariances and the Basepoint

                  Slide 82 in the PDF

                  The signature is translation invariant, \(\mathrm{Sig}(X + c) = \mathrm{Sig}(X)\), so the level of volatility enters separately through the log-HAR terms, which play the role of the external factors of Gu et al.                   Evaluation Protocol: the HAC Variance

                  Slide 84 in the PDF

                  The loss differential \(d_t\) is autocorrelated, through overlapping targets up to lag \(h - 1\) and volatility clustering in the losses, so the plain variance understates the uncertainty of \(\bar d\). The Newey–West heteroskedasticity and autocorrelation consistent (HAC) estimator (Newey & West, 1987) is

                  \[\hat\Omega = \hat\gamma_0 + 2\sum_{j=1}^{q}\Big(1 - \frac{j}{q+1}\Big)\hat\gamma_j,\]
                  (20.24)

                  with \(\hat\gamma_j\) the sample autocovariance of \(d_t\) at lag \(j\); the Bartlett weights guarantee \(\hat\Omega \ge 0\), and \(q = \max(h, \lceil T^{1/3}\rceil)\) lags cover at least the overlap.

                  In brief

                  • Why not the plain sample variance: \(d_t\) is autocorrelated
                    • overlapping targets up to lag \(h - 1\)
                    • volatility clustering in the losses
                    • so \(\hat\gamma_0/T\) understates the uncertainty of \(\bar d\) (and over-rejects) when the sum of the autocovariances is positive, the typical case with overlapping targets
                  • The Newey–West estimator Newey & West (1987), heteroskedasticity and autocorrelation consistent (HAC)
                    \begin{equation*} \hat\Omega = \hat\gamma_0 + 2\sum_{j=1}^{q}\Big(1 - \frac{j}{q+1}\Big)\hat\gamma_j, \qquad \hat\gamma_j = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=j+1}^{T}(d_t - \bar d)(d_{t-j} - \bar d) \end{equation*}
                    • \(\hat\gamma_j\): the sample autocovariance of \(d_t\) at lag \(j\)
                    • The Bartlett weights \(1 - j/(q+1)\) decline linearly with the lag and guarantee \(\hat\Omega \ge 0\)
                    • \(q = \max(h, \lceil T^{1/3}\rceil)\) lags: at least \(h\), for the dependence created by the overlap (\(\lceil\cdot\rceil\): rounding up)
