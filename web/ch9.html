Volatility drives risk, option prices and portfolio weights, but it is never observed. One daily return is a single draw from a distribution whose dispersion we want to estimate, and GARCH infers today's variance from past daily returns through a model. The chapter's question is how to measure a volatility we never see. The idea of Andersen & Bollerslev (1998) is that the sum of squared intraday returns measures the day's variance almost without a model; Andersen, Bollerslev, Diebold & Labys (2003) then use it to model and forecast volatility. Computed from 5-minute returns, the annualised volatility from the mean realised variance (RV) is 12.9% for SPY, the exchange-traded fund on the S&P 500, and 45.1% for Bitcoin. Overnight returns carry 38% of SPY's daily variance, although the market is closed for 17.5 hours.

Measurement has its own problems. Microstructure noise biases RV at very high frequencies; the SPY signature plot is approximately flat: 12.9% at 5 minutes, 12.8% at 30 minutes and 13.0% at 130 minutes. Jumps separate news from the continuous component, but daily tests need a correction for multiple testing. Of 1,489 daily tests on SPY, 236 reject at 5% without correction; after the periodicity correction and false discovery rate (FDR) control, 17 jump days remain.

Once measured daily, volatility can be modelled directly. The HAR (heterogeneous autoregressive) model of Corsi (2009) explains tomorrow's RV by its averages over one day, one week and one month. Out of sample, over 1,180 forecasts for SPY in 2022–2026, log-HAR has a QLIKE (quasi-likelihood) loss of 0.310 against 0.343 for GARCH(1,1)-t; the Diebold–Mariano test gives \(t = -2.30\) and \(p = 0.021\). MSE comparisons are inconclusive, because a few crisis days dominate the squared errors. The chapter ends with the question of whether volatility is rough, and how much of that roughness measurement error can produce.

Realised Variance

A Model for Intraday Prices (1/2)

Slide 7 in the PDF
  • Question: how do we turn a day's 5-minute returns into a measurement of that day's variance?
  • Notation: day \(t\) has \(M\) intervals of length \(\Delta = 1/M\), returns \(r_{t,i}\), daily return \(r_t = \sum_i r_{t,i}\)
    • Variances in %\(^2\)
    • annualised volatility \(\sqrt{252 \times \text{variance}}\) (SPY), \(\sqrt{365 \times \text{variance}}\) (Bitcoin)
    • the SPY overnight return (close to next open) is outside the bars
  • Log price in continuous time: \(dp(s) = \mu(s)\,ds + \sigma(s)\,dW(s)\), with \(W\) a Brownian motion (Chapter 11)
    • \(\sigma(s)\) is the spot volatility: it changes within the day and from day to day

A Model for Intraday Prices (2/2)

Slide 8 in the PDF
  • Integrated variance of day \(t\): the quantity we want to measure
    • \(\IV_t = \displaystyle\int_{t-1}^{t} \sigma^2(s)\,ds\): the spot variance accumulated over the day
    • in this chapter IV always means integrated variance, never implied volatility
  • Given the path of \(\sigma\), the daily return is Normal: \(r_t \mid \sigma \sim N(\int\mu, \IV_t)\)
    • assumption: \(\sigma\) is independent of \(W\) (no leverage)
    • assumption: the drift is zero (or fixed by the path of \(\sigma\))
    • With leverage (Appendix: Itô semimartingales) this conditional normality fails, but the limit theory of realised variance (RV, next slide) still holds

Definition: Realised Variance

Slide 9 in the PDF
  • Realised variance: the sum of squared intraday returns
    • \(\RV_t = \displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{M} r_{t,i}^2\), realised volatility \(= \sqrt{\RV_t}\)
  • Why squares: the conditional means of the squared increments approximate \(\int_{t-1}^{t} \sigma^2(s)\,ds\)
    • over a short interval \(\E[r_{t,i}^2 \mid \sigma] \approx \sigma^2(s_i)\,\Delta\)
    • the observed sum of squares converges to the quadratic variation; it is not a Riemann sum
  • Quadratic variation of day \(t\), with \(p = 100\ln P\): \([p]_t - [p]_{t-1} = \operatorname{plim}_{M \to \infty} \sum_{i=1}^{M} r_{t,i}^2 = \IV_t\) for a price without jumps Barndorff-Nielsen & Shephard (2002)
    • The drift adds terms of order \(\Delta\) per day, which vanish as \(M \to \infty\); for SPY the mean 5-minute return is below 1% of its standard deviation
  • No parameters to estimate: \(\RV_t\) is a model-free measurement of one day's variance
  • The squared daily return \(r_t^2\) is the special case \(M = 1\)
    • unbiased (with zero drift) but extremely noisy

        How Precise Is Realised Variance?

        Slide 16 in the PDF
        • Feasible central limit theorem (CLT) for RV Barndorff-Nielsen & Shephard (2002)
          • \(\sqrt{M}\,(\RV_t - \IV_t) / \sqrt{2\,\text{RQ}_t} \to N(0, 1)\)
          • \(\text{IQ}_t = \int_{t-1}^{t}\sigma^4(s)\,ds\): the integrated quarticity, which sets the variance of RV
          • \(\text{IQ}_t\) is estimated by the realised quarticity \(\text{RQ}_t = \frac{M}{3}\sum_i r_{t,i}^4\)
        • Standard error: \(\text{se}(\RV_t) = \sqrt{\tfrac{2}{3}\sum_i r_{t,i}^4}\)
        • 95% confidence interval (CI): \(\RV_t \exp\left(\pm 1.96\,\text{se}(\RV_t)/\RV_t\right)\)
          • on the log scale, so always positive
          • With constant volatility, \(\text{se}/\RV \approx \sqrt{2/M}\): \(16\%\) for \(M = 78\)
        • The limiting variance \(2\,\text{IQ}_t\) is itself random (a mixed Normal limit)
          • replacing it by \(2\,\text{RQ}_t\) is legitimate because the convergence is stable
          • formal statement in the Appendix

        Microstructure Noise

        Why Not Use Every Tick? (1/2)

        Slide 17 in the PDF
        • Question: why not use every trade?
          • more returns shrink the relative error of RV (\(\sqrt{2/M}\))
        • Observed prices are not the efficient price: \(p^{\text{obs}}_{i} = p^*_{i} + \varepsilon_{i}\)
          • so \(r_i = r^*_i + \varepsilon_i - \varepsilon_{i-1}\)
          • \(p^*_i\): the efficient (true) log price, \(\varepsilon_i\): the noise in the observed price, \(r^*_i\): the efficient return
          • Sources of \(\varepsilon\): bid–ask bounce, tick size (price discreteness), stale quotes (Chapter 10)

        Why Not Use Every Tick? (2/2)

        Slide 18 in the PDF
        • With i.i.d. noise of variance \(\omega^2\) and \(n\) returns per day Zhang, Mykland & Aït-Sahalia (2005), Hansen & Lunde (2006)
          • \(\E[\RV^{(n)}_t \mid \sigma] = \IV_t + 2n\omega^2\) (zero drift, no leverage): each squared return carries \(2\omega^2\) of noise, so the bias grows with the sampling frequency
          • Adjacent returns share \(\varepsilon_{i-1}\) with opposite signs: \(\mathrm{cov}(r_i, r_{i-1}) = -\omega^2\)
        • Trade-off: \(\E[(\RV^{(n)} - \IV)^2] \approx 2\,\text{IQ}/n + 4n^2\omega^4\)
          • discretisation error plus squared bias
          • MSE (mean squared error) optimal number of returns \(n^* = \left(\text{IQ}/(4\omega^4)\right)^{1/3}\) Bandi & Russell (2008): more returns cut the first term, but raise the second; derivation in the Appendix
        • Numbers: bias \(0.016\) at 5 minutes (\(n = 78\)), \(4.680\) at 1 second
          • \(\IV = 1\%^2\) with constant volatility within the day, so \(\text{IQ} = \IV^2 = 1\) (%\(^4\))
          • \(\omega = 0.01\%\), so \(\omega^2 = 10^{-4}\%^2\)
        • Optimal number of returns: \(n^* = 292\) (one return every \(80\) s)

        Why 5 Minutes?

        Slide 19 in the PDF
        • Question: which frequency, when \(n^*\) depends on \(\omega^2\) and \(\text{IQ}_t\)?
          • both are unknown and different across assets
        • The optimal frequency falls fast as the noise grows
          • \(n^* = 292\) with \(\omega = 0.01\%\)
          • \(n^* = 14\) (one return about every 29 minutes) with \(\omega = 0.1\%\)
        • Sparse sampling at 5 minutes (\(n = 78\) in a 6.5-hour session): a robust compromise Liu, Patton & Sheppard (2015)
          • the bias \(2n\omega^2\) stays small, and the relative discretisation error is \(\sqrt{2/78} = 16\%\)
          • the cost: most trades are discarded
        • The course data are 5-minute bars, the finest grid available
          • the signature plot on real data checks whether 5 minutes is sparse enough

          Jumps

          Prices Jump: Bipower Variation (1/2)

          Slide 22 in the PDF
          Ole E. Barndorff-Nielsen, 2007 Photo: Thomas Steiner (2007); CC BY-SA 2.5; Wikimedia Commons
          • Question: how much of a day's variance comes from jumps, and how do we separate jumps from diffusion?
          • Add jumps to the model: \(dp = \mu\,ds + \sigma\,dW + dJ\)
            • \(J\): a jump process (news, Chapter 11)
            • Quadratic variation now has two parts: \(\RV_t \to \IV_t + \sum_{j} \kappa_j^2\), with \(\kappa_j\) the jump sizes in day \(t\)
            • Why separate them? Jumps are less persistent than diffusive volatility, and they price differently (options, Chapter 12)

          Prices Jump: Bipower Variation (2/2)

          Slide 23 in the PDF
          • Bipower variation Barndorff-Nielsen & Shephard (2004): products of adjacent absolute returns
            • \(\BV_t = \mu_1^{-2}\,\frac{M}{M-1}\sum_{i=2}^{M} |r_{t,i}|\,|r_{t,i-1}| \to \IV_t\)
            • \(\mu_1 = \E|Z| = \sqrt{2/\pi} = 0.798\) for \(Z \sim N(0,1)\), so \(\mu_1^{-2} = \pi/2 = 1.571\); \(\frac{M}{M-1}\): finite-sample normalisation
          • Base case: jumps of finite activity and no noise; other settings need extra conditions
          • A jump then enters one return, which is multiplied by a small neighbour
            • its effect on BV vanishes as \(M\) grows
          • The jump part of the day is \(\max(\RV_t - \BV_t, 0)\)
            • Example, \(M = 4\) returns \(0.1, 0.1, 0.1, 1.0\) (%): \(\RV = 1.03\), \(\BV = \frac{\pi}{2}\cdot\frac{4}{3}(0.01 + 0.01 + 0.10) = 0.25\), jump part \(0.78\) (%\(^2\))

          A Test for Jumps

          Slide 24 in the PDF
          • Ratio statistic Huang & Tauchen (2005), Barndorff-Nielsen & Shephard (2006)
            • \(z_t = \dfrac{(\RV_t - \BV_t)/\RV_t}{\sqrt{\theta\,M^{-1}\max\left(1, \text{TQ}_t/\BV_t^2\right)}}\)
            • \(\theta = \pi^2/4 + \pi - 5 = 0.609\); tripower quarticity \(\text{TQ}_t = M \mu_{4/3}^{-3}\frac{M}{M-2}\sum_i |r_{t,i}|^{4/3}|r_{t,i-1}|^{4/3}|r_{t,i-2}|^{4/3}\), \(\mu_{4/3} = \E|Z|^{4/3}\)
            • Numerator: the relative jump share \((\RV_t - \BV_t)/\RV_t\); denominator: its standard error
            • \(\theta\): the asymptotic variance constant; TQ estimates \(\text{IQ}_t\) robustly to jumps
          • Under no jumps \(z_t \to N(0,1)\)
            • a jump makes \(\RV > \BV\) and \(z_t\) large and positive
          • We use \(\alpha = 0.1\%\) (critical value \(3.09\)) for the jump days plotted on the next slide
          • Significant day: jump part \(J_t = \RV_t - \BV_t\)
            • continuous part \(C_t = \RV_t - J_t\)
            • otherwise \(J_t = 0\)

            Many Days, Many Tests: Spurious Jumps

            Slide 27 in the PDF
            • 1 489 daily tests at 5%: 236 rejections; which of them are false?
              • against 74 expected under no jumps
            • Part of the excess is intraday seasonality (Further Topics)
              • dividing returns by the periodicity factor \(f_i\) Boudt, Croux & Laurent (2011) already cuts the 5% rejections from 236 to 169
            • Benjamini & Hochberg (1995): sort the \(p\)-values and reject the \(k\) smallest
              • \(k\): the largest index with \(p_{(k)} \le k q/N\); \(p_{(k)}\): \(k\)-th smallest \(p\)-value, \(N\): number of tests, \(q\): target FDR
              • here each day's \(p\)-value is the one-sided \(1 - \Phi(z_t)\) of the ratio statistic

                  The HAR-RV Model (1/2)

                  Slide 38 in the PDF
                  • Question: how do we forecast tomorrow's RV when the autocorrelation of \(\ln \RV\) decays very slowly?

                  The log-HAR Model

                  Slide 40 in the PDF
                  • Why logs
                    • \(\ln \RV\) is close to Normal, whereas RV is very heavy-tailed (slide Distributions)

                    • with heteroskedastic errors both are approximations

                  HARQ and SHAR

                  Slide 43 in the PDF
                  • HARQ Bollerslev, Patton & Quaedvlieg (2016): yesterday's RV is measured with error, and the error attenuates the daily slope

                  Evaluating Against a Noisy Target

                  Slide 45 in the PDF
                  • True variance is unobserved
                    • we compare forecasts \(F_t\) with the proxy \(\RV_t\)
                    • A loss is robust if the ranking by expected loss with a proxy that is conditionally unbiased (given the forecasts' information) equals the ranking with true variance Patton (2011)
                  • \(\text{MSE} = (\RV_t - F_t)^2\): robust
                    • measures errors in variance units
                    • is dominated by the most volatile days
                  • \(\text{QLIKE} = \dfrac{\RV_t}{F_t} - \ln\dfrac{\RV_t}{F_t} - 1 \ge 0\): robust
                    • depends only on the ratio
                    • penalises under-prediction more
                    • Where QLIKE comes from: for \(r \sim N(0, F)\), \(-2\ln\) density is \(\ln F + r^2/F\) plus a constant; replacing \(r^2\) by \(\RV_t\) and subtracting the minimum, reached at \(F_t = \RV_t\), gives the formula above
                  • Common mistake: MAE, or MSE on volatility instead of variance, is not robust
                    • MAE: mean absolute error
                    • Andersen & Bollerslev (1998): against \(r_t^2\), GARCH forecasts explained a few % of the variation; against RV the same forecasts explain far more: the proxy was noisy, not the model

                  Comparing Two Forecasts: Diebold–Mariano

                  Slide 46 in the PDF
                  • Diebold & Mariano (1995) (DM) test: \(t = \bar d / \text{se}_{\text{NW}}(\bar d) \to N(0,1)\)
                    • loss difference: \(d_t = L(\RV_t, F^A_t) - L(\RV_t, F^B_t)\)
                    • \(L(\RV_t, F_t)\): the loss (QLIKE or MSE) of forecast \(F_t\); \(\bar d\): mean of \(d_t\); \(\text{se}_{\text{NW}}\): Newey–West standard error
                    • \(t < 0\): model A has the lower loss
                    • HAC standard error because \(d_t\) is autocorrelated (Chapter 8)
                  • What drives \(t\): the mean gap and how stable the gap is from day to day
                    • Check, ignoring autocorrelation, \(T = 100\) days: a gap with mean \(0.01\) and standard deviation \(0.02\) gives \(t = 0.01/(0.02/\sqrt{100}) = 5\)
                    • a gap with mean \(0.03\) and standard deviation \(0.30\) gives \(t = 1\)
                    • two forecasts that move together have a stable gap: a small mean difference can then be highly significant
                  • Conditions: a central limit theorem for \(d_t\), with a positive long-run variance
                    • QLIKE: \(d_t = \RV_t(1/F^A_t - 1/F^B_t) + \ln(F^A_t/F^B_t)\), so the moments of \(d_t\) depend also on the forecasts in the denominators
                    • nested models (HAR inside SHAR or HARQ) with estimated parameters: under equal accuracy \(d_t\) can degenerate, and \(N(0,1)\) is only a conventional approximation (Clark & McCracken)
                    • valid alternatives: rolling windows of fixed length with the Giacomini–White (GW) test, or a bootstrap that re-estimates the models

                  Calibration: Mincer–Zarnowitz

                  Slide 47 in the PDF
                  • Mincer & Zarnowitz (1969) (MZ): \(\RV_t = a + b\,F_t + u_t\)
                    • regress the realised target on the forecast
                    • \(a\): intercept, \(b\): slope, \(u_t\): error
                    • a calibrated forecast satisfies \(a = 0\), \(b = 1\) in this linear regression; \(\E[\RV_t - F_t] = 0\) alone does not imply it
                    • \(b < 1\): high forecasts come with realised means below the 45° line
                    • \(b > 1\): high forecasts come with realised means above the 45° line
                    • Check: a forecast of 2 on calm days and 4 on busy days, with realised means 1.5 and 4.5, has \(b = (4.5 - 1.5)/(4 - 2) = 1.5 > 1\) and \(a = 1.5 - 1.5 \times 2 = -1.5\)

                    • not rejecting \(a = 0\), \(b = 1\) does not prove calibration
                    • \(R^2\) measures how much of the variation the forecast explains
                    • \(R^2\) is capped by the noise in RV: even the true variance would not reach 1 Andersen & Bollerslev (1998)

                    Rough Volatility

                    Is Volatility Rough?

                    Slide 53 in the PDF
                    • Question: are log-volatility paths rougher than those of a Brownian motion?
                    • Gatheral, Jaisson & Rosenbaum (2018): log volatility behaves like a fractional Brownian motion
                      • Hurst exponent \(H \approx 0.1\)
                      • \(H = 0.5\): ordinary Brownian motion
                      • \(H < 0.5\): increments are negatively correlated, paths are rougher
                      • \(H > 0.5\): smoother, trending
                    • Rough models reproduce the shape of the implied volatility smile
                      • for short maturities (Chapter 12)

                    Fractional Brownian Motion and the Scaling Law

                    Slide 54 in the PDF
                    • Fractional Brownian motion \(B_H\), \(0 < H < 1\): a Gaussian process with \(B_H(0) = 0\) and mean 0
                      • \(\mathrm{Cov}(B_H(t), B_H(s)) = \tfrac12\left(|t|^{2H} + |s|^{2H} - |t-s|^{2H}\right)\)
                      • \(H = 1/2\): the covariance is \(\min(t, s)\), ordinary Brownian motion
                      • increments: \(B_H(t+\Delta) - B_H(t) \sim N(0, \Delta^{2H})\) for every \(t\)
                    • Model \(\ln\sigma_t = \text{const} + \nu B_H(t)\)
                      • the change over \(\Delta\) days is \(\nu\Delta^H Z\) in distribution, \(Z \sim N(0, 1)\)
                      • Scaling law: \(m(q, \Delta) = \frac{1}{T}\sum_t |\ln\sigma_{t+\Delta} - \ln\sigma_t|^q\), with \(\sigma_t = \sqrt{\RV_t}\), estimates \(\nu^q\,\E|Z|^q\,\Delta^{qH}\), so the slope of \(\ln m(q, \Delta)\) on \(\ln\Delta\) is \(\zeta_q = qH\)
                    • Estimate: slopes \(\zeta_q\) for \(q \in \{0.5, 1, 1.5, 2, 3\}\)
                      • then regress \(\zeta_q\) on \(q\) through the origin: \(\hat H = \sum_q q\,\zeta_q / \sum_q q^2\)
                    • Check with \(q = 2\)
                      • for Brownian motion \(m(2, \Delta) = \nu^2\Delta\), so doubling \(\Delta\) doubles \(m\) and \(\zeta_2 = 1 = 2 \times 0.5\)
                      • with \(H = 0.1\), doubling \(\Delta\) multiplies \(m(2, \Delta)\) only by \(2^{0.2} \approx 1.15\)

                      Rough or Noisy? The Identification Problem

                      Slide 57 in the PDF
                      • The estimate \(H \approx 0.1\) comes from RV, a noisy proxy of the latent \(\sigma_t\)
                        • Cont & Das (2024): the scaling estimator applied to realised volatility finds roughness even when spot volatility is smooth: an estimation artefact
                        • Bolko, Christensen, Pakkanen & Veliyev (2023) (generalised method of moments, GMM, that models the RV error) and Fukasawa, Takabatake & Westphal (2022) (consistent under high-frequency asymptotics) still find \(H < 0.5\)
                      • Formal question: \(\ln \RV_t^{1/2} = \ln\sigma_t + e_t\)
                        • \(\ln\sigma_t\) is fractional of index \(H\), \(e_t\) is the measurement error of RV
                        • test \(H_0\): \(H = 1/2\) (classical diffusive volatility) against \(H_1\): \(H < 1/2\)
                        • Identification problem: a small \(H\) and a large \(\mathrm{Var}(e_t)\) both flatten \(m(2,\Delta)\) at short lags (next slide)
                      • Short-run roughness can coexist with long memory Bennedsen, Lunde & Pakkanen (2022)
                      • Stakes: the short-maturity skew and volatility forecasts depend on \(H\) (Chapter 12)

                      How Measurement Error Flattens the Scaling Law

                      Slide 58 in the PDF
                      • Observed increment = latent increment + error increment
                        • \((\ln\sigma_{t+\Delta} - \ln\sigma_t) + (e_{t+\Delta} - e_t)\)
                        • if \(e_t\) is i.i.d. and independent of \(\sigma\), the variances add: \(\mathrm{Var}(e_{t+\Delta} - e_t) = 2\,\mathrm{Var}(e_t)\) for every \(\Delta \ge 1\)
                        • hence \(m(2,\Delta) = c\,\Delta^{2H} + k\), \(c = \nu^2\), \(k = 2\,\mathrm{Var}(e_t)\): a constant that does not grow with \(\Delta\)
                      • Toy: \(H = 0.5\), \(c = 1\), \(k = 1\)
                        • \(m(2,1) = 1 + 1 = 2\), \(m(2,2) = 2 + 1 = 3\): log–log slope \(\ln(3/2)/\ln 2 = 0.58\), so \(\hat H = 0.58/2 = 0.29\) instead of 0.5
                        • at long lags the constant matters less: from \(\Delta = 20\) to 40, slope \(\ln(41/21)/\ln 2 = 0.97\), \(\hat H = 0.48\)
                      • Testable implication: under i.i.d. error, \(\hat H\) rises when short lags are dropped
                        • the mini-case in section AI for Scientific Discovery runs exactly this check on SPY

                        Case Study: Is Zero the Best Threshold?

                        Slide 62 in the PDF
                        • Bollerslev, Medeiros, Patton & Quaedvlieg (2022), Journal of Econometrics: is the sign of the return, i.e. the threshold 0 of semivariances, the best way to split yesterday's RV for forecasting?
                          • SHAR Patton & Sheppard (2015) splits yesterday's RV into the negative and the positive semivariance
                          • the threshold 0 has an economic motivation, loss aversion, but statistically it is arbitrary
                        • Partial variance (PV): the part of RV contributed by the returns between two thresholds
                          • \(\text{PV}^{(g)}_t = \sum_i r_{t,i}^2\,\mathbf{1}\{c_{g-1} < r_{t,i} \le c_g\}\), with \(c_0 = -\infty\) and \(c_G = +\infty\), so \(\sum_{g=1}^{G} \text{PV}^{(g)}_t = \RV_t\)
                          • \(G = 2\) and \(c_1 = 0\): the semivariances; \(G = 1\): RV
                        • PV(\(G\))-HAR: in HAR, yesterday's RV is replaced by the \(G\) partial variances
                          • the weekly and monthly components stay RV; SV-HAR is SHAR, RV-HAR is HAR
                        • The paper's claims, on SPY and 28 DJIA stocks, 1997–2013
                          • with a single threshold, the threshold 0 is hard to beat
                          • two thresholds, one in the left tail and one in the right, improve the forecasts significantly
                          • for large covariance matrices, the threshold 0 remains the robust choice

                          Subsampling and Two-Scale RV

                          Slide 83 in the PDF
                          • Subsampling: compute RV on \(K\) shifted grids and average
                            • offsets \(0, 1, \ldots, K-1\); \(\RV^{(k)}_t\): RV on the grid with offset \(k\)
                            • \(\RV^{\text{avg}}_t = \frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=0}^{K-1} \RV^{(k)}_t\): same bias as one sparse grid, smaller variance
                          • Two-scale RV (TSRV) Zhang, Mykland & Aït-Sahalia (2005): remove the remaining bias with the all-data RV
                            • \(\text{TSRV}_t = \RV^{\text{avg}}_t - \frac{\bar n}{n}\RV^{\text{all}}_t\), \(\bar n = K^{-1}\sum_k n_k\), \(n_k\): returns on grid \(k\); \(\RV^{\text{all}}_t\): RV on all \(n\) returns, which estimates the noise term \(2n\omega^2\)
                            • standard grids (grid \(k\) uses the prices \(k, k+K, \ldots\)): \(\bar n = (n - K + 1)/K\), e.g. \(73/6 = 12.17\) for \(n = 78\), \(K = 6\)
                            • adding the day's first and last price to every grid gives \(83/6 = 13.83\), but the common end-point noise then does not vanish: a finite-sample approximation, not the consistent TSRV
                            • In small samples, divide TSRV by \(1 - \bar n/n\)

                          Realised Kernels

                          Slide 84 in the PDF
                          Neil Shephard, 2004 Photo: Renate Schmid (2004); CC BY-SA 2.0 de; Wikimedia Commons
                          • Realised kernel Barndorff-Nielsen, Hansen, Lunde & Shephard (2008): add weighted autocovariances of returns
                            • \(\text{RK}_t = \gamma_0 + 2\sum_{h=1}^{H} k\!\left(\frac{h}{H+1}\right)\gamma_h\), \(\gamma_h = \sum_i r_{t,i}\,r_{t,i-h}\)
                            • Parzen weight function \(k\): 1 at 0, falling smoothly to 0 at 1; keeps RK positive
                          • Noise makes \(\gamma_1\) negative
                            • the kernel adds it back and cancels the bias
                          • Bandwidth \(H\) grows with the noise-to-signal ratio
                            • \(H \approx 3.5\,\xi^{4/5} n^{3/5}\), with \(\xi^2 = \omega^2/\sqrt{\text{IQ}}\) the noise-to-signal ratio
                            • course code (a heuristic pilot): \(\hat\omega^2 = \RV/(2n)\), and the 20-minute RV in place of \(\sqrt{\text{IQ}}\)
                          • Jittering: the first and last prices are replaced by the averages of the first and last two prices, so the end-point noise does not stay in RK

                          Itô Semimartingales and Stable Convergence

                          Slide 98 in the PDF
                          • Itô semimartingale: \(dp = \mu\,ds + \sigma\,dW + dJ\)
                            • \(\mu\), \(\sigma\) adapted (known from the past at each time)
                            • \(\sigma\) càdlàg (right-continuous with left limits, so it may jump)
                            • \(\sigma\) may depend on \(W\) (leverage)
                            • Without jumps: \(\sqrt{M}(\RV_t - \IV_t) \xrightarrow{\mathcal{L}\text{-}s} \text{MN}(0, 2\,\text{IQ}_t)\), a mixed Normal distribution whose variance \(2\,\text{IQ}_t\), with integrated quarticity IQ, \(\text{IQ}_t = \int \sigma^4(s)\,ds\), is itself random Barndorff-Nielsen & Shephard (2002)
                          • Stable convergence (\(\mathcal{L}\)-\(s\)) Mykland & Zhang (2009)
                            • the limit holds jointly with every variable of the path's \(\sigma\)-field
                            • Hence \((\RV_t - \IV_t)/\sqrt{2\,\text{RQ}_t/M} \to N(0,1)\): studentising by \(\sqrt{2\,\text{RQ}_t/M}\), with \(\text{RQ}_t \to \text{IQ}_t\) random, is legitimate only under stable convergence
                          • Finite samples: the \(t\)-statistic for \(\ln \RV_t\) has better coverage than the one in levels Barndorff-Nielsen & Shephard (2005)
                            • the confidence intervals of the lecture use it

                          Deriving the Noise Bias and the Optimal Frequency

                          Slide 99 in the PDF
                          • Observed return \(r_i = r^*_i + \varepsilon_i - \varepsilon_{i-1}\), with \(\varepsilon\) i.i.d. \((0, \omega^2)\) and independent of \(p^*\)
                            • Given the path of \(\sigma\) (zero drift, no leverage: \(\sigma\) independent of \(W\)): \(\E[\RV^{(n)} \mid \sigma] = \sum_i \E[r^{*2}_i \mid \sigma] + \sum_i \E[(\varepsilon_i - \varepsilon_{i-1})^2] = \IV + 2n\omega^2\)
                            • \(\mathrm{cov}(r_i, r_{i-1}) = -\E[\varepsilon_{i-1}^2] = -\omega^2\)
                            • By a law of large numbers for the noise terms, \(\RV^{(n)}/(2n) \xrightarrow{p} \omega^2\) as \(n \to \infty\): the all-tick RV estimates the noise, not IV Bandi & Russell (2008)
                          • MSE: \(\E[(\RV^{(n)} - \IV)^2] \approx \underbrace{2\,\text{IQ}/n}_{\text{discretisation}} + \underbrace{4n^2\omega^4}_{\text{squared bias}} + O(n)\)
                            • First-order condition \(-2\,\text{IQ}/n^2 + 8n\omega^4 = 0\) gives \(n^* = \left(\text{IQ}/(4\omega^4)\right)^{1/3}\) Bandi & Russell (2008)

                          • \(\RV_t = \IV_t + u_t\), with \(\E[u_t \mid \sigma] = 0\) and \(\mathrm{Var}(u_t \mid \sigma) = 2\,\text{IQ}_t/M\)
                            • the CLT of the section Realised Variance
                            • If \(\IV_{t+1} = \beta_0 + \beta\,\IV_t + \eta_{t+1}\), OLS of \(\RV_{t+1}\) on \(\RV_t\) gives \(\text{plim}\,\hat\beta = \beta\,\dfrac{\mathrm{Var}(\IV)}{\mathrm{Var}(\IV) + \E[2\,\text{IQ}/M]} < \beta\) for \(\beta > 0\): attenuation (\(\eta_{t+1}\): the shock to IV; plim: probability limit)
                          • If the signal variance does not rise with \(\text{IQ}_t\), the signal-to-noise ratio of \(\RV_t\) falls when \(\text{IQ}_t\) is high
                            • a working approximation
                            • HARQ approximates the falling weight by \(\beta_d + \beta_Q\sqrt{\text{RQ}_t}\), \(\beta_Q < 0\) Bollerslev, Patton & Quaedvlieg (2016)

                          Beyond HAR, Semivariances and Joint Return–RV Models (2/2)

                          Slide 113 in the PDF
                          • Realized GARCH Hansen, Huang & Shek (2012): a GARCH equation plus a measurement equation
                            • the GARCH equation is driven by the realised measure \(x_t\)
                            • \(\ln h_t = \omega + \beta\ln h_{t-1} + \gamma \ln x_{t-1}\), \(\ln x_t = \xi + \varphi\ln h_t + \tau(z_t) + u_t\), \(r_t = \sqrt{h_t}\,z_t\): joint quasi-maximum likelihood

                            • \(\xi\), \(\varphi\): measurement-equation coefficients, \(u_t\): its error
                          • HEAVY (high-frequency-based volatility) models Shephard & Sheppard (2010): consistent multi-step forecasts
                            • one GARCH-type equation for the daily variance, driven by lagged RV
                            • one equation for RV itself
