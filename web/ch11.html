Central chart: Which Model Reproduces the S&P 500?

Osborne (1959) described the logarithms of stock prices with the tools of statistical physics: not prices but their logarithms behave like Brownian motion. The resulting model, geometric Brownian motion (GBM), is the price model behind the formula of Black & Scholes (1973). Prices are recorded daily or every few milliseconds, and continuous time gives one language for all these scales: Itô calculus, stochastic differential equations (SDEs) and the change of measure. The chapter's question is whether a single model can describe prices at any time scale.

The daily log returns of the S&P 500 since 1990 reject GBM in two respects. The excess kurtosis is 10.9 instead of 0, the worst day lies at −11.3 standard deviations, and 62 days lie beyond ±4 standard deviations, against 0.59 expected under the Normal distribution. The autocorrelation of absolute returns is 0.27 at lag 1 and still 0.14 at lag 50, so volatility comes in clusters (volatility clustering). Quadratic variation grows in steps: between September 2008 and March 2009, 1.6% of the days bring 15.2% of the quadratic variation of the whole sample. The drift cannot be pinned down from the data either: for a precision of ±1 percentage point, the S&P 500 would need 1,243 years of data and Bitcoin 17,028 years.

The chapter adds mean reversion, jumps (the Merton model) and stochastic volatility (the Heston model) in turn, then compares the estimated models with the data on 100 simulated samples of 9,245 days each. Merton reproduces the Hill tail statistic, 2.70 against 2.56 in the data, but not volatility clustering. Heston reproduces volatility clustering, with an autocorrelation of 0.28 at lag 1 against 0.27 in the data. No estimated model reaches the excess kurtosis of the data, 10.88: Merton gives 3.66 and Heston 3.21. The case studies show what one-factor models also miss: on SPY, the noise-robust non-linear least squares (NLLS) estimator gives a roughness exponent of −0.35, against 0 for Brownian motion.

Chapter Route

The chapter builds one language for prices observed at any frequency. The first part constructs Brownian motion as the limit of a rescaled random walk, develops Itô calculus from quadratic variation, and uses it to solve and simulate stochastic differential equations: geometric Brownian motion (GBM) for prices and the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck (OU) process for mean-reverting variables. The second part asks which parameters the data identify, changes the probability measure to price claims, and adds jumps and stochastic volatility. Each model is estimated on the same data and then compared with the S&P 500 on three yardsticks.

How do we describe prices in continuous time?

Prices are recorded at very different frequencies: daily closes for long histories, five-minute bars for recent years, and quotes every few milliseconds in electronic markets. From Random Walk to Brownian Motion

Brownian motion is the building block of every model in the chapter. This section obtains it as the limit of the random walk used for log prices in Chapter 2, and lists the properties that make it both tractable and unusual: Gaussian independent increments, the martingale property, self-similarity and paths that are continuous but nowhere differentiable.

From Random Walk to Brownian Motion: Donsker's Theorem (1/3)

Slide 4 in the PDF

Prices move in discrete steps, and the question is which process arises when the steps become smaller and more frequent. Start from the random walk \(S_0 = 0\), \(S_k = S_{k-1} + \xi_k = \xi_1 + \cdots + \xi_k\), where the steps \(\xi_i\) are independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) with mean 0 and variance 1. In the log-price model of Chapter 2, \(\xi_k\) is the standardised return of day \(k\).

To pass to continuous time, put \(n\) steps in each unit of time and rescale:

\[W_n(t) = \frac{S_{\lfloor nt \rfloor}}{\sqrt{n}}, \qquad \operatorname{Var}\big(W_n(t)\big) = \frac{\lfloor nt \rfloor}{n} \to t,\]
(11.1)

where \(\lfloor nt \rfloor\) is the integer part of \(nt\). The factor \(\sqrt{n}\) is the only one that gives a non-degenerate limit: dividing by \(n\) would send the process to zero, and not dividing at all would make the variance explode. For each fixed \(t\), the central limit theorem (CLT) gives \(W_n(t) \to N(0, t)\).

Donsker's theorem is stronger: \(W_n \Rightarrow W\), where \(\Rightarrow\) denotes convergence in distribution of the whole path, not only of its value at each date (Billingsley, 1999). The limit \(W\) is Brownian motion, whatever the distribution of the steps. Functionals of the path, such as its maximum, therefore also converge to those of Brownian motion.

In brief

  • Question: which process do we get as price steps become smaller and more frequent?
    • prices move in discrete steps
  • Random walk: \(S_0 = 0\), \(S_k = S_{k-1} + \xi_k = \xi_1 + \cdots + \xi_k\)
    • steps \(\xi_i\) i.i.d. (independent and identically distributed), mean 0 and variance 1
    • it is the log-price model of Chapter 2
    • \(\xi_k\) is the standardised return of day \(k\)
  • Scaled random walk: \(W_n(t) = S_{\lfloor nt \rfloor}/\sqrt{n}\), with \(n\) steps per unit of time
    • \(\lfloor nt \rfloor\): integer part of \(nt\)
    • why \(\sqrt{n}\): \(\Var(W_n(t)) = \lfloor nt \rfloor/n \to t\)
    • divided by \(n\) the limit would be 0
    • undivided the variance would explode
  • Central limit theorem (CLT): \(W_n(t) \to N(0, t)\) for each \(t\) separately
  • Donsker's theorem: \(W_n \Rightarrow W\) for the whole path Billingsley (1999)
    • \(\Rightarrow\): convergence in distribution
    • holds whatever the law of the steps

    Brownian Motion: Definition and Properties

    Slide 7 in the PDF

    A standard Brownian motion \((W_t)_{t \ge 0}\) is defined by four properties: \(W_0 = 0\); increments over disjoint intervals are independent; \(W_t - W_s \sim N(0, t - s)\) for \(s < t\); and the paths are continuous. Its existence was established by Wiener (1923). On a grid it is simulated exactly by \(W_{t + \Delta t} = W_t + \sqrt{\Delta t}\,Z\), with \(Z \sim N(0, 1)\).

    Several properties follow from the definition. Brownian motion is a Gaussian process with \(\operatorname{Cov}(W_s, W_t) = \min(s, t)\). It is a martingale: \(\mathbb{E}[W_t \mid \mathcal{F}_s] = W_s\), where \(\mathcal{F}_s\) is the information available at \(s\); this is the continuous-time version of the efficient-market random walk of Chapter 2. It is self-similar: for any \(c > 0\), the rescaled process \(\{c^{-1/2} W_{ct}\}\) is again a standard Brownian motion, so a zoomed piece of a path has the same law as the whole path.

    The last property is the one that changes the calculus. The paths are continuous but nowhere differentiable: the difference quotient \((W_{t+h} - W_t)/h\) has standard deviation \(1/\sqrt{h}\), which tends to infinity as \(h \to 0\). Ordinary calculus, which relies on derivatives, does not apply to Brownian paths. Paths and their scaling are illustrated in Further Topics.

    In brief

    • Standard Brownian motion: four defining properties
      • \(W_0 = 0\)
      • independent increments
      • \(W_t - W_s \sim N(0, t - s)\) for \(s < t\)
      • continuous paths
      • existence: Wiener (1923)
      • simulation on a grid: \(W_{t + \Delta t} = W_t + \sqrt{\Delta t}\,Z\), \(Z \sim N(0, 1)\)
    • A Gaussian process with \(\Cov(W_s, W_t) = \min(s, t)\)
    • A martingale: \(\E[W_t \mid \mathcal{F}_s] = W_s\)
      • \(\mathcal{F}_s\): the information up to \(s\)
      • the efficient-market random walk of Chapter 2
    • Self-similarity: \(\{c^{-1/2} W_{ct}\}\) is again a standard Brownian motion for any \(c > 0\)
    • Paths are continuous but nowhere differentiable
      • \((W_{t + h} - W_t)/h\) has standard deviation \(1/\sqrt{h} \to \infty\)
      • ordinary calculus fails

    Section summary

    Donsker's theorem justifies Brownian motion as the limit of any random walk with finite-variance steps. Its paths are continuous but too irregular for ordinary calculus; the next section measures this irregularity and builds the calculus that replaces it.

    Quadratic Variation and Itô Calculus

    A Brownian path has infinite length but a finite and deterministic sum of squared increments. This section defines the two notions of variation, measures them on simulations and on the S&P 500, and uses quadratic variation to build the Itô integral and Itô's lemma, the chain rule of stochastic calculus.

    Total and Quadratic Variation

    Slide 8 in the PDF

    Why does ordinary calculus fail on a Brownian path, and what replaces it? Take a grid \(0 = t_0 < \cdots < t_n = T\) with step \(\Delta t = T/n\) and increments \(\Delta W_k = W_{t_k} - W_{t_{k-1}}\). The total variation \(\sum_k |\Delta W_k|\), the length of the path measured on the grid, has mean \(\sqrt{2nT/\pi}\), which tends to infinity: a Brownian path has infinite length.

    The quadratic variation behaves differently:

    \[[W]_T = \lim_{n \to \infty} \sum_{k=1}^{n} (\Delta W_k)^2 = T, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(Q_n) = \frac{2T^2}{n},\]
    (11.2)

    where \(Q_n = \sum_{k=1}^{n} (\Delta W_k)^2\). In (11.2) a sum of random terms has a deterministic limit, because its variance vanishes as the grid becomes finer. With one year of daily data the relative error of \(Q_n\) is \(\sqrt{2/252}\), about 9%. This is the idea behind realised variance in Chapter 9.

    Itô calculus summarises (11.2) in the shorthand \((\mathrm{d}W_t)^2 = \mathrm{d}t\): over a short interval the squared increment is not negligible relative to the length of the interval, as it would be for a differentiable path, but equal to it in the limit.

    In brief

    • Question: why does ordinary calculus fail on a Brownian path, and what replaces it?
    • Total variation \(\sum_k |\Delta W_k|\)
      • grid \(0 = t_0 < \cdots < t_n = T\), step \(\Delta t = T/n\), \(\Delta W_k = W_{t_{k}} - W_{t_{k-1}}\)
      • its mean is \(\sqrt{2nT/\pi} \to \infty\)
      • a Brownian path has infinite length
    • Quadratic variation \([W]_T = \lim \sum_k (\Delta W_k)^2 = T\)
      • a random sum with a deterministic limit
      • \(Q_n = \sum_{k=1}^{n} (\Delta W_k)^2\), \(\Var(Q_n) = 2T^2/n\)
      • one year of daily data gives about 9% relative error (\(\sqrt{2/252}\))
      • the idea behind realised variance (Chapter 9)
    • Shorthand of Itô calculus: \((\dd W_t)^2 = \dd t\)

      The Itô Integral (1/2)

      Slide 11 in the PDF

      The gain from holding \(H_t\) units of an asset whose price moves like \(W\) is \(\int_0^T H_t\,\mathrm{d}W_t\). The integral cannot be defined path by path as a Riemann–Stieltjes integral, because the total variation of \(W\) is infinite. The Itô integral defines it as a limit in mean square of sums in which the integrand is evaluated at the left end point of each interval:

      \[\int_0^T H_t\,\mathrm{d}W_t = \lim_{n \to \infty} \sum_{k=1}^{n} H_{t_{k-1}}\,\big(W_{t_k} - W_{t_{k-1}}\big).\]
      (11.3)

      The integrand must be adapted, that is, \(H_t\) uses only the information available up to \(t\), and must satisfy \(\mathbb{E}\big[\int_0^T H_t^2\,\mathrm{d}t\big] < \infty\). The left end point has a financial meaning: the position over \([t_{k-1}, t_k]\) is chosen before the next price move is known.

      In brief

      • Goal: a meaning for \(\int_0^T H_t\,\dd W_t\)
        • the gain of holding \(H_t\) units of an asset priced \(W\)
        • Riemann–Stieltjes integration fails because the total variation is infinite
      • Itô integral: the limit in mean square of sums with the integrand at the left end point
        • \(\int_0^T H_t\,\dd W_t = \lim_{n \to \infty} \sum_{k=1}^{n} H_{t_{k-1}}\,(W_{t_k} - W_{t_{k-1}})\)
        • for \(H\) adapted with \(\E\left[\int_0^T H_t^2\,\dd t\right] < \infty\)
        • \(H_t\) uses only information up to \(t\)
        • the position is chosen before the next price move

      The Itô Integral (2/2)

      Slide 12 in the PDF

      The Itô integral has zero mean, \(\mathbb{E}\big[\int_0^T H_t\,\mathrm{d}W_t\big] = 0\), and its second moment is given by the Itô isometry,

      \[\mathbb{E}\Big[\Big(\int_0^T H_t\,\mathrm{d}W_t\Big)^2\Big] = \mathbb{E}\Big[\int_0^T H_t^2\,\mathrm{d}t\Big].\]
      (11.4)

      Moreover, \(t \mapsto \int_0^t H_s\,\mathrm{d}W_s\) is a martingale: no trading strategy in a driftless asset earns money on average. Under weaker integrability conditions the integral is only a local martingale; the details are in the Appendix.

      The choice of the evaluation point matters. The Stratonovich integral evaluates the integrand at the average of the end points, \(\tfrac12(H_{t_{k-1}} + H_{t_k})\), and gives a different number, because the extra term picks up the quadratic variation. Finance uses the Itô integral, since a position evaluated at the right end point would use the price at \(t_k\) before it is known. A worked example, \(\int_0^T W_t\,\mathrm{d}W_t\), is in Further Topics.

      In brief

      • Zero mean: \(\E\left[\int_0^T H_t\,\dd W_t\right] = 0\)
      • Itô isometry: \(\E\left[\left(\int_0^T H_t\,\dd W_t\right)^2\right] = \E\left[\int_0^T H_t^2\,\dd t\right]\)
      • \(t \mapsto \int_0^t H_s\,\dd W_s\) is a martingale
        • no strategy earns money on average
        • weaker conditions: Appendix
      • Stratonovich integral: the integrand averaged over the end points, \(\tfrac12(H_{t_{k-1}} + H_{t_k})\)
        • it gives a different number

      Itô Processes and Itô's Lemma

      Slide 13 in the PDF

      An Itô process is written \(\mathrm{d}X_t = a_t\,\mathrm{d}t + b_t\,\mathrm{d}W_t\), shorthand for \(X_t = X_0 + \int_0^t a_s\,\mathrm{d}s + \int_0^t b_s\,\mathrm{d}W_s\), with an adapted drift \(a_t\) and an adapted diffusion coefficient \(b_t\). For a function \(f(t, x)\) that is continuously differentiable once in \(t\) and twice in \(x\), Itô's lemma (Itô, 1944) gives

      \[\mathrm{d}f(t, X_t) = \Big(f_t + a_t f_x + \tfrac{1}{2} b_t^2 f_{xx}\Big)\,\mathrm{d}t + b_t f_x\,\mathrm{d}W_t,\]
      (11.5)

      where \(f_t = \partial f/\partial t\), \(f_x = \partial f/\partial x\) and \(f_{xx} = \partial^2 f/\partial x^2\) are evaluated at \((t, X_t)\).

      The lemma follows from a Taylor expansion to second order, with the multiplication rules \(\mathrm{d}t \cdot \mathrm{d}t = 0\), \(\mathrm{d}t \cdot \mathrm{d}W = 0\) and \(\mathrm{d}W \cdot \mathrm{d}W = \mathrm{d}t\). In ordinary calculus the second-order term disappears; here \((\mathrm{d}X_t)^2 = b_t^2\,\mathrm{d}t\) by (11.2), so the term \(\tfrac{1}{2} b_t^2 f_{xx}\,\mathrm{d}t\) remains. This term is the whole difference from the ordinary chain rule, and it reappears in every model of the chapter. The multivariate version, with quadratic covariation, is in Further Topics.

      In brief

      • Itô process: \(\dd X_t = a_t\,\dd t + b_t\,\dd W_t\)
        • drift \(a_t\), diffusion coefficient \(b_t\), both adapted
        • shorthand for \(X_t = X_0 + \int_0^t a_s\,\dd s + \int_0^t b_s\,\dd W_s\)
      • Itô's lemma: for \(f(t, x)\) continuously differentiable once in \(t\) and twice in \(x\) Itô (1944)
        • \(\dd f(t, X_t) = \left(f_t + a_t f_x + \tfrac{1}{2} b_t^2 f_{xx}\right)\dd t + b_t f_x\,\dd W_t\)
        • partial derivatives evaluated at \((t, X_t)\)
        • \(f_t = \partial f/\partial t\), \(f_x = \partial f/\partial x\), \(f_{xx} = \partial^2 f/\partial x^2\)
      • Rule of thumb: Taylor expansion to second order
        • with \(\dd t \cdot \dd t = 0\), \(\dd t \cdot \dd W = 0\), \(\dd W \cdot \dd W = \dd t\)
        • the second-order term \(\tfrac{1}{2} b_t^2 f_{xx}\,\dd t\) does not vanish
        • this is the whole novelty

      Stochastic Differential Equations and Simulation

      Most models of the chapter are stochastic differential equations (SDEs) whose drift and diffusion depend on the current state. Some have exact solutions, others must be simulated on a grid. This section introduces the two standard schemes and the measure of their accuracy along paths.

      Stochastic Differential Equations and the Euler–Maruyama Scheme

      Slide 14 in the PDF

      A stochastic differential equation specifies the dynamics of \(X\) through functions of the current state:

      \[\mathrm{d}X_t = a(t, X_t)\,\mathrm{d}t + b(t, X_t)\,\mathrm{d}W_t, \qquad X_0 \text{ given}.\]
      (11.6)

      GBM, the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process and the Merton model have exact transition distributions, so they can be simulated on any grid with no discretisation error. The Heston model has a closed-form characteristic function but no elementary transition that is easy to simulate; exact simulation exists Broadie & Kaya (2006) but is costly. Conditions for existence and uniqueness of solutions are in the Appendix.

      The simplest approximation, the Euler–Maruyama scheme (Higham, 2001), freezes the coefficients over each step:

      \[X_{k+1} = X_k + a(X_k)\,\Delta t + b(X_k)\,\Delta W_k, \qquad \Delta W_k = \sqrt{\Delta t}\,Z_k,\]
      (11.7)

      where \(X_k\) is the approximation at \(t_k = k\Delta t\) and the \(Z_k\) are i.i.d. \(N(0, 1)\). The question is how large the resulting discretisation error is.

      The Milstein Scheme and Strong Convergence

      Slide 15 in the PDF

      The Milstein scheme (Milstein, 1975) adds the next term of the Itô–Taylor expansion:

      \[X_{k+1} = X_k + a(X_k)\,\Delta t + b(X_k)\,\Delta W_k + \tfrac{1}{2}\, b(X_k)\, b'(X_k)\,\big[(\Delta W_k)^2 - \Delta t\big],\]
      (11.8)

      with \(b' = \mathrm{d}b/\mathrm{d}x\). The correction has conditional mean zero, because \(\mathbb{E}[(\Delta W_k)^2] = \Delta t\), so it does not change the expected value of the step; it improves the accuracy of each path.

      Path accuracy is measured by the strong error, \(\mathbb{E}|X_T - X_T^{\Delta t}| \le C\,\Delta t^{\gamma}\), where \(X_T\) is the exact solution, \(X_T^{\Delta t}\) the scheme with step \(\Delta t\), \(C\) a constant and \(\gamma\) the strong order. Euler–Maruyama has \(\gamma = 1/2\) and Milstein \(\gamma = 1\). The strong order matters for path-dependent quantities, such as hedging errors and barrier crossings. Expected values of functions of \(X_T\) depend on the weak order instead, which is discussed with a numerical comparison of the two schemes in Further Topics.

      Geometric Brownian Motion

      Geometric Brownian motion is the reference model for prices: it keeps prices positive and gives returns whose distribution does not depend on the price level. This section solves it with Itô's lemma, estimates it by maximum likelihood, shows how imprecisely the drift is identified, and tests its implications on the S&P 500.

      Geometric Brownian Motion (GBM) from Itô's Lemma (1/2)

      Slide 16 in the PDF

      A price model should keep prices positive and give percentage returns with the same distribution at every price level. GBM, proposed by Osborne (1959) and Samuelson (1965) and used by Black & Scholes (1973), satisfies

      \[\mathrm{d}S_t = \mu S_t\,\mathrm{d}t + \sigma S_t\,\mathrm{d}W_t,\]
      (11.9)

      so the percentage change \(\mathrm{d}S_t/S_t\) has constant drift \(\mu\) and volatility \(\sigma\) per year.

      Itô's lemma (11.5) with \(f(x) = \ln x\), \(f_x = 1/x\), \(f_{xx} = -1/x^2\), \(a_t = \mu S_t\) and \(b_t = \sigma S_t\) gives

      \[\begin{gathered} \mathrm{d}\ln S_t = \big(\mu - \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2\big)\,\mathrm{d}t + \sigma\,\mathrm{d}W_t, \\ S_t = S_0 \exp\Big(\big(\mu - \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2\big)t + \sigma W_t\Big). \end{gathered}\]
      (11.10)

      The log price is a Brownian motion with drift, so prices stay positive, and exact simulation needs only Normal draws.

      By (11.10) the log price grows at the log drift \(m = \mu - \sigma^2/2\), not at \(\mu\). With \(\sigma = 18\%\) the cost is \(\sigma^2/2 = 1.6\) percentage points a year; with \(\sigma = 67\%\), the volatility of Bitcoin, it reaches 22 percentage points a year.

      GBM has sharp testable implications. The log returns \(r_t = \ln(S_t/S_{t - \Delta t})\) are i.i.d. \(N(m\Delta t, \sigma^2\Delta t)\) at every frequency. The rest of the section estimates the model and checks these implications. The distribution of future prices under GBM is illustrated in Further Topics.

      Estimating GBM by Maximum Likelihood (1/2)

      Slide 18 in the PDF

      With \(n\) log returns at step \(\Delta t\) over a span of \(T = n\Delta t\) years, maximum likelihood estimation (MLE) gives

      \[\hat\sigma^2 = \frac{1}{n\Delta t}\sum_t (r_t - \bar r)^2, \qquad \hat m = \frac{\bar r}{\Delta t}, \qquad \hat\mu = \hat m + \frac{\hat\sigma^2}{2},\]

      where \(\bar r\) is the mean log return. Under GBM the two estimators have very different precision:

      \[\mathrm{SE}(\hat\sigma) = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{2n}}, \qquad \mathrm{SE}(\hat m) = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{T}}.\]
      (11.11)

      The standard error (SE) of \(\hat\sigma\) shrinks with the number of observations, whereas the SE of \(\hat m\) depends only on the calendar span \(T\). The reason is that \(\sum_t r_t = \ln(S_T/S_0)\): the estimator of the drift uses only the first and the last price, however many prices lie between them.

For Bitcoin, over 12.0 years, the log drift of 43.1% has an SE of 19.2 percentage points.

          Mean Reversion: the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck Process

          Interest rates, volatility indices and spreads do not drift away like stock prices; they are pulled back towards a normal level. The Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process is the simplest diffusion with this property. This section defines it, shows that its discrete samples follow an autoregression of order 1, introduces the two classical short-rate models built on it, and estimates the Vasicek model on the 3-month Treasury bill rate.

          The Ornstein–Uhlenbeck (OU) Process

          Slide 24 in the PDF

          How do we model a variable that returns to a normal level? The Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process (Uhlenbeck & Ornstein, 1930) solves

          \[\mathrm{d}x_t = \kappa(\theta - x_t)\,\mathrm{d}t + \sigma\,\mathrm{d}W_t, \qquad \kappa > 0,\]
          (11.12)

          where \(\theta\) is the long-run mean, \(\kappa\) the speed of mean reversion and \(\sigma\) the volatility. Above \(\theta\) the drift is negative, below \(\theta\) it is positive: the drift acts as a force that pulls \(x\) back to \(\theta\), with a strength proportional to the distance. The process is used for interest rates (Vasicek, 1977), volatility, spreads and commodity prices.

          The speed is usually reported as a half-life, \(\ln 2/\kappa\), the time needed for the expected deviation from \(\theta\) to halve. With \(\kappa = 0.5\) per year the half-life is 1.39 years. The solution of (11.12), obtained with Itô's lemma in three steps, is derived in Further Topics: \(x_t\) is Normal, with a conditional mean that decays exponentially towards \(\theta\) and a variance that converges to \(\sigma^2/(2\kappa)\).

          Exact Discretisation: an AR(1)

          Slide 25 in the PDF

          Sampled at step \(\Delta t\), the OU process is exactly an autoregressive model of order 1, AR(1):

          \[\begin{gathered} x_{t+\Delta t} = a + b\,x_t + \varepsilon_t, \\ b = e^{-\kappa\Delta t}, \quad a = \theta(1 - b), \quad \operatorname{Var}(\varepsilon_t) = \frac{\sigma^2}{2\kappa}\,(1 - b^2). \end{gathered}\]
          (11.13)

          The errors \(\varepsilon_t\) are i.i.d. Normal, so the maximum-likelihood estimator coincides with ordinary least squares (OLS) of \(x_{t + \Delta t}\) on \(x_t\). OLS gives the intercept \(\hat a\), the slope \(\hat b\) and the residual standard deviation \(\hat s_\varepsilon\), and the mapping (11.13) is inverted: \(\hat\kappa = -\ln\hat b/\Delta t\), \(\hat\theta = \hat a/(1 - \hat b)\) and \(\hat\sigma = \hat s_\varepsilon\sqrt{2\hat\kappa/(1 - \hat b^2)}\).

          Standard errors of \(\hat\kappa\) and \(\hat\theta\) follow from the delta method applied to the OLS covariance of \((\hat a, \hat b)\). Because \(\hat\kappa\) depends on \(\hat b\) only, with \(\partial\kappa/\partial b = -1/(b\Delta t)\), the variance of \(\hat b\) suffices (Seminar 11, A1).

          With daily data, \(b = e^{-\kappa\Delta t}\) is very close to 1, so the regression is close to a unit root. The speed \(\kappa\) is then estimated imprecisely, for the same reason that unit-root tests have low power. Three speeds of reversion are compared on simulated paths in Further Topics.

            Estimating Diffusions: What Is Identified from What

            The estimates so far share a pattern: volatility is precise, while the drift of GBM and the speed of the OU process are not. This section explains the pattern with two asymptotic schemes. Infill asymptotics refines the grid on a fixed span; long-span asymptotics lengthens the span. Each identifies a different set of parameters.

            Infill Asymptotics: What Sampling More Often Buys

            Slide 29 in the PDF

            Which parameters do we learn from sampling more often, and which only from more years? Under infill asymptotics the step \(\Delta\) tends to zero on a fixed interval \([0, T]\). For an Itô process the realised quadratic variation converges:

            \[\sum_k (\Delta X_k)^2 \to \int_0^T b_t^2\,\mathrm{d}t.\]
            (11.15)

            The quadratic variation, and hence, under regularity conditions, \(b_t^2\) along the path, is identified without error in the limit. This requires that the quadratic variation be observable: a continuous Itô process measured without noise. Recovering the whole diffusion function \(b(x)\), or the parameters of a given specification, needs further conditions.

            Sampling more often therefore helps the volatility \(\sigma\) of GBM (Section 5) and the diffusion function \(b(x)\) of a general model, which can be estimated nonparametrically (Further Topics).

            In brief

            Long-Span Asymptotics: What Only More Years Buy

            Slide 30 in the PDF

            The drift is not consistently estimable on a fixed span. The Fisher information \(I(m) = -\mathbb{E}[\partial^2\ell/\partial m^2]\), where \(\ell\) is the log-likelihood, bounds the variance of any unbiased estimator, \(\operatorname{Var}(\hat m) \ge 1/I(m)\). For \(n\) GBM returns,

            \[I(m) = \frac{n\Delta t}{\sigma^2} = \frac{T}{\sigma^2}, \qquad \mathrm{SE}(\hat m) = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{T}},\]
            (11.16)

            so the information grows with the span \(T\), not with the number of observations \(n\).

Its asymptotic variance is \(\operatorname{Var}(\hat\kappa) \approx (e^{2\kappa\Delta} - 1)/(T\Delta)\), which tends to \(2\kappa/T\) as \(\Delta \to 0\) (derivation in Seminar 11, A1). The bias of \(\hat\kappa\) does not shrink with finer sampling either (Yu, 2012): as \(\Delta \to 0\), \(b = e^{-\kappa\Delta} \to 1\), and \(\hat\kappa\) is far from Normal whenever \(\kappa T\) is small.

            Change of Measure: Girsanov and Feynman–Kac

            Prices of traded claims are not computed with the real-world probabilities. This section shows why on a one-period example, states Girsanov's theorem, which builds the pricing measure in continuous time, interprets the change of drift as the market price of risk, and derives the pricing equation with the Feynman–Kac formula.

            Why Prices Use a Different Probability: a One-Period Example (1/2)

            Slide 31 in the PDF

            A stock costs 100 today and tomorrow is worth either 120 (up) or 90 (down). The interest rate is 0, and the real-world probability of the up state is \(p = 0.6\). A claim pays 1 in the up state and 0 in the down state.

            The claim can be replicated with \(\Delta\) shares and \(B\) in cash. The conditions \(120\Delta + B = 1\) and \(90\Delta + B = 0\) give \(\Delta = 1/30\) and \(B = -3\), so the replicating portfolio costs \(100/30 - 3 = 1/3\). Any other price for the claim would allow an arbitrage: buy the cheaper of the claim and its replica and sell the other.

            The same price is an expectation, but not under \(p\). The weight \(q = (100 - 90)/(120 - 90) = 1/3\) is the only one with \(120q + 90(1 - q) = 100\), that is, the only probability under which the stock is priced at its expected value, and the claim's price is \(q \cdot 1 = 1/3\). The real-world probability \(p\) never enters.

            Girsanov's Theorem (1/2)

            Slide 33 in the PDF

            Two measures are equivalent if they assign probability zero to the same events. An equivalent measure \(Q\) is defined from \(P\) through the Radon–Nikodym density

            \[\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}Q}{\mathrm{d}P}\right|_{\mathcal{F}_T} = Z_T = \exp\Big(-\int_0^T \theta_s\,\mathrm{d}W_s - \tfrac{1}{2}\int_0^T \theta_s^2\,\mathrm{d}s\Big),\]
            (11.17)

            with \(\theta_s\) adapted. Since \(Z_T > 0\) and \(\mathbb{E}^P[Z_T] = 1\), the density reweights the probability of each path: \(Q(A) = \mathbb{E}^P[Z_T\,\mathbf{1}_A]\).

            Girsanov (1960) shows that under \(Q\) the process \(\tilde W_t = W_t + \int_0^t \theta_s\,\mathrm{d}s\) is a Brownian motion. A sufficient condition is the Novikov condition, \(\mathbb{E}\big[e^{\frac{1}{2}\int_0^T \theta_s^2\,\mathrm{d}s}\big] < \infty\). Below, \(\theta_s\) will be the market price of risk.

              • \(\theta_s\) is adapted (below: the market price of risk)
              • \(Z_T > 0\) with \(\E^P[Z_T] = 1\) reweights the probability of each path

            Girsanov's Theorem (2/2)

With stochastic volatility, \(P\) and \(Q\) weight future variance paths differently, and historical variance is a benchmark for implied variance, not a calibration of it.

            Feynman–Kac: From Expectations to the Pricing PDE (1/2)

            Slide 37 in the PDF

            Under \(Q\) the price of a claim is a discounted expectation, and the Feynman–Kac formula (Kac, 1949) turns this expectation into a partial differential equation (PDE). If \(\mathrm{d}X = a(X)\,\mathrm{d}t + b(X)\,\mathrm{d}\tilde W\) under \(Q\), the function

            \[u(t, x) = \mathbb{E}^Q\Big[e^{-\int_t^T r_s\,\mathrm{d}s}\,g(X_T) \;\Big|\; X_t = x\Big]\]

            solves

            \[u_t + a\,u_x + \tfrac{1}{2}b^2 u_{xx} - r\,u = 0, \qquad u(T, x) = g(x).\]
            (11.19)

            Here \(u(t, x)\) is the price at \(t\) of the pay-off \(g(X_T)\) received at \(T\), discounted at the short rate \(r_s\).

            The proof uses the tools of the chapter. The discounted price \(e^{-\int_0^t r_s\,\mathrm{d}s}\,u(t, X_t)\) is a \(Q\)-martingale, so its drift, computed with Itô's lemma (11.5), must vanish; setting the drift to zero gives (11.19). By the same route Chapter 12 derives the Black–Scholes equation.

            In brief

            • If \(\dd X = a(X)\,\dd t + b(X)\,\dd\tilde W\) under \(Q\) Kac (1949)
              • \(u(t, x) = \E^Q\left[e^{-\int_t^T r_s\,\dd s}\,g(X_T) \mid X_t = x\right]\) solves a PDE (partial differential equation)
              • the PDE: \(u_t + a\,u_x + \tfrac{1}{2}b^2 u_{xx} - r\,u = 0\), \(u(T, x) = g(x)\)
              • \(u\) is the price at \(t\) of the pay-off \(g(X_T)\) at \(T\)
              • the pay-off is discounted at the short rate \(r_s\)
            • Proof idea: \(e^{-\int_0^t r_s \dd s}\,u(t, X_t)\) is a \(Q\)-martingale
              • so its Itô drift is zero
              • by the same route, Chapter 12 derives Black–Scholes

            Jumps: the Merton Model

            Brownian paths are continuous, but prices gap on news. This section adds compound Poisson jumps to GBM, writes the resulting return density as a Poisson mixture of Normal densities, estimates the model on three markets and compares the fitted density with the data.

            Merton's Jump-Diffusion (1/2)

            Slide 40 in the PDF

            Which model explains crash days that are practically impossible under GBM? On 19 October 1987 the Dow Jones fell 22.6%, about 20 standard deviations under GBM with 18% volatility. A move of this kind is a discontinuity, and a jump cannot be hedged by continuous trading (Merton, 1976).

            The Merton model adds a compound Poisson process to the log price:

            Merton's Jump-Diffusion (2/2)

            Slide 41 in the PDF

            Conditional on \(k\) jumps in an interval of length \(\Delta t\), the log return is Normal, \(N\big(m\Delta t + k\mu_J,\ \sigma^2\Delta t + k\sigma_J^2\big)\). Unconditionally it is a Poisson mixture of Normal densities, i.i.d. over time:

            \[f(r) = \sum_{k \ge 0} e^{-\lambda\Delta t}\,\frac{(\lambda\Delta t)^k}{k!}\;\varphi\big(r;\ m\Delta t + k\mu_J,\ \sigma^2\Delta t + k\sigma_J^2\big),\]
            (11.22)

            where the weight is the Poisson probability of \(k\) jumps and \(\varphi(r; \text{mean}, \text{variance})\) is the Normal density.

            The mixture gives the model its exact simulation: draw the number of jumps \(N \sim \mathrm{Poisson}(\lambda\Delta t)\), then draw the return from the Normal distribution with the conditional moments. The moments of the Merton return are derived from its cumulants in Further Topics.

              Stochastic Volatility: the Heston Model

              The step-wise growth of quadratic variation in Section 3 shows that volatility is random. The Heston model gives the variance its own mean-reverting dynamics, correlated with the price. This section states the model, explains how simulation must handle its square-root variance, and estimates its parameters from the VIX.

              The Heston Model (1/2)

              Slide 47 in the PDF

              Which model generates volatility clustering and negative skewness? GBM and Merton with constant parameters produce neither volatility clustering nor dependence between returns and volatility. Merton can produce negative marginal skewness, through jumps with \(\mu_J < 0\), but here the target is the negative skewness generated by the leverage effect.

              The model of Heston (1993) has two SDEs, one for the log price and one for the instantaneous variance \(v_t\):

              \[\begin{aligned} \mathrm{d}\ln S_t &= \big(\mu - \tfrac{1}{2}v_t\big)\,\mathrm{d}t + \sqrt{v_t}\,\mathrm{d}W_t^{(1)}, \\ \mathrm{d}v_t &= \kappa(\theta - v_t)\,\mathrm{d}t + \xi\sqrt{v_t}\,\mathrm{d}W_t^{(2)}, \end{aligned}\]
              (11.23)

              with \(\operatorname{Corr}(\mathrm{d}W^{(1)}, \mathrm{d}W^{(2)}) = \rho\). The variance is a CIR process, with long-run level \(\theta\), speed \(\kappa\) and volatility of volatility \(\xi\). The first equation is the log of GBM (11.10) with \(\sigma^2\) replaced by \(v_t\). Under the Feller condition \(2\kappa\theta \ge \xi^2\) (Feller, 1951) the variance never touches zero.

              The Heston Model (2/2)

              Slide 48 in the PDF

              A negative correlation \(\rho\) produces the leverage effect: a price fall raises the variance that scales the next moves, so large losses tend to be followed by more volatile periods, and returns are negatively skewed. The effect is measured on simulated returns in Section 11.

              The square-root variance requires care in simulation. A plain Euler step can make \(v\) negative, so the chapter uses the Euler scheme with full truncation, which replaces \(v\) by \(v^+ = \max(v, 0)\) in the coefficients (Lord, Koekkoek & van Dijk, 2010); the quadratic-exponential scheme of Andersen (2008) is an alternative.

              Daily returns need several steps per day. With a single step, the daily return is conditionally Normal given \(v\) at the start of the day, which removes the intraday channel through which \(\rho\) shapes skewness; the unconditional skewness can then be small, though not necessarily zero. Section 11 uses 390 steps per day, one per minute of a 6.5-hour trading session.

              The instantaneous variance is not observed. A practical proxy is \(y_t = (\mathrm{VIX}_t/100)^2\), the squared VIX, used in place of \(v_t\) in the discretised CIR regression without an extra intercept:

              \[\frac{y_{t+\Delta t} - y_t}{\sqrt{y_t}} = \kappa\theta\,\frac{\Delta t}{\sqrt{y_t}} - \kappa\Delta t\,\sqrt{y_t} + \xi\sqrt{\Delta t}\,\varepsilon_t.\]

              Dividing by \(\sqrt{y_t}\) makes the error homoskedastic, so OLS on the two regressors gives \(\kappa\theta\) and \(\kappa\), and the residual standard deviation gives \(\xi\). The correlation \(\rho\) is estimated from daily S&P 500 returns and changes in the proxy.

              For the S&P 500 and the VIX over 1990–2026 the estimates are \(\hat\kappa = 4.13\), a half-life of 42 days, \(\hat\xi = 0.56\) and \(\hat\rho = -0.69\). For the simulated paths, \(\theta\) is therefore set to the realised variance of the S&P 500 rather than to the VIX level.

                Background: Fractional Brownian Motion and the Hurst Exponent (deeper) (1/2)

                Slide 56 in the PDF

                Can one process describe volatility that is both irregular at small scales and persistent at large scales, as the slow decay of the VIX ACF in Further Topics suggests? The standard tool for irregularity is fractional Brownian motion \(B^H\), \(0 < H < 1\), a Gaussian process with \(B^H_0 = 0\) and

                \[\operatorname{Cov}(B^H_s, B^H_t) = \tfrac12\big(s^{2H} + t^{2H} - |t - s|^{2H}\big).\]
                (11.25)

                For \(H = 1/2\) the covariance is \(\min(s, t)\), the covariance of Brownian motion. In general the increments have variance \(\mathbb{E}[(B^H_{t+h} - B^H_t)^2] = h^{2H}\). For \(H < 1/2\) successive increments are negatively correlated and the paths are rougher than Brownian motion; for \(H > 1/2\) they are positively correlated and the paths are smoother.

                Background: Fractional Brownian Motion and the Hurst Exponent (deeper) (2/2)

                Slide 57 in the PDF

                The fractional OU process is the OU equation (11.12) driven by \(B^H\) instead of \(W\). A single exponent \(H\) then fixes both ends of its ACF. At short lags the variogram behaves like \(h^{2H} = h^{2\alpha + 1}\), where \(\alpha = H - 1/2\) is the roughness index; \(\alpha < 0\) means rough paths. At long lags the ACF decays like \(h^{2H - 2} = h^{-\beta}\), so \(\beta = 2 - 2H\); \(\beta < 1\) gives a non-integrable ACF, that is, long memory.

                The two features are tied together: roughness (\(H < 1/2\)) implies \(\beta > 1\), short memory, and long memory (\(\beta < 1\)) implies \(H > 1/2\), smooth paths.

                    Second Case Study (3/6): The Paper's Statistic and the Course Adaptation

                    Slide 64 in the PDF

                    The paper's statistic (its Eq. 11–14) compares a local estimate of the drift with a local estimate of volatility, both computed with the one-sided kernel \(K(x) = e^{-|x|}\) for \(x \le 0\), which uses only the past:

                    \[\begin{gathered} \hat\mu_t = \frac{1}{h}\sum_i K\Big(\frac{t_{i-1} - t}{h}\Big)\Delta_i X, \\ \hat\sigma_t^2 = \frac{1}{h'}\sum_i K\Big(\frac{t_{i-1} - t}{h'}\Big)(\Delta_i X)^2, \\ T_t = \sqrt{\frac{h}{K_2}}\;\frac{\hat\mu_t}{\hat\sigma_t}, \end{gathered}\]
                    (11.27)

                    with \(K_2 = \int K^2 = 1/2\). Without a drift burst \(T_t\) is asymptotically standard Normal; with one, \(|T_t| \to \infty\). The paper uses \(h\) = 5 minutes and \(h'\) = 25 minutes, with pre-averaging and a HAC correction for microstructure noise, counts drift bursts at fixed thresholds between 4 and 6, and estimates the false alarms by simulation.

                    The course adapts the statistic to five-minute bars, with the sums starting at the open. The local variance is \(\widehat s_j^2 = \sum_i u_{ij} r_i^2 / (\Delta\sum_i u_{ij})\) and the statistic \(T_j = \sum_i w_{ij} r_i \big/ \sqrt{\widehat s_j^2\,\Delta\sum_i w_{ij}^2}\), with weights \(w_{ij} = e^{-(t_j - t_{i-1})/h}\) and \(u_{ij} = e^{-(t_j - t_{i-1})/h'}\). The denominator is random and computed from the same returns, so \(T_j\) is not exactly Normal, and with \(h/\Delta = 3\) bars the asymptotic approximation is not guaranteed. Critical values are therefore calibrated by simulation.

                    Weak Convergence

                    Slide 86 in the PDF

                    The weak error, \(|\mathbb{E}[g(X_T^{\Delta t})] - \mathbb{E}[g(X_T)]| \le C\,\Delta t^{\beta}\), measures how close the simulated distribution is to the true one.                     Data Cleaning and Technical Conditions

                    Slide 131 in the PDF

                    Equity indices are used on weekdays with stale quotes removed and genuine zero returns kept; Bitcoin trades seven days a week. The Itô integral is defined for adapted integrands by approximation with simple processes, and is only a local martingale when merely \(\int_0^T H_t^2\,\mathrm{d}t < \infty\) almost surely. An SDE has a unique strong solution if its coefficients are Lipschitz with linear growth (Kloeden & Platen, 1992); square-root coefficients are not Lipschitz at zero but still have unique strong solutions. Donsker's theorem holds in the space of càdlàg paths with the Skorokhod topology (Billingsley, 1999).

                    In brief

                    • Data: equity indices on weekdays, stale quotes removed
                      • stale quote: unchanged close and no intraday range
                      • genuine zero returns are kept
                      • Bitcoin: 7 days a week
                    • Itô integral: left-point sums converge for regular integrands
                      • e.g. continuous and adapted integrands
                      • a general \(H\) is reached by approximation with simple processes
                      • if only \(\int_0^T H_t^2\,\dd t < \infty\) almost surely, \(\int H\,\dd W\) is merely a local martingale
                      • the mean of this local martingale need not be zero
                    • SDE: unique strong solution if \(a\), \(b\) are Lipschitz in \(x\) with at most linear growth Kloeden & Platen (1992)
                      • square-root coefficients (CIR, Heston) are not Lipschitz at zero
                      • they still have unique strong solutions (Yamada–Watanabe conditions)
