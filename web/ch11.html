Central chart: Which Model Reproduces the S&P 500?

Osborne (1959) described the logarithms of stock prices with the tools of statistical physics: not prices but their logarithms behave like Brownian motion. The resulting model, geometric Brownian motion (GBM), is the price model behind the formula of Black & Scholes (1973). Prices are recorded daily or every few milliseconds, and continuous time gives one language for all these scales: Itô calculus, stochastic differential equations (SDEs) and the change of measure. The chapter's question is whether a single model can describe prices at any time scale.

The daily log returns of the S&P 500 since 1990 reject GBM in two respects. The excess kurtosis is 10.9 instead of 0, the worst day lies at −11.3 standard deviations, and 62 days lie beyond ±4 standard deviations, against 0.59 expected under the Normal distribution. The autocorrelation of absolute returns is 0.27 at lag 1 and still 0.14 at lag 50, so volatility comes in clusters (volatility clustering). Quadratic variation grows in steps: between September 2008 and March 2009, 1.6% of the days bring 15.2% of the quadratic variation of the whole sample. The drift cannot be pinned down from the data either: for a precision of ±1 percentage point, the S&P 500 would need 1,243 years of data and Bitcoin 17,028 years.

The chapter adds mean reversion, jumps (the Merton model) and stochastic volatility (the Heston model) in turn, then compares the estimated models with the data on 100 simulated samples of 9,245 days each. Merton reproduces the Hill tail statistic, 2.70 against 2.56 in the data, but not volatility clustering. Heston reproduces volatility clustering, with an autocorrelation of 0.28 at lag 1 against 0.27 in the data. No estimated model reaches the excess kurtosis of the data, 10.88: Merton gives 3.66 and Heston 3.21. The case studies show what one-factor models also miss: on SPY, the noise-robust non-linear least squares (NLLS) estimator gives a roughness exponent of −0.35, against 0 for Brownian motion.

From Random Walk to Brownian Motion

From Random Walk to Brownian Motion: Donsker's Theorem (1/3)

Slide 4 in the PDF
  • Question: which process do we get as price steps become smaller and more frequent?
    • prices move in discrete steps
  • Random walk: \(S_0 = 0\), \(S_k = S_{k-1} + \xi_k = \xi_1 + \cdots + \xi_k\)
    • steps \(\xi_i\) i.i.d. (independent and identically distributed), mean 0 and variance 1
    • it is the log-price model of Chapter 2
    • \(\xi_k\) is the standardised return of day \(k\)
  • Scaled random walk: \(W_n(t) = S_{\lfloor nt \rfloor}/\sqrt{n}\), with \(n\) steps per unit of time
    • \(\lfloor nt \rfloor\): integer part of \(nt\)
    • why \(\sqrt{n}\): \(\Var(W_n(t)) = \lfloor nt \rfloor/n \to t\)
    • divided by \(n\) the limit would be 0
    • undivided the variance would explode
  • Central limit theorem (CLT): \(W_n(t) \to N(0, t)\) for each \(t\) separately
  • Donsker's theorem: \(W_n \Rightarrow W\) for the whole path Billingsley (1999)
    • \(\Rightarrow\): convergence in distribution
    • holds whatever the law of the steps

    Brownian Motion: Definition and Properties

    Slide 7 in the PDF
    • Standard Brownian motion: four defining properties
      • \(W_0 = 0\)
      • independent increments
      • \(W_t - W_s \sim N(0, t - s)\) for \(s < t\)
      • continuous paths
      • existence: Wiener (1923)
      • simulation on a grid: \(W_{t + \Delta t} = W_t + \sqrt{\Delta t}\,Z\), \(Z \sim N(0, 1)\)
    • A Gaussian process with \(\Cov(W_s, W_t) = \min(s, t)\)
    • A martingale: \(\E[W_t \mid \mathcal{F}_s] = W_s\)
      • \(\mathcal{F}_s\): the information up to \(s\)
      • the efficient-market random walk of Chapter 2
    • Self-similarity: \(\{c^{-1/2} W_{ct}\}\) is again a standard Brownian motion for any \(c > 0\)
    • Paths are continuous but nowhere differentiable
      • \((W_{t + h} - W_t)/h\) has standard deviation \(1/\sqrt{h} \to \infty\)
      • ordinary calculus fails

    Quadratic Variation and Itô Calculus

    Total and Quadratic Variation

    Slide 8 in the PDF
    • Question: why does ordinary calculus fail on a Brownian path, and what replaces it?
    • Total variation \(\sum_k |\Delta W_k|\)
      • grid \(0 = t_0 < \cdots < t_n = T\), step \(\Delta t = T/n\), \(\Delta W_k = W_{t_{k}} - W_{t_{k-1}}\)
      • its mean is \(\sqrt{2nT/\pi} \to \infty\)
      • a Brownian path has infinite length
    • Quadratic variation \([W]_T = \lim \sum_k (\Delta W_k)^2 = T\)
      • a random sum with a deterministic limit
      • \(Q_n = \sum_{k=1}^{n} (\Delta W_k)^2\), \(\Var(Q_n) = 2T^2/n\)
      • one year of daily data gives about 9% relative error (\(\sqrt{2/252}\))
      • the idea behind realised variance (Chapter 9)
    • Shorthand of Itô calculus: \((\dd W_t)^2 = \dd t\)

      The Itô Integral (1/2)

      Slide 11 in the PDF
      • Goal: a meaning for \(\int_0^T H_t\,\dd W_t\)
        • the gain of holding \(H_t\) units of an asset priced \(W\)
        • Riemann–Stieltjes integration fails because the total variation is infinite
      • Itô integral: the limit in mean square of sums with the integrand at the left end point
        • \(\int_0^T H_t\,\dd W_t = \lim_{n \to \infty} \sum_{k=1}^{n} H_{t_{k-1}}\,(W_{t_k} - W_{t_{k-1}})\)
        • for \(H\) adapted with \(\E\left[\int_0^T H_t^2\,\dd t\right] < \infty\)
        • \(H_t\) uses only information up to \(t\)
        • the position is chosen before the next price move

      The Itô Integral (2/2)

      Slide 12 in the PDF
      • Zero mean: \(\E\left[\int_0^T H_t\,\dd W_t\right] = 0\)
      • Itô isometry: \(\E\left[\left(\int_0^T H_t\,\dd W_t\right)^2\right] = \E\left[\int_0^T H_t^2\,\dd t\right]\)
      • \(t \mapsto \int_0^t H_s\,\dd W_s\) is a martingale
        • no strategy earns money on average
        • weaker conditions: Appendix
      • Stratonovich integral: the integrand averaged over the end points, \(\tfrac12(H_{t_{k-1}} + H_{t_k})\)
        • it gives a different number
        • finance uses Itô, because that position would use the price at \(t_k\)

      Itô Processes and Itô's Lemma

      Slide 13 in the PDF
      • Itô process: \(\dd X_t = a_t\,\dd t + b_t\,\dd W_t\)
        • drift \(a_t\), diffusion coefficient \(b_t\), both adapted
        • shorthand for \(X_t = X_0 + \int_0^t a_s\,\dd s + \int_0^t b_s\,\dd W_s\)
      • Itô's lemma: for \(f(t, x)\) continuously differentiable once in \(t\) and twice in \(x\) Itô (1944)
        • \(\dd f(t, X_t) = \left(f_t + a_t f_x + \tfrac{1}{2} b_t^2 f_{xx}\right)\dd t + b_t f_x\,\dd W_t\)
        • partial derivatives evaluated at \((t, X_t)\)
        • \(f_t = \partial f/\partial t\), \(f_x = \partial f/\partial x\), \(f_{xx} = \partial^2 f/\partial x^2\)
      • Rule of thumb: Taylor expansion to second order
        • with \(\dd t \cdot \dd t = 0\), \(\dd t \cdot \dd W = 0\), \(\dd W \cdot \dd W = \dd t\)
        • the second-order term \(\tfrac{1}{2} b_t^2 f_{xx}\,\dd t\) does not vanish
        • this is the whole novelty

      Stochastic Differential Equations and Simulation

      Stochastic Differential Equations and the Euler–Maruyama Scheme

      Slide 14 in the PDF
      • Question: how do we simulate a model with no elementary, easy-to-simulate transition (Heston)?
        • exact simulation exists, but is costly: Broadie & Kaya (2006)
      • Question: how large is the discretisation error?
      • A stochastic differential equation (SDE): \(\dd X_t = a(t, X_t)\,\dd t + b(t, X_t)\,\dd W_t\), \(X_0\) given
        • drift and diffusion depend on the current state
        • GBM, Ornstein–Uhlenbeck and Merton have exact transitions
        • we simulate them with no discretisation error
        • Heston has only a closed-form characteristic function
        • Existence conditions: Appendix
      • Euler–Maruyama: freeze \(a\) and \(b\) over each step Higham (2001)
        • \(X_{k+1} = X_k + a(X_k)\,\Delta t + b(X_k)\,\Delta W_k\), \(\Delta W_k = \sqrt{\Delta t}\,Z_k\)
        • \(X_k\) is the approximation at \(t_k = k\Delta t\)
        • \(Z_k\) i.i.d. \(N(0, 1)\)

      The Milstein Scheme and Strong Convergence

      Slide 15 in the PDF
      • Milstein: add the next Itô–Taylor term Milstein (1975)
        • \(X_{k+1} = X_k + a(X_k)\,\Delta t + b(X_k)\,\Delta W_k + \tfrac{1}{2} b(X_k)\,b'(X_k)\left[(\Delta W_k)^2 - \Delta t\right]\)
        • \(b' = \dd b/\dd x\)
        • the correction has conditional mean zero
        • it raises the strong order (path accuracy), not the weak order
      • Strong error: \(\E|X_T - X_T^{\Delta t}| \le C\,\Delta t^{\gamma}\)
        • how close each simulated path is to the true one
        • \(X_T\) is the exact solution, \(X_T^{\Delta t}\) the scheme with step \(\Delta t\)
        • \(C\) is a constant and \(\gamma\) the strong order
        • \(\gamma = 1/2\) for Euler–Maruyama and \(\gamma = 1\) for Milstein
        • it matters for path-dependent quantities (hedging errors, barrier crossings)

      Geometric Brownian Motion

      Geometric Brownian Motion (GBM) from Itô's Lemma (1/2)

      Slide 16 in the PDF
      • Question: which price model stays positive?
        • it should give percentage returns with the same distribution at every price level
      • SDE: \(\dd S_t = \mu S_t\,\dd t + \sigma S_t\,\dd W_t\)
        • the percentage change has constant drift \(\mu\) and volatility \(\sigma\) per year
        • proposed by Osborne (1959) and Samuelson (1965)
        • the price model behind Black & Scholes (1973)
      • Itô's lemma for \(f(x) = \ln x\)
        • \(f_x = 1/x\), \(f_{xx} = -1/x^2\), \(a_t = \mu S_t\), \(b_t = \sigma S_t\)
        • \(\dd \ln S_t = \left(\mu - \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2\right)\dd t + \sigma\,\dd W_t\)
        • so \(S_t = S_0 \exp\left(\left(\mu - \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2\right)t + \sigma W_t\right)\)
        • prices stay positive
        • exact simulation needs only Normal draws

      Geometric Brownian Motion (GBM) from Itô's Lemma (2/2)

      Slide 17 in the PDF
      • The log price grows at the log drift \(m = \mu - \sigma^2/2\), not at \(\mu\)
        • volatility lowers the growth of the median investor's wealth
        • With \(\sigma = 18\%\), the cost is \(\sigma^2/2 = 1.6\) percentage points a year
        • With \(\sigma = 67\%\) (Bitcoin), the cost reaches 22 percentage points a year
      • Testable implications: \(r_t = \ln(S_t/S_{t - \Delta t})\) i.i.d. \(N(m\Delta t, \sigma^2\Delta t)\) at every frequency
        • Skewness and excess kurtosis are zero
        • There is no autocorrelation in \(r_t\), \(|r_t|\) or \(r_t^2\)
        • The variance is proportional to the horizon

      Estimating GBM by Maximum Likelihood (1/2)

      Slide 18 in the PDF
      • Maximum likelihood estimation (MLE) from \(n\) log returns at step \(\Delta t\), over \(T = n\Delta t\) years
        • \(\hat\sigma^2 = \frac{1}{n\Delta t}\sum_t (r_t - \bar r)^2\), \(\hat m = \bar r / \Delta t\), \(\hat\mu = \hat m + \hat\sigma^2/2\) (\(\bar r\): mean log return)
        • under GBM, the standard error (SE) \(\mathrm{SE}(\hat\sigma) = \sigma/\sqrt{2n}\) shrinks with the number of observations
        • \(\mathrm{SE}(\hat m) = \sigma/\sqrt{T}\) depends only on the calendar span
        • reason: \(\sum_t r_t = \ln(S_T/S_0)\) uses only the first and the last price

          Mean Reversion: the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck Process

          The Ornstein–Uhlenbeck (OU) Process

          Slide 24 in the PDF
          • Question: how do we model a variable that returns to a normal level?
            • examples: an interest rate or the VIX
            • unlike a stock price, it does not drift away
          • SDE: \(\dd x_t = \kappa(\theta - x_t)\,\dd t + \sigma\,\dd W_t\), \(\kappa > 0\) Uhlenbeck & Ornstein (1930)
            • \(\theta\): long-run mean; \(\kappa\): speed of mean reversion; \(\sigma\): volatility
            • Above \(\theta\) the drift is negative, below \(\theta\) positive
            • the drift is a force pulling \(x\) back to \(\theta\)
            • Used for interest rates Vasicek (1977), volatility, spreads and commodity prices
          • Half-life \(\ln 2/\kappa\): the time for the expected deviation from \(\theta\) to halve
            • With \(\kappa = 0.5\) per year, the half-life is 1.39 years
          Appendix: solving the OU equation

          Exact Discretisation: an AR(1)

          Slide 25 in the PDF
          • Sampled at step \(\Delta t\), the OU process is exactly an autoregressive model of order 1 (AR(1))
            • \(x_{t+\Delta t} = a + b\,x_t + \varepsilon_t\), \(b = e^{-\kappa\Delta t}\), \(a = \theta(1 - b)\), \(\Var(\varepsilon_t) = \frac{\sigma^2}{2\kappa}(1 - b^2)\)
          • Maximum likelihood = ordinary least squares (OLS) of \(x_{t + \Delta t}\) on \(x_t\)
            • OLS gives the intercept \(\hat a\), the slope \(\hat b\) and the residual standard deviation \(\hat s_\varepsilon\)
            • then invert the mapping: \(\hat\kappa = -\ln\hat b/\Delta t\), \(\hat\theta = \hat a/(1 - \hat b)\), \(\hat\sigma = \hat s_\varepsilon\sqrt{2\hat\kappa/(1 - \hat b^2)}\)
          • Standard errors of \(\hat\kappa\) and \(\hat\theta\): the delta method, from the OLS covariance of \((\hat a, \hat b)\)
            • \(\hat\kappa\) depends on \(\hat b\) only: \(\partial\kappa/\partial b = -1/(b\Delta t)\)
            • so \(\Var(\hat b)\) suffices (Seminar 11, A1)
          • With daily data \(b\) is very close to 1
            • \(\kappa\) is estimated imprecisely, as in unit-root tests

            Why Prices Use a Different Probability: a One-Period Example (1/2)

            Slide 31 in the PDF

            Girsanov's Theorem (1/2)

            Slide 33 in the PDF
            • Real-world measure \(P\) and an equivalent measure \(Q\), linked by the Radon–Nikodym density
              • equivalent: the same events of probability zero
              • \(\left.\frac{\dd Q}{\dd P}\right|_{\mathcal{F}_T} = Z_T = \exp\left(-\int_0^T \theta_s\,\dd W_s - \tfrac{1}{2}\int_0^T \theta_s^2\,\dd s\right)\)
              • \(\theta_s\) is adapted (below: the market price of risk)
              • \(Z_T > 0\) with \(\E^P[Z_T] = 1\) reweights the probability of each path
              • \(Q(A) = \E^P[Z_T\mathbf{1}_A]\)
            • Girsanov (1960): under \(Q\), \(\tilde W_t = W_t + \int_0^t \theta_s\,\dd s\) is a Brownian motion
              • condition: \(\E\left[e^{\frac{1}{2}\int_0^T \theta_s^2 \dd s}\right] < \infty\) (Novikov condition)

            Girsanov's Theorem (2/2)

            Slide 34 in the PDF
            • For \(\dd X = a\,\dd t + b\,\dd W\): \(\dd X = (a - b\,\theta)\,\dd t + b\,\dd\tilde W\)

            • With jumps (Section 9), \(Q\) may also change the jump intensity and the jump-size distribution
              • \(\hat\lambda\) from returns is not the intensity that prices options
            • With stochastic volatility, \(P\) and \(Q\) weigh future variance paths differently
              • historical variance is a benchmark, not a calibration

            Feynman–Kac: From Expectations to the Pricing PDE (1/2)

            Slide 37 in the PDF
            • If \(\dd X = a(X)\,\dd t + b(X)\,\dd\tilde W\) under \(Q\) Kac (1949)

            Merton's Jump-Diffusion (1/2)

            Slide 40 in the PDF
            • Question: which model explains crash days that are practically impossible under GBM?
            • Brownian paths are continuous, but prices gap on news
              • on 19 October 1987 the Dow Jones fell 22.6%
              • about 20 standard deviations under GBM with 18% volatility
              • A jump cannot be hedged by continuous trading Merton (1976)
            • Log price = Brownian motion with drift + compound Poisson jumps
              • \(\dd \ln S_t = m\,\dd t + \sigma\,\dd W_t + \dd J_t\), \(J_t = \sum_{i \le N_t} Y_i\)

              • the model has five parameters, \((m, \sigma, \lambda, \mu_J, \sigma_J)\)

            Merton's Jump-Diffusion (2/2)

            Slide 41 in the PDF
            • Given \(k\) jumps, the return over \(\Delta t\) is \(N\left(m\Delta t + k\mu_J,\ \sigma^2\Delta t + k\sigma_J^2\right)\)
            • Unconditionally, it is a Poisson mixture of Normal densities, i.i.d. over time
              • \(f(r) = \sum_{k \ge 0} e^{-\lambda\Delta t}\frac{(\lambda\Delta t)^k}{k!}\,\varphi\left(r;\ m\Delta t + k\mu_J,\ \sigma^2\Delta t + k\sigma_J^2\right)\)
              • Weight: Poisson probability of \(k\) jumps
              • \(\varphi(r; \text{mean}, \text{variance})\): the Normal density
            • Exact simulation: draw \(N \sim \mathrm{Poisson}(\lambda\Delta t)\)
              • then draw from the Normal distribution with the conditional moments

              Slide 47 in the PDF

              The Heston Model (2/2)

                Case Studies: Rough and Persistent Volatility, Drift Bursts

                Background: Fractional Brownian Motion and the Hurst Exponent (deeper) (1/2)

                Slide 56 in the PDF
                • Question: can one process describe volatility that is both irregular and persistent?
                  • irregular at small scales, persistent at large scales (the VIX ACF, Section 6)
                • Fractional Brownian motion \(B^H\), \(0 < H < 1\): a Gaussian process with \(B^H_0 = 0\)
                  • \(\Cov(B^H_s, B^H_t) = \tfrac12\left(s^{2H} + t^{2H} - |t - s|^{2H}\right)\)
                  • \(H = 1/2\) gives \(\min(s, t)\), i.e. Brownian motion
                  • increments have variance \(\E[(B^H_{t+h} - B^H_t)^2] = h^{2H}\)
                  • \(H < 1/2\): successive increments are negatively correlated, paths rougher than Brownian motion
                  • \(H > 1/2\): positively correlated, smoother

                Background: Fractional Brownian Motion and the Hurst Exponent (deeper) (2/2)

                Slide 57 in the PDF
                • Fractional OU: the OU equation driven by \(B^H\) instead of \(W\)
                  • one \(H\) sets both ends of its ACF
                • Short lags: the variogram behaves like \(h^{2H} = h^{2\alpha + 1}\)
                  • the roughness index is \(\alpha = H - 1/2\)
                  • \(\alpha < 0\): rough
                • Long lags: the ACF decays like \(h^{2H - 2} = h^{-\beta}\)
                  • so \(\beta = 2 - 2H\)
                  • \(\beta < 1\): non-integrable ACF, long memory

                    Second Case Study (3/6): The Paper's Statistic and the Course Adaptation

                    Slide 64 in the PDF
                    • The paper's statistic (Eq. 11–14), with the kernel \(K(x) = e^{-|x|}\) for \(x \le 0\) (past only)

                      • \(\widehat s_j^2 = \dfrac{\sum_i u_{ij} r_i^2}{\Delta\sum_i u_{ij}}\) and \(T_j = \dfrac{\sum_i w_{ij} r_i}{\sqrt{\widehat s_j^2\,\Delta\sum_i w_{ij}^2}}\)

                    Quadratic Covariation and the Multivariate Itô Formula (1/2)

                    Slide 84 in the PDF
                    • Quadratic covariation \([X, Y]_t = \lim \sum_k \Delta X_k\,\Delta Y_k\)
                      • if \(\Corr(\dd W^{(1)}, \dd W^{(2)}) = \rho\), then \(\dd[W^{(1)}, W^{(2)}]_t = \rho\,\dd t\)

                    Why a Negative \(\rho\) Skews Daily Returns

                    Slide 123 in the PDF
                    • Over one day \(h = 1/252\): \(R = \int_0^h \sqrt{v_s}\,\dd W_s^{(1)}\)
                      • drift terms of order \(h\) are ignored
                      • Itô's lemma on \(\sqrt{v}\): \(\dd\sqrt{v_s} = \tfrac12\xi\,\dd W_s^{(2)} + O(\dd s)\)
                      • so \(\sqrt{v_s} \approx \sqrt{v_0} + \tfrac12\xi W_s^{(2)}\)
                      • \(R \approx \sqrt{v_0}\,W_h^{(1)} + \tfrac12\xi\int_0^h W_s^{(2)}\,\dd W_s^{(1)}\)
                    • Third moment: the cross term gives \(\E[R^3] \approx \tfrac32\,\xi\rho\,v_0 h^2\)
                      • while \(\Var(R) \approx v_0 h\)
                      • skewness \(\approx \tfrac32\,\rho\,\xi\sqrt{h}/\sqrt{v_0}\), a leading-order approximation
                      • with \(\hat\rho = -0.69\), \(\hat\xi = 0.56\) and \(\sqrt{v_0} = 18.0\%\): about \(-0.20\)
                      • the simulation with 390 steps per day (Section 11): median \(-0.21\)
