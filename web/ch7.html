The chapter starts from the question of how large tomorrow's tail losses are and how precisely we can estimate them. Value-at-Risk (VaR) gives the loss threshold exceeded with a small probability, Expected Shortfall (ES) the mean loss beyond that threshold. On S&P 500 daily losses over 2000–2026, the historical VaR 1% is 3.45%: the loss exceeded it on 68 of 6,717 days. ES 1%, the average loss over the worst 1% of days, is 5.03%. The largest loss, 12.77% on 16 March 2020, is about ten standard deviations; the Normal distribution fits the centre but is far too thin in the tail.

Artzner, Delbaen, Eber & Heath (1999) stated four properties that a risk measure should have and called the measures satisfying them coherent. VaR violates one of them, subadditivity. Two independent bonds, each losing 100 with probability 0.9%, each have a VaR 1% of 0, while their portfolio has a VaR 1% of 100. A VaR limit would therefore reward concentration. ES 1% is 90 for each bond and 100.81 for the portfolio, below the sum of 180. Precision is the second problem: over the last 500 days, ES 2.5% rests on only 12.5 losses, and its HAC (heteroskedasticity and autocorrelation consistent) interval is 2.55 percentage points wide.

Tomorrow's risk is a conditional question. Over 2006–2026, the share of days with a loss above the one-day VaR 1%, against a target of 1%, is 1.67% for 500-day historical simulation, 1.54% for filtered historical simulation (FHS) and 2.34% for GARCH with Normal shocks. The historical window reacts months late, and its exceedances cluster in 2008 and 2020; the filtered VaR peaked at 18.01% on 18 March 2020. Even FHS has 80 exceedances in 5,210 days against 52.1 expected, and the Kupiec test rejects its coverage. The chapter goes on to extreme value theory, the 10-day horizon, the risk contributions of a portfolio and the Basel rules.

Losses and the Sign Convention

Slide 5 in the PDF
  • Return over one day: \(X_{t+1} = r_{t+1}\), the log return in %
  • Loss: \(L_{t+1} = -X_{t+1}\), a log loss
    • \(L > 0\) means the position lost money
  • In money, with \(V_t\) the value of the position: loss \(= V_t\,(1 - e^{-L_{t+1}/100}) \approx V_t L_{t+1}/100\) for small \(L\)
  • Why log: log returns add over days, simple returns add across assets
    • over days: the horizon \(h\); across assets: the portfolio
  • The map \(L \mapsto 1 - e^{-L/100}\) is increasing
    • so VaR in money is \(V_t(1 - e^{-\VaR_\alpha/100})\)
    • ES in money is computed scenario by scenario, then averaged
  • Sign convention: VaR and ES are positive numbers when they are losses

Horizon and Level: Why 1 Day, 10 Days, 1% and 2.5%

Slide 6 in the PDF
  • Horizon \(h\): the number of days over which the loss is measured
  • \(h = 1\) day: the horizon of trading limits and of backtesting, with a new observation every day
  • \(h = 10\) days: the time Basel assumes for liquidating a position Basel Committee (1996)
  • Level \(\alpha\): the tail probability
    • we write “VaR 1%”, “ES 2.5%”
  • \(\alpha = 1\%\): the VaR level of the 1996 Basel amendment Basel Committee (1996), one exceedance every 100 days on average
  • \(\alpha = 2.5\%\): the ES level of FRTB Basel Committee (2019)
    • chosen because under the Normal distribution ES 2.5% is close to VaR 1%

Value-at-Risk and Expected Shortfall

Definitions (1/2): Value-at-Risk

Slide 7 in the PDF
  • The question of this section: which loss is exceeded on only a fraction \(\alpha\) of days?
    • and how much do we lose, on average, beyond it?
  • VaR at level \(\alpha\): the loss exceeded with probability \(\alpha\) (exactly for continuous \(L\), at most \(\alpha\) in general)
    • \(\VaR_\alpha(X) = -\inf\{x \in \mathbb{R}: P(X \le x) > \alpha\} = -q_\alpha(X)\)
    • \(X\): the return of the position over the horizon; \(q_\alpha(X)\): its \(\alpha\)-quantile, negative for small \(\alpha\), so VaR is a positive loss
    • For a continuous loss \(L = -X\): \(\VaR_\alpha = q_{1-\alpha}(L)\), the upper quantile of the loss
  • Reading of VaR 1%
    • “With probability \(1\%\), tomorrow's loss will exceed \(\VaR_{1\%}\)”
    • VaR does not say how large the loss is beyond this threshold

Definitions (2/2): Expected Shortfall

Slide 8 in the PDF
  • Expected Shortfall: the average of all quantiles in the tail of probability \(\alpha\)
    • \(\ES_\alpha(X) = -\dfrac{1}{\alpha}\displaystyle\int_0^\alpha q_u(X)\,du = \dfrac{1}{\alpha}\displaystyle\int_0^\alpha \VaR_u(X)\,du\)
    • \(u\): the integration variable, a tail probability between 0 and \(\alpha\)
  • For a continuous distribution: the mean loss on the days beyond VaR
    • \(\ES_\alpha(X) = -\E[X \mid X \le q_\alpha(X)]\)
    • Always \(\ES_\alpha \ge \VaR_\alpha\): ES looks into the tail, VaR only at its entrance; two portfolios with the same VaR can have very different ES
  • Other names: Conditional VaR (CVaR), Tail VaR, Average VaR Rockafellar & Uryasev (2002), Acerbi & Tasche (2002)

    The Normal Distribution: ES in One Line (1/2)

    Slide 11 in the PDF
    • Setting: \(X \sim N(\mu, \sigma^2)\), a Normal return with mean \(\mu\) and standard deviation \(\sigma\)
      • \(\Phi\), \(\varphi\): the distribution function and the density of the standard Normal distribution \(N(0, 1)\)
      • \(z_\alpha = \Phi^{-1}(\alpha) < 0\): the standard Normal \(\alpha\)-quantile, e.g. \(z_{1\%} = -2.326\)
    • VaR: the mean minus \(|z_\alpha|\) standard deviations, sign changed
      • \(\VaR_\alpha = -(\mu + \sigma z_\alpha)\)
    • ES: the same form, with the larger factor \(\varphi(z_\alpha)/\alpha\) in place of \(-z_\alpha\)
      • \(\ES_\alpha = -\mu - \dfrac{\sigma}{\alpha}\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{z_\alpha} u\varphi(u)\,du = -\mu + \sigma\,\dfrac{\varphi(z_\alpha)}{\alpha}\)
      • The integral equals \(-\varphi(z_\alpha)\) because \(\varphi'(u) = -u\varphi(u)\)

    The Normal Distribution: ES in One Line (2/2)

    Slide 12 in the PDF
    • Constants to remember
      • \(-z_{1\%} = 2.326\), \(\varphi(z_{1\%})/0.01 = 2.665\)
      • \(-z_{2.5\%} = 1.960\), \(\varphi(z_{2.5\%})/0.025 = 2.338\)
      • So, under the Normal distribution, ES 2.5% is almost equal to VaR 1% (\(2.338\) against \(2.326\) standard deviations)
    • S&P 500 (\(\hat\mu = 0.025\%\), \(\hat\sigma = 1.213\%\)): Normal \(\VaR_{1\%} = 2.80\%\) vs historical \(3.45\%\)
      • Arithmetic: \(\VaR_{1\%} = -\hat\mu + \hat\sigma\,(-z_{1\%}) = -0.025 + 1.213 \times 2.326 \approx 2.80\%\)
      • The historical VaR is 1.24 times the Normal VaR
    • Under the Normal distribution, for a given \(\mu\), VaR and ES carry no information beyond \(\sigma\)

    The Student-\(t\) Distribution: VaR and ES

    Slide 13 in the PDF
    • Model: the return is a shifted and rescaled Student-\(t\) variable, \(X = m + s\,T\) with \(T \sim t_\nu\)
      • \(m\): location; \(s > 0\): scale; \(\nu\): degrees of freedom, smaller \(\nu\) = heavier tails
      • \(t_\alpha = t_\nu^{-1}(\alpha) < 0\): the \(\alpha\)-quantile of \(t_\nu\); \(g_\nu\): the density of \(t_\nu\)
    • VaR as for the Normal distribution, with \(t_\alpha\) in place of \(z_\alpha\)
      • ES gets an extra factor \((\nu + t_\alpha^2)/(\nu - 1) > 1\) for the heavy tail
      • \(\VaR_\alpha = -(m + s\,t_\alpha), \qquad \ES_\alpha = -m + s\,\dfrac{g_\nu(t_\alpha)}{\alpha}\,\dfrac{\nu + t_\alpha^2}{\nu - 1}\) (for \(\nu > 1\))
      • Variance \(s^2\nu/(\nu - 2)\) exists only for \(\nu > 2\), so \(s\) is not the standard deviation

    Coherent Risk Measures

    Coherent Risk Measures (1/2): the Four Axioms

    Slide 14 in the PDF
    • The question: which properties must a number used as capital have?
      • so that diversification is never penalised
    • A risk measure \(\rho\) maps a loss \(L\) (a random variable) to the capital to hold
      • it is coherent if it satisfies four axioms Artzner, Delbaen, Eber & Heath (1999)
      • \(L_1, L_2\): two losses; \(c\): a sure amount (constant); \(\lambda \ge 0\): a scaling factor (position size)
    • Monotone: \(L_1 \le L_2 \Rightarrow \rho(L_1) \le \rho(L_2)\)
      • A position that always loses more needs more capital
    • Translation invariant: \(\rho(L + c) = \rho(L) + c\)
      • Adding a sure loss \(c\) raises the capital by exactly \(c\)
    • Positively homogeneous: \(\rho(\lambda L) = \lambda\rho(L)\)
      • Doubling the position doubles the capital
    • Subadditive: \(\rho(L_1 + L_2) \le \rho(L_1) + \rho(L_2)\)
      • Merging two positions never needs more capital than holding them apart: diversification does not add risk

    Coherent Risk Measures (2/2): Why the Axioms Matter

    Slide 15 in the PDF
    • Limits
      • By subadditivity, bank-wide risk does not exceed the sum of the desk risk limits, \(\sum_i \rho_i\)
      • No regulatory arbitrage by splitting a firm
    • Optimisation: homogeneity + subadditivity \(\Rightarrow\) convexity in the portfolio weights
      • Every local minimum is global; uniqueness would need strict convexity, which scenario-based ES lacks (it is piecewise linear)
    • VaR satisfies the first three axioms, but not subadditivity in general
      • Exception: jointly elliptical losses (multivariate Normal or Student-\(t\)) and \(\alpha < 50\%\), where VaR and ES are both \(\mu_p + \sigma_p\times\) constant McNeil, Frey & Embrechts (2015)
      • \(\mu_p\), \(\sigma_p\): mean and standard deviation of the portfolio loss (heavy-tailed refinements: Appendix)

    Counterexample: Two Bonds, Step by Step

    Slide 16 in the PDF
    • Bonds A and B, independent: each loses 100 with probability \(p = 0.9\%\) (default)
      • and 0 otherwise
      • \(X\): the value change, \(X = -L\)
    • General formula for a discrete loss \(L = -X\): \(\ES_\alpha = \frac{1}{\alpha}\left(\E[L\,\mathbf{1}\{L > \VaR_\alpha\}] + \VaR_\alpha\,(\alpha - P(L > \VaR_\alpha))\right)\)
      • \(\mathbf{1}\{\cdot\}\): indicator, 1 if the condition holds and 0 otherwise; first term: losses strictly beyond VaR; second term: the rest of the tail probability \(\alpha\), placed at VaR itself
    • One bond: \(P(X \le -100) = p = 0.9\%\), not above \(1\%\), so \(q_{1\%} = 0\) and \(\VaR_{1\%}(A) = \VaR_{1\%}(B) = 0\)
      • \(\ES_{1\%}(A) = (100 \cdot 0.009 + 0 \cdot 0.001)/0.01 = 90\)
    • Portfolio A + B: \(P(X \le -100) = 1 - (1-p)^2 = 1.79\% > 1\%\)
      • \(P(X \le -200) = p^2 = 0.0081\%\), so \(q_{1\%} = -100\)
      • \(\VaR_{1\%}(A + B) = 100 > 0 + 0 = \VaR_{1\%}(A) + \VaR_{1\%}(B)\)
    • \(\ES_{1\%}(A + B) = 100.81 \le 180 = \ES_{1\%}(A) + \ES_{1\%}(B)\)

    Subadditivity: VaR Fails, ES Holds

    Slide 17 in the PDF
      Question for the room Keep the two bonds independent, with a loss of 100 on each default. If each bond’s default probability rises from 0.9% to 1.1%, what is the portfolio VaR 1%?
      Answer

      100. The probability that at least one defaults becomes 2.19%, above 1%, but the probability of two defaults is only 0.012%, below 1%; the portfolio VaR 1% stays at 100. Each individual VaR becomes 100, so their sum is 200 ≥ 100 and subadditivity holds: 200 is the sum of the individual VaRs, not the portfolio VaR, and 0 was the individual VaR at 0.9%. At 0.9% (the slide), the probability of at least one default is 1.79%, and the portfolio VaR, 100, exceeds the sum 0 + 0. In this example, VaR violates subadditivity only when the default probability lies between about 0.5% and 1%. ES is subadditive: at 0.9%, 100.8 for the portfolio against 90 + 90 = 180.

      • Blue: sum of the two stand-alone measures
        • red: the measure of the combined portfolio
      • VaR jumps from \(0 + 0\) to 100 when the bonds are combined (the counterexample of the previous slide)
      • A VaR limit would reward concentration: holding only A looks riskless
        • ES sees the size of the tail and is coherent Acerbi & Tasche (2002)
      • Credit portfolios, options and other skewed pay-offs are where VaR fails in practice

      Coherence versus Robustness

      Slide 19 in the PDF
      • Qualitative robustness Cont, Deguest & Scandolo (2010)
        • a small change in the data distribution changes the law of the estimator only a little
        • Historical VaR is qualitatively robust at distributions with a unique \(\alpha\)-quantile; historical ES is not: one extreme loss moves it without bound (the precision section)
      • S&P 500, last 500 days: drop the single largest loss (\(6.16\%\), 2025-04-04)
        • \(\ES_{2.5\%}\): \(2.98\% \to 2.64\%\); \(\VaR_{1\%}\): \(2.73\% \to 2.68\%\)
      • A trade-off, not a free lunch
        • subadditivity needs the tail beyond the quantile
        • this is exactly what makes ES sensitive to single observations
      • FRTB chose coherence for capital (ES 2.5%) and kept VaR for backtesting
        • VaR is robust
        • VaR forecasts can be compared directly through a score (elicitability, next slide)

      Elicitability: Why ES Is Harder to Evaluate (Chapter 8)

      Slide 20 in the PDF
      • A scoring function \(S(v, X)\): a penalty computed from the forecast \(v\) and the outcome \(X\)
        • lower means a better forecast
      • A risk measure is elicitable if it is the unique minimiser of \(\E[S(v, X)]\) for some \(S\)
        • such an \(S\) is a strictly consistent score
        • VaR: the quantile \(q_\alpha(X)\) minimises the pinball loss \(\E[(\mathbf{1}\{X \le v\} - \alpha)(v - X)]\)
        • The pinball loss charges \(1 - \alpha\) per unit when \(X\) falls below \(v\) and \(\alpha\) per unit when it stays above
      • ES alone is not elicitable Gneiting (2011)
        • no strictly consistent scoring function exists for ES on its own
      • The pair (VaR, ES) is jointly elicitable Fissler & Ziegel (2016)
        • joint scores such as FZ0 rank (VaR, ES) forecasts (Appendix; Chapter 8)
        • FZ0: the Fissler–Ziegel score of Patton, Ziegel & Chen (2019)
        • \(S(cv, ce; cY) = S(v, e; Y) + \ln c\): score differences, not the score itself, are homogeneous of degree zero

      Estimation Methods

      Historical Simulation

      Slide 21 in the PDF
      • The question of this section: how do we estimate VaR and ES from data?
        • and how much does the answer depend on the tail assumption?
      • Take the last \(n\) daily losses \(L_{t-n+1}, \dots, L_t\) and sort them: \(L_{(1)} \le \dots \le L_{(n)}\)
        • \(L_{(i)}\): the \(i\)-th smallest loss
        • \(\widehat{\VaR}_\alpha = L_{(j)} + (\ell - j)\,(L_{(j+1)} - L_{(j)})\), with \(\ell = 1 + (n-1)(1-\alpha)\) and \(j = \lfloor \ell \rfloor\): the empirical \((1-\alpha)\)-quantile, interpolated linearly
        • \(\widehat{\ES}_\alpha = \frac{1}{n\alpha}\big[\sum_{i=1}^{m} L_{(n-i+1)} + (n\alpha - m)\,L_{(n-m)}\big]\), with \(m = \lfloor n\alpha \rfloor\): the integral of the empirical quantile function over the worst fraction \(\alpha\) of the losses
        • The \(m\) largest losses get weight 1; the next one, \(L_{(n-m)}\), gets the fractional weight \(n\alpha - m\)
        • Alternative, the mean of the selected exceedances: the simple mean of the \(k_\alpha\) losses \(\ge \widehat{\VaR}_\alpha\); it differs from \(\widehat{\ES}_\alpha\) when \(n\alpha\) is not an integer
      • Choice of window \(n\): 250 days reacts faster, 1000 days is more precise
        • With \(n = 500\): 5 losses lie beyond VaR 1%; ES 2.5% gives weight 1 to the 12 largest losses and weight 0.5 to the 13th (\(n\alpha = 12.5\))
      • No loss beyond the worst one in the window can appear in the estimate
      • Ghost effect: a crash day enters the window, stays for \(n\) days, then drops out and VaR falls abruptly

      Cornish–Fisher: Correcting the Normal Quantile, and Where It Breaks

      Slide 22 in the PDF
      • Adjust the Normal quantile for skewness \(S = \E[(X - \mu)^3]/\sigma^3\) and excess kurtosis \(K = \E[(X - \mu)^4]/\sigma^4 - 3\) Cornish & Fisher (1938)
        • \(\tilde z_\alpha = z_\alpha + \dfrac{(z_\alpha^2 - 1)S}{6} + \dfrac{(z_\alpha^3 - 3z_\alpha)K}{24} - \dfrac{(2z_\alpha^3 - 5z_\alpha)S^2}{36}\), \(\quad\VaR_\alpha = -(\mu + \sigma\tilde z_\alpha)\)
        • \(\tilde z_\alpha\): the corrected quantile; \(S = K = 0\) gives back the Normal VaR
        • For \(\alpha\) below about \(4\%\) (\(|z_\alpha| > \sqrt{3}\)): \(S < 0\) (long left tail of returns) or \(K > 0\) (heavy tails) push \(\tilde z_\alpha\) further below \(z_\alpha\) and raise VaR; the \(S^2\) term pushes the other way. At \(\alpha = 5\%\) the \(K\) term has the opposite sign
      • ES: average of \(-(\mu + \sigma\tilde z_u)\) over \(u \in (0, \alpha)\), numerically
      • An expansion around the Normal distribution: accurate only for small \(S\) and \(K\)
        • for large \(K\) the map \(z \mapsto \tilde z\) is not even monotone (Seminar 7, A6)
      • S&P 500 returns: \(S = -0.35\), \(K = 10.7\): far outside that range
        • the sample \(K\) requires a finite fourth moment, which Chapter 1 questions
        • \(\VaR_{1\%}^{CF} = 6.08\%\) versus historical \(3.45\%\): the kurtosis term overshoots

        How Precise Are VaR and ES?

        Two Usable Standard Errors (1/2): VaR

        Slide 25 in the PDF
        • The question of this section: how much would the estimated VaR and ES change on another sample of the same length?
        • VaR: i.i.d. (independent and identically distributed) losses with density \(f\) continuous and positive at \(\VaR_\alpha\)
          • \(\mathrm{SE}(\widehat{\VaR}_\alpha) \approx \dfrac{1}{f(\VaR_\alpha)}\sqrt{\dfrac{\alpha(1 - \alpha)}{n}}\) (SE: standard error)
          • \(\sqrt{\alpha(1 - \alpha)/n}\): the SE of the share of days beyond VaR; dividing by the density \(f(\VaR_\alpha)\) turns it into an error on the loss scale
          • The tail density is small, so the SE is large
          • \(\sigma = 1.2\%\), \(n = 500\): SE of \(\VaR_{1\%}\) = \(0.20\) percentage points (pp) under the Normal distribution, \(0.43\) pp under a \(t_4\) (Student-\(t\), 4 degrees of freedom) with the same \(\sigma\)

        Two Usable Standard Errors (2/2): ES

        Slide 26 in the PDF
        • ES Chen (2008): influence function \(\mathrm{IF}(x) = \VaR_\alpha + (x - \VaR_\alpha)_+/\alpha - \ES_\alpha\), unbounded in \(x\)
          • \(\mathrm{IF}(x)\): the effect of one extra observation \(x\) on the estimate; it grows without bound with \(x\), so one extreme loss can move ES arbitrarily far
        • The standard error
          • If \(\E[L^2] < \infty\): \(\mathrm{SE}(\widehat{\ES}_\alpha) \approx \dfrac{1}{\alpha}\sqrt{\dfrac{\Var((L - \VaR_\alpha)_+)}{n}}\) ; \(\Var\): the variance; estimating VaR adds no first-order term (Rockafellar–Uryasev)
          • For Pareto-type tails with tail index \(a > 2\) the relative error falls like \(1/\sqrt{n\alpha}\): the tail count governs precision Yamai & Yoshiba (2005), Pele & Mazurencu-Marinescu-Pele (2026)
        • Under volatility clustering
          • Replace the variances by long-run HAC (heteroskedasticity and autocorrelation consistent) variances Newey & West (1987) of \(\mathbf{1}\{L_t > \VaR_\alpha\}\) and \((L_t - \VaR_\alpha)_+\)
          • Or use a block bootstrap (derivations: Appendix)

          Filtered Historical Simulation (1/2): the Model

          Slide 30 in the PDF
          • The question of this section: how do we adapt VaR and ES to today's volatility?
            • rather than to the average of recent years
          • Idea Barone-Adesi, Giannopoulos & Vosper (1999), Hull & White (1998)
            • history gives the shape of the shocks, a GARCH model gives today's scale
            • GARCH: generalised autoregressive conditional heteroskedasticity
            • \(r_t = \mu_t + \sigma_t z_t\), \(\mu_t = c + \phi r_{t-1}\), \(\sigma_t^2 = \omega + \alpha_1 (r_{t-1} - \mu_{t-1})^2 + \beta_1 \sigma_{t-1}^2\) (Chapter 5, Bollerslev (1986); \(\alpha_1\) is a GARCH coefficient, not the level \(\alpha\))
          • Notation
            • \(\mu_t\): the conditional mean, an AR(1) (autoregressive of order 1) in the previous return with constant \(c\) and coefficient \(\phi\)
            • \(\sigma_t\): the conditional volatility; \(z_t\): i.i.d. shock with mean 0 and variance 1, distribution left unspecified
            • \(\omega > 0\): constant; \(\alpha_1 \ge 0\): reaction to yesterday's squared surprise; \(\beta_1 \ge 0\): weight on yesterday's variance; \(\alpha_1 + \beta_1\): persistence; \(\alpha_1 + \beta_1 < 1\) ensures a finite unconditional variance (close to 1: shocks fade slowly)

          Filtered Historical Simulation (2/2): Three Steps

          Slide 31 in the PDF
          • Step 1: estimate the AR(1)-GARCH(1,1) model by quasi-maximum likelihood
            • Normal likelihood, robust to other shock distributions
          • Step 2: standardised residuals \(\hat z_t = (r_t - \hat\mu_t)/\hat\sigma_t\): approximately i.i.d., still heavy-tailed
          • Step 3: rescale by tomorrow's volatility
            • \(\VaR_{\alpha,t+1} = -\hat\mu_{t+1} + \hat\sigma_{t+1}\, \hat\xi_\alpha\), \(\hat\xi_\alpha = -q_\alpha(\hat z)\); \(\quad \ES_{\alpha,t+1} = -\hat\mu_{t+1} - \hat\sigma_{t+1}\, \bar z_\alpha\)
            • \(\hat\mu_{t+1}\), \(\hat\sigma_{t+1}\): the model forecasts for tomorrow; \(\hat\xi_\alpha\): minus the empirical \(\alpha\)-quantile of the residuals
            • \(\bar z_\alpha\): the average of the lowest fraction \(\alpha\) of the residuals \(\hat z_t\), with the fractional weight of historical simulation

            Modelling the Quantile Directly: CAViaR

            Slide 34 in the PDF
            • CAViaR Engle & Manganelli (2004): the quantile follows its own recursion; SAV specification
              • CAViaR: Conditional Autoregressive Value at Risk

              • SAV (symmetric absolute value): \(\beta_1 \in (0, 1)\) is the persistence, \(\beta_2 > 0\) the response to yesterday's absolute return

              • with no assumption on the shock distribution
              • \(\hat\beta = \arg\min_\beta \sum_t (\alpha - \mathbf{1}\{r_t < q_t(\beta)\})(r_t - q_t(\beta))\)

            • CAViaR reacts linearly to \(|r_{t-1}|\), GARCH to \(r_{t-1}^2\)

                Block Maxima and the GEV Distribution (1/2)

                Slide 38 in the PDF
                • Split the sample into blocks of \(n\) days
                  • keep each block maximum \(M_n = \max(L_1, \dots, L_n)\)
                  • Fisher–Tippett–Gnedenko theorem Fisher & Tippett (1928), Gnedenko (1943): if \((M_n - b_n)/a_n\) has a non-degenerate limit for i.i.d. losses, the limit is the generalised extreme value (GEV) distribution
                  • \(a_n > 0\), \(b_n\): normalising constants that rescale and recentre the maximum
                • The GEV distribution function of a block maximum
                  • \(H_\xi(x) = \exp\left\{-\left(1 + \xi\,\frac{x - \mu}{\sigma}\right)^{-1/\xi}\right\}\), \(1 + \xi(x - \mu)/\sigma > 0\) (\(\xi = 0\): \(\exp\{-e^{-(x-\mu)/\sigma}\}\))
                  • \(\mu\): location; \(\sigma > 0\): scale; \(\xi\): shape (the tail type, on the slide GEV (2/2); reused symbols: Appendix)

                Block Maxima and the GEV Distribution (2/2)

                Slide 39 in the PDF
                • The shape \(\xi\) decides the tail
                  • \(\xi > 0\): Fréchet, power-law tail with tail index \(1/\xi\) (financial returns)
                  • \(\xi = 0\): Gumbel domain, the normalised excesses have an exponential limit (e.g. Normal, lognormal)
                  • within the Gumbel domain the lognormal tail is still heavier than any exponential tail
                  • \(\xi < 0\): Weibull, bounded tail
                • Return level for \(k\) blocks: exceeded on average once every \(k\) blocks
                  • \(x_k = H_\xi^{-1}(1 - 1/k) = \mu + \dfrac{\sigma}{\xi}\left[\left(-\ln(1 - 1/k)\right)^{-\xi} - 1\right]\)
                  • 10 years: \(k = 120\) months
                • Block size: one month (about 21 trading days) is a compromise Embrechts, Klüppelberg & Mikosch (1997), Longin (2000)
                  • longer blocks bring the maximum closer to its GEV limit but leave fewer maxima

                Peaks over Threshold and the GPD (1/2)

                Slide 40 in the PDF
                • Fix a high threshold \(u\)
                  • model the excesses \(Y = L - u\) given \(L > u\)
                  • Pickands–Balkema–de Haan theorem Pickands (1975), Balkema & de Haan (1974): for a wide class of distributions, as \(u\) approaches the right end-point of the distribution the excesses follow a generalised Pareto distribution
                • The GPD distribution function of the excess
                  • \(G_{\xi,\beta}(y) = 1 - \left(1 + \xi y/\beta\right)^{-1/\xi}\) (\(\xi \ne 0\)), \(1 - e^{-y/\beta}\) (\(\xi = 0\))
                  • \(y \ge 0\), and \(y < -\beta/\xi\) if \(\xi < 0\); scale \(\beta > 0\): the typical size of an excess; shape \(\xi\): the same as in the GEV, \(\xi > 0\) = heavy tail

                Peaks over Threshold and the GPD (2/2)

                Slide 41 in the PDF
                • Tail estimator with \(N_u\) exceedances among \(n\) observations McNeil, Frey & Embrechts (2015)
                  • \(\widehat{\VaR}_\alpha = u + \dfrac{\hat\beta}{\hat\xi}\left[\left(\dfrac{n}{N_u}\,\alpha\right)^{-\hat\xi} - 1\right], \qquad \widehat{\ES}_\alpha = \dfrac{\widehat{\VaR}_\alpha}{1 - \hat\xi} + \dfrac{\hat\beta - \hat\xi u}{1 - \hat\xi}\) (\(\alpha < N_u/n\), \(\hat\xi \ne 0\); ES needs \(\hat\xi < 1\))
                  • In words: VaR is the threshold \(u\) plus the GPD quantile of the excess that leaves probability \(\alpha\) in the tail; \(N_u/n\) is the share of days above \(u\)
                  • ES adds to VaR the mean excess beyond VaR, which grows with \(\hat\xi\); \(\hat\xi \ge 1\) means an infinite mean
                • Bias–variance trade-off: a low \(u\) violates the limit theorem, a high \(u\) leaves few exceedances

                Choosing the Threshold: the Mean Excess Function

                Slide 42 in the PDF
                • Mean excess function: \(e(u) = \E[L - u \mid L > u]\)
                  • empirically, the average excess above \(u\), plotted against \(u\)
                • For a GPD tail \(e(u)\) is linear in \(u\)
                  • \(e(u) = \dfrac{\beta + \xi (u - u_0)}{1 - \xi}\) for \(u \ge u_0\), finite only if \(\xi < 1\); slope \(\xi/(1 - \xi)\): upward for heavy tails, flat for exponential tails
                  • \(u_0\): the lowest threshold above which the GPD holds; \(\beta\): the GPD scale at \(u_0\)
                • Rule of thumb: choose \(u\) where the plot becomes roughly linear
                  • check the stability of \(\hat\xi\) across thresholds
                • The Hill estimator Hill (1975), on the largest \(k+1\) losses, \(L^{(1)} \ge \dots \ge L^{(k+1)}\)
                  • \(\hat\xi^{H} = \frac1k\sum_{i=1}^{k}\ln L^{(i)} - \ln L^{(k+1)}\)
                  • the tail index is \(1/\hat\xi^{H}\) (Chapter 1)

                      • Autocorrelation: \(\Var(r_{t+1} + \dots + r_{t+h}) = h\sigma^2\,\mathrm{VR}(h)\)
                        • variance ratio \(\mathrm{VR}(h) = 1\) without autocorrelation (Chapter 2)
                        • \(\mathrm{VR}(h) > 1\) (positive autocorrelation): \(\sqrt{h}\) underestimates the \(h\)-day variance
                        • VaR and ES follow the variance only in a location-scale family, e.g. the Normal distribution
                      • Volatility clustering: volatility reverts to its mean over \(h\) days
                        • Calm today: the \(h\)-day variance exceeds \(h\,\sigma_{t+1}^2\), so \(\sqrt{h}\) tends to give too low a risk
                        • Turbulent today: \(\sqrt{h}\) tends to give too high a risk
                        • For the quantile, the direction is checked by filtered Monte Carlo (next slides)
                      • Heavy tails: for i.i.d. shocks, \(\sqrt{h}\) can overstate the far-tail quantile Daníelsson (2011)
                        • tail index \(a > 2\): \(P(L > x)\) decays like \(x^{-a}\) (regular variation)
                        • with \(h\) fixed and \(\alpha \to 0\), the quantile of the sum grows like \(h^{1/a} < \sqrt{h}\)
                        • at the finite levels of the course (1%, 2.5%) this is an approximation, not a rule
                      • With jumps the rule can badly misstate multi-day risk Daníelsson & Zigrand (2006)

                          Portfolio VaR and Euler Allocation (1/2)

                          Slide 55 in the PDF
                          • Setting: portfolio loss \(L_p = -w^\top R\)
                            • \(w\): vector of weights; \(R\): vector of simple returns, with covariance matrix \(\Sigma\); \(\sigma_i\): standard deviation of asset \(i\)
                          • Normal returns, mean ignored: VaR is a multiple of the portfolio standard deviation
                            • \(\VaR_\alpha(L_p) = z_{1-\alpha}\sigma_p\), \(\quad\sigma_p = \sqrt{w^\top\Sigma w}\); \(z_{1-\alpha} = -z_\alpha > 0\)
                            • For long-only \(w \ge 0\), the undiversified VaR \(\sum_i z_{1-\alpha} w_i\sigma_i\) (the sum of stand-alone VaRs) is larger unless all correlations are 1

                          Portfolio VaR and Euler Allocation (2/2)

                          Slide 56 in the PDF
                          • Marginal VaR: the change in portfolio VaR per marginal increase of weight \(w_i\)
                            • \(\partial\VaR_\alpha/\partial w_i = z_{1-\alpha}(\Sigma w)_i/\sigma_p\); \((\Sigma w)_i\): the covariance of asset \(i\) with the portfolio
                          • Component VaR \(C_i = w_i\,\partial\VaR_\alpha/\partial w_i\): the part of VaR attributed to asset \(i\)
                            • Euler's theorem for a function homogeneous of degree 1: \(\sum_i w_i\,\partial\rho/\partial w_i = \rho\), so the components add up, \(\sum_i C_i = \VaR_\alpha\) Tasche (2008)
                          • ES, no distributional assumption (continuous losses)
                            • the mean loss of asset \(i\) on the portfolio's tail days
                            • \(C_i^{ES} = \E[-w_i R_i \mid L_p \ge \VaR_\alpha(L_p)]\), \(\quad\sum_i C_i^{ES} = \ES_\alpha(L_p)\), estimated historically as the fractionally weighted average over the tail days (the weights of historical ES)
                            • A negative \(C_i\) means the asset hedges the portfolio
                          • Why Euler Tasche (2008)
                            • homogeneity of degree 1 gives full allocation, but many allocations add up to the risk
                            • Euler is characterised by compatibility with RORAC (return on risk-adjusted capital), for differentiable \(\rho\)
                            • that is, it correctly shows where a larger weight improves the return per unit of risk

                          Liquidity-Adjusted VaR

                          Slide 57 in the PDF
                          • The question: how much does selling the position in a stressed market add to VaR?
                          • VaR assumes a sale at the mid price \(= (\text{bid} + \text{ask})/2\)
                            • an immediate sale goes at the bid and costs half the relative spread \(S = (\text{ask} - \text{bid})/\text{mid}\)
                          • The spread widens exactly when markets fall
                          • Bangia, Diebold, Schuermann & Stroughair (2001): \(\mathrm{LVaR}_\alpha = \VaR_\alpha + \tfrac12(\mu_S + k\,\sigma_S)\)
                            • \(\mu_S\), \(\sigma_S\): the mean and standard deviation of \(S\) (here \(S\) is the spread, not skewness)
                            • \(k\): the number of standard deviations that take the spread to its stressed level
                          • Banca Transilvania, a position of 1 million RON
                            • the one-day historical VaR 1% over the last 496 days is \(3.16\%\)
                          • With assumed \(\mu_S = 0.5\%\), \(\sigma_S = 0.3\%\) and \(k = 3\): \(\mathrm{LVaR} = 3.86\%\) (\(+22\%\))
                            • In money: VaR \(\approx 31\,581\) RON, spread cost \(\tfrac12(0.5 + 3 \times 0.3)\% = 0.70\% \to 7\,000\) RON, LVaR \(\approx 38\,581\) RON
                          • Large positions also move the price (endogenous liquidity)
                            • that is why FRTB handles liquidity through liquidity horizons of up to 120 days

                                  ES as an Optimisation Problem: Rockafellar–Uryasev

                                  Slide 78 in the PDF
                                  • Theorem Rockafellar & Uryasev (2000): for any integrable loss \(L\)
                                    • \(\ES_\alpha(L) = \min_{v \in \mathbb{R}} F(v)\), \(\quad F(v) = v + \tfrac{1}{\alpha}\E[(L - v)_+]\), \(\quad (x)_+ = \max(x, 0)\)
                                    • \(v\): a candidate threshold; \(F(v)\): the threshold plus the expected excess of the loss over it, scaled up by \(1/\alpha\)
                                    • The minimiser is \(\VaR_\alpha(L)\), the minimum value is \(\ES_\alpha(L)\); \(F(\VaR_\alpha)\) is the discrete ES formula of the two-bond slide
                                  • \(F\) is convex in \(v\) and, for a portfolio loss \(-w^\top R\), jointly convex in \((w, v)\)
                                    • \(w\): vector of weights; \(R\): vector of asset returns
                                    • Convexity together with positive homogeneity gives subadditivity: \(\ES_\alpha(L_1+L_2) = 2\,\ES_\alpha(\tfrac12 L_1 + \tfrac12 L_2) \le \ES_\alpha(L_1) + \ES_\alpha(L_2)\) (proof in the Appendix)
                                    • With historical scenarios, minimising ES becomes a linear program (the Portfolio VaR section)
                                  • S&P 500 losses, \(\alpha = 2.5\%\): \(\arg\min F = 2.51\%\) (the minimising \(v\)), \(\min F = 3.76\%\)
                                    • \(\min F\) equals the historical ES 2.5% exactly
                                    • \(\arg\min F\) equals the historical VaR 2.5% only after rounding: the empirical minimiser is an order statistic

                                  Bootstrap and HAC: How the Intervals Are Built

                                  Slide 81 in the PDF
                                  • Bootstrap (i.i.d.): draw \(n\) losses with replacement from the sample and recompute \(\widehat{\VaR}\) or \(\widehat{\ES}\)
                                    • Repeat \(B = 2\,000\) times; the 95% interval runs from the \(2.5\%\) to the \(97.5\%\) quantile of the \(B\) recomputed values
                                  • Moving-block bootstrap: draw blocks of 20 consecutive days instead of single days
                                    • join \(n/20\) of them
                                    • Calm and turbulent stretches stay intact inside each block, so every resample keeps the volatility clustering
                                  • HAC long-run variance of a series \(y_t\) (here \(\mathbf{1}\{L_t > \VaR_\alpha\}\) or \((L_t - \VaR_\alpha)_+\)): \(\hat\gamma_0 + 2\sum_{j=1}^{m}\left(1 - \frac{j}{m+1}\right)\hat\gamma_j\)
                                    • \(\hat\gamma_j\): sample autocovariance of \(y_t\) at lag \(j\), \(\hat\gamma_0\) its variance; Bartlett weights \(1 - j/(m+1)\) shrink distant lags; lag \(m = \lfloor 4(n/100)^{2/9} \rfloor\) (\(m = 10\) for the full S&P 500 sample)
                                    • It replaces the i.i.d. variance \(\hat\gamma_0\) in the SE formulas. Toy check: \(\hat\gamma_0 = 1\), \(\hat\gamma_1 = 0.3\), all other \(\hat\gamma_j = 0\), \(m = 1\): long-run variance \(1 + 2 \times 0.5 \times 0.3 = 1.3\), so the SE grows by \(\sqrt{1.3} \approx 1.14\)
                                  • Worked numbers for the BET: Seminar 7, B1 (i.i.d., HAC and block-bootstrap intervals for VaR 1%)

                                  Clustered Extremes: the Extremal Index

                                  Slide 87 in the PDF
                                  • Stationary losses with clustering: if \(n P(L > u_n) \to \tau\), then \(P(M_n \le u_n) \to e^{-\theta\tau}\), \(\theta \in (0, 1]\) Embrechts, Klüppelberg & Mikosch (1997)
                                    • \(u_n\): a threshold rising with \(n\); \(\tau\): the expected number of exceedances of \(u_n\) in \(n\) days; \(M_n\): the maximum of the \(n\) losses
                                    • \(\theta\) = extremal index, \(1/\theta\) = mean cluster size; for the maximum, \(n\) dependent days act like \(n\theta\) independent ones
                                  • S&P 500, GPD threshold (\(N = 336\) exceedances): intervals estimator Ferro & Segers (2003) gives \(\hat\theta = 0.19\)
                                    • i.e. 62 clusters of 5.4 exceedances on average

                                  • Standardised GARCH residuals: \(\hat\theta = 0.99\)
                                    • after filtering, threshold exceedances no longer cluster (estimator and declustering: Appendix)
                                    • \(\hat\theta \approx 1\) concerns the clustering of extremes, not independence of the whole series
                                  • This is why conditional EVT fits the GPD after the filter

                                  Filtered Monte Carlo over \(h\) Days

                                  Slide 88 in the PDF
                                  • Simulate many future paths from the fitted model
                                    • read VaR and ES from the simulated losses
                                    • handles autocorrelation, volatility clustering and heavy tails at once, as well as non-linear pay-offs (options)
                                  • For each path \(j = 1, \dots, M\) (\(M\): the number of simulated paths)
                                    • draw \(z^{(j)}_{t+1}, \dots, z^{(j)}_{t+h}\) with replacement from the standardised residuals \(\hat z\)
                                    • update \(\sigma^{(j)}\) and \(r^{(j)}\) day by day with the AR(1)-GARCH(1,1) recursion, starting from today's state
                                    • aggregate: \(L^{(j)} = -\sum_{i=1}^h r^{(j)}_{t+i}\), the \(h\)-day loss of path \(j\) (\(i\): day within the path)
                                  • \(\widehat{\VaR}_\alpha\), \(\widehat{\ES}_\alpha\) = historical estimates on the \(M\) simulated losses
                                    • simulation error falls as \(1/\sqrt{M}\)

                                  Minimum-ES Portfolio: a Linear Program

                                  Slide 89 in the PDF
                                  • With \(N\) historical scenarios \(R_s\), the Rockafellar–Uryasev objective becomes a linear program Rockafellar & Uryasev (2000)

                                  Case Study: the Conditional Quantile as a Neural Network

                                  Slide 92 in the PDF
                                  • Model (Eqs. 5–10): the conditional return quantile is a neural network of the predictors
                                    • \(Q_{r_t}(\alpha \mid x_t) \approx G_\alpha(x_t, w)\)
                                    • \(Q_{r_t}(\alpha \mid x_t)\): the \(\alpha\)-quantile of \(r_t\) given \(x_t\); \(G_\alpha\): a feed-forward network with ReLU (rectified linear unit, \(\max(\cdot, 0)\)) hidden layers; \(w\): its weights

                                  Proof of the Rockafellar–Uryasev Representation

                                  Slide 95 in the PDF
                                  • Statement: for continuous losses, \(\ES_\alpha(L) = \min_v F(v)\) Rockafellar & Uryasev (2000)
                                    • \(F(v) = v + \E[(L - v)_+]/\alpha\)
                                    • the minimum is attained at \(v = \VaR_\alpha\)
                                  • Proof: \(F\) is convex, with right derivative \(1 - P(L > v)/\alpha\), which is \(\ge 0\) exactly when \(v \ge \VaR_\alpha\)
                                  • Value of the minimum: \(F(\VaR_\alpha) = \VaR_\alpha + \E[(L - \VaR_\alpha)_+]/\alpha = \E[L \mid L \ge \VaR_\alpha] = \ES_\alpha\)
                                  • Corollary: \((w, v) \mapsto v + \E[(-w^\top R - v)_+]/\alpha\) is jointly convex
                                    • minimising over \(v\) keeps convexity
                                    • ES is convex in \(w\); with positive homogeneity this proves subadditivity

                                  When Is VaR Subadditive After All?

                                  Slide 96 in the PDF
                                  • Jointly elliptical losses, \(\alpha < 0.5\): VaR is subadditive (slide “Coherent Risk Measures (2/2)”) McNeil, Frey & Embrechts (2015)
                                    • Normal or \(t\) marginals alone are not enough: the joint distribution must be elliptical
                                  • Heavy but not too heavy tails: VaR is subadditive far enough in the tail Daníelsson et al. (2013)
                                    • for multivariate regularly varying losses with tail index above 1
                                    • asymptotically, as \(\alpha \to 0\)
                                    • Estimated tail indices of equity and FX returns are in that range (between 4.8 and 6.5 in the Extreme Value Theory section), but the result is asymptotic, not a guarantee at a given \(\alpha\)
                                  • It fails for discrete jumps (defaults), extremely heavy tails and some option pay-offs
                                  • VaR vs ES in practice: ES needs more data for the same precision Yamai & Yoshiba (2005)

                                  Asymptotic Theory for Historical VaR and ES

                                  Slide 100 in the PDF
                                  • VaR: for i.i.d. losses with density \(f\) continuous and positive at \(\VaR_\alpha\), \(\sqrt{n}\,(\widehat{\VaR}_\alpha - \VaR_\alpha) \to N\!\left(0, \dfrac{\alpha(1 - \alpha)}{f(\VaR_\alpha)^2}\right)\)

                                  Estimation Risk in Conditional VaR

                                  Slide 104 in the PDF
                                  • \(\VaR_{\alpha,t+1} = -\mu_{t+1}(\theta) + \sigma_{t+1}(\theta)\,\xi_\alpha\), \(\xi_\alpha = -q_\alpha(z)\)
                                    • both \(\theta\) (GARCH, by QML) and \(\xi_\alpha\) (a residual quantile) are estimated
                                    • \(\theta\): the AR(1)-GARCH(1,1) parameters; QML: quasi-maximum likelihood; \(\mu_{t+1}(\theta)\), \(\sigma_{t+1}(\theta)\): the forecasts they imply
                                  • Francq & Zakoïan (2015): estimate the risk parameter \((\theta, \xi_\alpha)\) jointly
                                    • conditions: strict stationarity, \(\E[z_t^4] < \infty\) and a positive density of \(z\) at \(q_\alpha(z)\)
                                    • then the conditional VaR is asymptotically Normal, with a variance from \(\hat\theta\), from \(\hat\xi_\alpha\) and from their covariance
                                  • Residual bootstrap Christoffersen & Gonçalves (2005), for \(b = 1, \dots, B\)
                                    • \(B\): number of bootstrap samples; a star marks a value computed on a bootstrap sample
                                    • simulate an AR(1)-GARCH(1,1) path of the sample length with resampled \(\hat z\) and re-estimate \(\theta^*\) on it
                                    • \(\sigma^*_{t+1}\) from the original returns with \(\theta^*\); \(\xi^*_\alpha\) from the path's own residuals; \(\VaR^*_{t+1} = -\mu^*_{t+1} + \sigma^*_{t+1}\xi^*_\alpha\); 90% percentile interval: the 5% and 95% quantiles of the \(B\) values \(\VaR^*_{t+1}\)
                                  • Heavy tails and skewness of \(z\) slow the convergence
                                    • Spierdijk (2016) compares interval methods for ARMA–GARCH VaR in exactly this case
                                    • ARMA: autoregressive moving average conditional mean
