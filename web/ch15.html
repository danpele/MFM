Tetlock (2007) measures the pessimism of a Wall Street Journal column and shows that it is followed by downward pressure on prices and then by a reversion. The chapter starts from the same question with today's tools: how well do models read the sentiment of a financial text, and can that sentiment be traded? Four routes are compared: word dictionaries, the FinBERT model trained on labelled financial sentences, large language models (LLMs) used zero-shot, and embeddings followed by a classifier. General dictionaries misread financial text: in the lists used here, 77% of the negative words of the Harvard General Inquirer (GI) are not among the negative words of the financial dictionary of Loughran & McDonald (2011).

On financial headlines from Twitter, no model wins outright. The best is the simplest supervised model, TF-IDF (term frequency–inverse document frequency) followed by a logistic regression, with 83.2% accuracy, against 74.2% for Qwen2.5-7B and 72.5% for FinBERT. Labels from the target domain matter more than model size. An LLM also raises a look-ahead bias problem: asked only for the month, Qwen2.5-14B separates up from down months of the S&P 500 with an area under the ROC curve (AUC) of 0.69, where 0.5 means no discrimination.

On 16 large US stocks, with 106 820 distinct headlines from FNSPID (Financial News and Stock Price Integration Dataset), the scores explain the price move on the day of the news: for FinBERT, one standard deviation of the score corresponds to an excess return 39.2 basis points (bp) higher. Many headlines, however, report the price move itself. A daily long-short strategy on the sign of the score, with positions opened after the news day, brings no net profit. The best score, Qwen2.5-7B, earns 5.64 bp per day before costs, but \(p\) = 0.071 after the Holm correction, and its break-even cost, 1.5 bp per trade, is below the assumed cost of 5 bp. The Sharpe ratio is +0.63 before costs and −1.48 after costs.

Dictionaries

Can a Word List Measure the Tone of a Financial Text?

Slide 7 in the PDF
  • Bag of words: a document is reduced to its word counts
    • Word order is ignored
    • Tokens: case-insensitive words, without digits and punctuation
  • A dictionary lists positive words \(\mathcal{P}\) and negative words \(\mathcal{N}\)
    • \(P_d\), \(N_d\): number of positive and negative words in document \(d\)
    • Tone: \(\tau_d = \dfrac{P_d - N_d}{P_d + N_d} \in [-1, 1]\), and \(\tau_d = 0\) if \(P_d + N_d = 0\); the class is the sign of \(\tau_d\) (neutral for \(\tau_d = 0\))
  • Transparent, fast and free of training data
    • Blind to context and negation

Generic, Financial and Rule-Based Dictionaries

Slide 8 in the PDF
  • Harvard General Inquirer (GI), word list Harvard IV-4: a psychology dictionary from the 1960s
    • 2 007 negative and 1 637 positive words
  • Loughran & McDonald (2011) (LM): built from annual reports (10-K filings) of US firms
    • 2 355 negative and only 354 positive words
    • Their finding: almost three quarters of the GI negative words are not negative in a financial context
    • In the lists used here, 1 552 GI negative words (77%) are not LM negative words
  • VADER (Hutto & Gilbert (2014)): about 7,500 terms with intensity scores, built for social media
    • Rules for negation ("not good"), intensifiers ("very"), capital letters, exclamation marks and emoticons
    • Compound score in \([-1, 1]\), usual cut-offs \(\pm 0.05\)
    • The rules fix some context problems, but the vocabulary is generic, like GI

    Which Texts Do We Judge a Classifier On? Two Labelled Corpora

    Slide 11 in the PDF

      • Question: good, bad or neutral news for the investor?
      • Agreement levels: at least 50%, 66%, 75% or 100% of the sentence's annotators; 2 264 sentences with full agreement, 629 below 66%
      • Labels: 59% neutral, 28% positive, 12% negative
    • Twitter Financial News (Hugging Face Hub; MIT licence): headlines from 2022
      • Labelled bearish, bullish or neutral
      • Training set of 9,543, validation set of 2 388 headlines (used for evaluation)
      • Labels: 66% neutral, 20% positive, 15% negative
      • Short, noisy texts with tickers and links: closer to the real news flow
    • Two corpora, because a result on one may not carry over to the other

    Accuracy or Macro-F1? Metrics for Unbalanced Classes

    Slide 12 in the PDF
    • The question: how do we compare classifiers when one class dominates?
    • Accuracy: \(\text{acc} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} \mathbf{1}\{\hat y_i = y_i\}\)
    • For class \(k\): precision \(P_k = \dfrac{TP_k}{TP_k + FP_k}\), recall \(R_k = \dfrac{TP_k}{TP_k + FN_k}\), \(F1_k = \dfrac{2 P_k R_k}{P_k + R_k}\)
      • \(TP_k\): texts of class \(k\) predicted \(k\); \(FP_k\): texts of other classes predicted \(k\); \(FN_k\): texts of class \(k\) predicted otherwise
    • Macro-F1 \(= \frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K} F1_k\), with \(K = 3\)
      • Every class counts the same, however rare (Chapter 13)
    • Benchmark "always neutral": 66% accuracy, but a macro-F1 of only 26.4%
      • \(F1_{\text{neutral}} = 2 \times 0.656 \times 1/(0.656 + 1) = 0.792\), and \(F1 = 0\) for the other two classes, so macro-F1 \(= 0.792/3\)

    When Is One Classifier Better? The McNemar Test on the Same Texts

    Slide 13 in the PDF
    • Two classifiers A and B on the same \(n\) texts: their accuracies are correlated
      • So two separate CIs do not answer the question
    • \(n_{01}\): number of texts where A is wrong and B is right
    • \(n_{10}\): number of texts where A is right and B is wrong
    • Texts on which both classifiers agree carry no information
      • The accuracy gap is \((n_{01} - n_{10})/n\)
    • Under \(H_0\) (equal accuracy), \(n_{01} \sim \text{Binomial}(n_{01} + n_{10}, 0.5)\)
      • An exact test (worked example: Appendix)
    • For large \(n_{01} + n_{10}\): \(\chi^2 = (|n_{01} - n_{10}| - 1)^2/(n_{01} + n_{10})\) is approximately \(\chi^2_1\)
      • \(-1\): continuity correction
    • Example: A is wrong and B right on 4 texts, the reverse on 1
      • So \(p = 2\,P(X \ge 4) = 2 \times 6/32 = 0.375\), with \(X \sim \text{Binomial}(5, 0.5)\)

      Language Models: FinBERT and LLMs

      Does Context Help? From Words to BERT

      Slide 16 in the PDF
      • A dictionary gives "lower" one fixed sign
        • A language model reads the sentence around it
        • Each token gets a vector that depends on all other tokens (attention, Chapter 14; Vaswani et al. (2017))
      • BERT (Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers; Devlin et al. (2019))
        • A Transformer encoder pretrained on books and Wikipedia by guessing words hidden at random
        • About 110 million parameters
        • For classification it uses the final vector \(h_{\text{CLS}} \in \mathbb{R}^{768}\) of the special token [CLS], placed at the start of every text
      • Fine-tuning: a classification layer on top of \(h_{\text{CLS}}\)
        • The whole network is trained on labelled sentences
        • \(p = \operatorname{softmax}(W h_{\text{CLS}} + c)\), with \(W \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times 768}\): probabilities of the negative, neutral and positive classes
        • Training minimises the cross-entropy \(-\frac{1}{n}\sum_i \ln p_{i, y_i}\), with \(y_i\) the human label of sentence \(i\)

      FinBERT: BERT Adapted to Financial Text

      Slide 17 in the PDF
      • Araci (2019): FinBERT is BERT further pretrained on financial news, then fine-tuned for sentiment
        • The public model used here (ProsusAI/finbert) was fine-tuned on Financial PhraseBank
        • A few milliseconds per sentence on a laptop
      • The score used in this chapter: \(s = p_{\text{pos}} - p_{\text{neg}} \in [-1, 1]\)
        • The label is \(\arg\max_k p_k\)
      • Why FinBERT as the benchmark: public, fast and widely used in the finance literature
      • Huang, Wang & Yang (2023): a FinBERT trained on analyst reports, earnings-call transcripts and 10-K filings
        • It outperforms dictionaries, mainly on sentences that dictionaries misread

      The In-Sample Trap

      Slide 18 in the PDF
      • FinBERT: 89.0% on PhraseBank, most of which it was trained on
        • Araci (2019): 3,101 of 4,845 sentences
        • 72.5% on Twitter: a drop of 16.4 points
        • Qwen2.5-7B, not fine-tuned on these labels, also drops by 7.2 points: Twitter headlines are harder
      • Difference in differences: \(\Delta = (a_{F,P} - a_{F,T}) - (a_{Q,P} - a_{Q,T})\) = 9.2 points
        • Bootstrap CI 95% [6.7; 11.9]
        • \(a_{m,D}\): accuracy of model \(m\) (F: FinBERT, Q: Qwen) on data \(D\) (P: PhraseBank, T: Twitter)
        • Identifying assumption: without training overlap, both models would lose the same amount from PhraseBank to Twitter
        • The CI measures sampling noise, not the assumption (the two architectures may react differently to a new domain)
      • What the result supports: a descriptive difference between the accuracy drops
        • Attributing \(\Delta\) to training overlap also needs an uncontaminated comparator and no other differential change
        • Qwen was not fine-tuned on PhraseBank, but the texts may be in its pretraining data
      • Rule: evaluate a language model only on texts it has never seen
        • As in the out-of-sample tests of Chapters 8 and 13

      From Next-Word Prediction to Instructions

      Slide 19 in the PDF
      • Shannon (1951): English is highly predictable from its past letters
        • A large language model (LLM) estimates \(P(w_{t+1} \mid w_1, \dots, w_t)\) over tokens
        • \(w_t\): the \(t\)-th token, a word or part of a word
        • Brown et al. (2020): at scale, tasks are solved from a few examples in the prompt (few-shot)
        • Ouyang et al. (2022): instruction tuning and RLHF (reinforcement learning from human feedback) teach the model to follow requests
      • BloombergGPT (Wu et al. (2023)): 50 billion parameters, financial and general text
        • Background: Quantinar: The Next Word Problem, Quantinar: Natural Language Processing

      Zero-Shot Classification with Qwen2.5: the Prompt and the Probabilities

      Slide 20 in the PDF
      • Prompt: a system instruction plus the text to classify
        • You classify the sentiment of financial news for investors. Answer with one word: positive, negative or neutral.
        • Headline: <text> Sentiment:
      • We read the logits \(z_k\) of the tokens "positive", "negative", "neutral" at the first generated position
        • \(p_k = e^{z_k} / \sum_j e^{z_j}\), normalised over the three answer words
        • Label: \(\arg\max_k p_k\)
        • Score: \(p_{\text{pos}} - p_{\text{neg}}\), as for FinBERT

      Temperature and the Qwen2.5 Models

      Slide 21 in the PDF
      • When generating, the model draws the next token from \(p_k(T) = e^{z_k/T} / \sum_j e^{z_j/T}\)
        • With temperature \(T > 0\)
        • \(T \to 0\): always the most likely token (greedy decoding), so the same answer on every run; Lopez-Lira & Tang (2026) use temperature 0
        • \(T = 1\): the model's own distribution; \(T > 1\) flattens the probabilities, so answers vary more
      • No text is generated here: the label \(\arg\max_k p_k\) is the \(T \to 0\) answer
        • The score keeps the \(T = 1\) probabilities, with nothing to parse
      • Qwen Team (2024): open-weight models, from 0.5 to 72 billion parameters
        • Licences: Apache 2.0; Qwen Research License for 3B
        • Released on 19 September 2024
      • We use 0.5B, 1.5B, 3B, 7B and 14B (B: billion parameters)
        • Processing times: Appendix
      • Open weights: results can be reproduced
        • The release date bounds what the model could know

        Embeddings as Features

        Can Generic Text Vectors Measure Sentiment?

        Slide 23 in the PDF
          Question for the room The best direction in the plane of the first two principal components of the MiniLM vectors separates positive from negative headlines with AUC ≈ 0.636. What does this imply about the sentiment information in the embeddings?
          Answer

          Linear separation in this plane is modest, but the result does not rule out useful information in the other dimensions. PCA maximises explained variance without using the labels. Here the AUC measures only the ranking of positive against negative headlines; it is not three-class accuracy. A logistic regression on all 384 dimensions reaches 78.6% accuracy on Twitter.

          • Embedding: a vector \(e_d \in \mathbb{R}^k\), close for similar texts
            • word2vec (Mikolov et al. (2013)), Sentence-BERT (Reimers & Gurevych (2019))
            • Here MiniLM (Wang et al. (2020)), \(k = 384\), unit length: similarity \(\cos(e_a, e_b) = e_a^\top e_b\); embeddings enter a classifier or regression like any feature
          • Chart: Twitter validation headlines, first two principal components of the MiniLM vectors
            • Coloured by label
          • The first two components do not separate sentiment
            • PCA (principal component analysis, Chapter 3) keeps the directions of largest variance
            • Not those tied to the label
          • A logistic regression on all 384 dimensions finds the sentiment direction: 78.6% accuracy on Twitter

            Look-Ahead Bias and Memorisation

            May the Model Already Know the Answer?

            Slide 26 in the PDF
            • An LLM trained on text up to 2024 may have read what happened after a 2015 headline
              • A backtest on 2015 news then mixes forecasting with remembering
            • Glasserman & Lin (2023): two biases in sentiment backtests
              • Look-ahead: the model knows the returns that followed the news
              • Distraction: general knowledge about the named company interferes with reading the text; in their tests it mattered more than look-ahead
            • Sarkar & Vafa (2024): direct tests show look-ahead in LLM applications to earnings calls and elections

            Our Test: Ask About the Past, Without Context

            Slide 27 in the PDF
            • Monthly: "Did the S&P 500 index rise or fall during <month year>?"
              • Every month from 296 pre-release months plus 23 after
              • Daily: "Did Apple stock close higher or lower on <date> than on the previous trading day?", 500 random days 2010–2023 and 493 days after October 2024

                  Inference with 16 Stocks: Clustering by Stock and Two-Way

                  Slide 37 in the PDF
                  • The question: do the slopes survive if errors are also correlated over time within a stock?
                  • Firm effects persist, so the errors of one stock are correlated across days: cluster by stock as well
                  • Two-way clustering (Cameron, Gelbach & Miller (2011)): \(\widehat V_{\text{2way}} = \widehat V_{\text{day}} + \widehat V_{\text{stock}} - \widehat V_{\text{day} \cap \text{stock}}\)
                    • Each term is the sandwich formula of the previous slide, with clusters given by day, by stock, and by the stock–day pair
                    • A stock–day pair holds a single observation here, so the last term is the White estimator, robust to heteroskedasticity only
                    • Subtracting it avoids counting the variance of each observation twice
                  • Driscoll & Kraay (1998) also allow for correlation across different days

                  Few Clusters: the Wild Cluster Bootstrap

                  Slide 38 in the PDF
                  • With only \(G = 16\) stocks, the stock-clustered SE is too small and the \(t\) test rejects too often (Cameron, Gelbach & Miller (2008))
                  • Wild cluster bootstrap with the null imposed: fit the restricted model (\(b = 0\))
                    • Constant \(\tilde a\) and residuals \(\tilde\varepsilon_{i,t}\)
                    • For \(j = 1, \dots, B\), \(B = 9{,}999\): Rademacher weights \(w_g = \pm 1\) with probability \(\frac12\), one draw per stock \(g\)
                    • \(r^{e*}_{i,t} = \tilde a + w_{g(i)}\,\tilde\varepsilon_{i,t}\); re-fit the regression and compute the stock-clustered \(t^*_j\)
                    • \(p = \frac{1}{B}\sum_j \mathbf{1}\{|t^*_j| \ge |t|\}\)
                  • The same sign for all residuals of a stock keeps the correlation within the cluster
                  • The bootstrap assumes independent stocks, so read it next to the two-way \(t\)

                  Nine Regressions: the Holm Correction

                  Slide 39 in the PDF
                  • Nine regressions: 3 scores \(\times\) 3 horizons (\(h\) = 0, 1, 2)
                  • If no score had any effect, nine tests at 5% would still give \(9 \times 0.05 = 0.45\) false rejections on average
                  • The Holm correction (Chapter 2) keeps the FWER at or below 5%
                    • FWER (family-wise error rate): the chance of any false rejection
                    • Sort the \(p\)-values: \(p_{(1)} \le \dots \le p_{(m)}\), with \(m = 9\)
                    • \(\tilde p_{(k)} = \max_{j \le k} \min\{1, (m - j + 1)\,p_{(j)}\}\); reject \(H_{(k)}\) when \(\tilde p_{(k)} \le 0.05\)
                    • More powerful than Bonferroni (\(m\,p_{(k)}\) for all) and valid under any dependence between the tests

                  A Daily Long-Short Sentiment Strategy

                  Slide 41 in the PDF
                  • The question: does the sign of the score earn a profit we can actually collect?
                  • At the close of \(d+1\): for every stock with news and a non-zero score on \(d\), go long or short
                    • Long if its score is positive and short if negative
                    • Each position hedged with SPY; a one-for-one hedge removes the market move only when \(\beta = 1\)
                    • Equal weights; positions are closed after one day, at the close of \(d+2\)
                    • The LM score is zero on 48% of stock-days: these do not enter the portfolio
                  • Gross return on \(d+2\): \(G_{d+2} = \frac{1}{N_d}\sum_{i \in A_d} \operatorname{sign}(s_{i,d})\,(r_{i,d+2} - r_{\text{SPY},d+2})\)
                    • \(A_d = \{i : |s_{i,d}| > b\}\), with threshold \(b = 0\): the stocks with news and a non-zero score on day \(d\)
                    • \(N_d = |A_d|\): the positions opened at the close of \(d+1\); if \(A_d\) is empty, \(G_{d+2} = 0\) and no costs are paid

                  Trading Costs: Round Trips and the Break-Even Cost

                  Slide 42 in the PDF
                  • \(c\) = 5 bp per one-way trade, per unit of traded notional
                    • Assumed, not estimated; plausible for large, liquid US stocks
                  • A round trip (in and out) costs \(2c\)
                  • On a day with positions: one round trip for the stock book and one for the SPY hedge
                    • Each of notional 1, so \(4c\) = 20 bp
                  • Net return: \(G^{\text{net}}_{d+2} = G_{d+2} - 4c\,\mathbf{1}\{N_d > 0\}\)
                  • Break-even cost \(c^*\): the value of \(c\) at which the mean net return is 0
                    • I.e. \(c^* = \bar G_{\text{active}}/4\)
                    • \(\bar G_{\text{active}}\): the mean gross return on days with positions

                    Sentiment as a Generated Regressor: Classical Error

                    Slide 46 in the PDF
                    • The question of this section: what happens to the slope and its interval
                      • When the regressor is a model output, not the true tone?
                    • Model: \(r = a + \beta s^* + \varepsilon\) with true tone \(s^*\)
                      • We observe a model output \(\hat s = s^* + u\)
                      • Classical error (\(u\) independent of \(s^*\) and \(\varepsilon\)): \(\operatorname{plim} \hat b = \beta\lambda\), \(\lambda = \text{Var}(s^*)/[\text{Var}(s^*) + \text{Var}(u)] < 1\)
                      • \(\lambda\): the reliability of the score, the share of its variance that is signal
                      • The test of \(\beta = 0\) stays valid; magnitudes are attenuated towards 0

                    Standardised Score: Correcting by the Correlation with the True Tone

                    Slide 47 in the PDF
                    • Standardised score \(z\), non-differential error: \(\text{Cov}(r, z) = \beta\,\text{Cov}(s^*, z)\)
                      • Non-differential error: \(E[r \mid s^*, \hat s] = E[r \mid s^*]\)
                      • Since \(\text{Var}(z) = 1\), the slope is \(b_z = \text{Cov}(r, z) = \beta\,\sigma_{s^*}\,\rho(s^*, \hat s)\)
                      • Slope on \(z\) = (effect of one standard deviation of \(s^*\)) \(\times\ \rho(s^*, \hat s)\): divide by the correlation of truth and score
                      • A binary label is not classical error: misclassification shrinks a dummy slope by a different factor (Aigner (1973); Appendix and Seminar 15, A1)
                    • Econometrics of AI-generated variables
                      • Battaglia et al. (2024): bias correction, joint estimation
                      • Ludwig, Mullainathan & Rambachan (2024): prediction versus estimation uses of LLMs

                    Valid Inference with Model Labels: Prediction-Powered Inference

                    Slide 49 in the PDF
                    • Data: \(n\) texts labelled by humans (\(X_j\), \(Y_j\)) and \(N\) unlabelled texts \(\tilde X_i\)
                      • Independent random samples of one population
                    • Target: \(\theta = E[Y]\), the population mean of the human label
                      • \(f(\cdot)\) is the score of a fixed model, trained on other data
                    • PPI (prediction-powered inference, Angelopoulos et al. (2023)): \(\hat\theta_{\text{PPI}} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} f(\tilde X_i) - \frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n} \bigl(f(X_j) - Y_j\bigr)\)
                      • First term: the mean prediction over the \(N\) unlabelled texts
                      • Second term: the mean bias \(f - Y\), estimated on the labelled texts and subtracted
                    • \(\widehat{\text{Var}}(\hat\theta_{\text{PPI}}) = \widehat{\text{Var}}(f)/N + \widehat{\text{Var}}(f - Y)/n\)
                      • An asymptotically valid CI under this sampling design, whatever the quality of \(f\)

                          Method: Robust MNIR and What the Course Replication Changes

                          Slide 59 in the PDF
                          • MNIR (multinomial inverse regression, Taddy (2013), Appendix): the count of word \(j\) in text \(i\) is Poisson, with mean \(m_i e^{\alpha_j+\varphi_j v_i}\)
                            • \(m_i\): the number of words of the text; \(v_i\): the standardised return
                          • The robust step: MNIR on 500 subsamples of 5 000 texts each, drawn without replacement
                            • \(D^+_j\): the share of subsamples with \(\varphi_j > 0\) minus the share with \(\varphi_j < 0\)
                            • Positive list: \(D^+_j \ge 80\%\); negative list: \(D^+_j \le -80\%\)
                          • The score of a text: the number of list words divided by the number of words, scaled to unit standard deviation
                          • The course replication, on the chapter's data:
                            • The text: all FNSPID headlines of one stock on one day, 18 282 stock-days
                            • Training: Oct 2009 – Dec 2019, without the texts whose return window reaches into 2020 (10 286 texts); test: 2020–2023 (7 980 texts)
                            • Vocabulary (15 376 words) and winsorization cut-offs: from the training texts only
                            • The sign of \(\varphi_j\): a score-test approximation of the AIC selection (Akaike information criterion, \(z^2 > 2\)), instead of the gamma-lasso penalty

                          The Family of Tests: FWER and FDR

                          Slide 62 in the PDF
                          • Formally: \(H_{0,j}: E[r^{(j)}_t] = 0\) for candidate strategies \(j = 1, \dots, M\)
                            • Control the FWER (Holm) or the FDR (Benjamini & Hochberg (1995))
                            • FDR: expected share of false rejections among all rejections
                            • BH at level \(q\): reject the hypotheses with \(p_{(1)}, \dots, p_{(k^*)}\), where \(k^* = \max\{k : p_{(k)} \le k q / M\}\)
                            • \(M\) counts every variant the assistant proposed, including those discarded; if the hypotheses were built on the test outcomes, the \(p\)-values need untouched test data or selective (sequential) inference

                          The Discovery Loop and What the Researcher Must Check

                          Slide 63 in the PDF
                          • Literature, hypotheses, data and code, robustness fixed in advance, critique, report
                            • An assistant (Claude, ChatGPT, Gemini, GitHub Copilot) drafts each step
                            • The researcher verifies each one (reusable prompts: Appendix)
                          • Memorisation: the model knows what followed the headline
                            • Test: news after the release of the weights; anonymised text as a diagnostic (Glasserman & Lin (2023)); the memory test of this chapter
                          • Forking paths: prompt, threshold, horizon tuned on the test period
                            • Test: a pre-registered grid and a stated error rate: Holm (FWER) or BH (FDR, under independence or positive dependence); \(|t| > 3\) (Harvey, Liu & Zhu (2016)) is a heuristic, not an error-rate guarantee
                          • HARKing (hypothesising after the results are known)
                            • A plausible mechanism written after the result, with references that may not exist
                            • Test: state the mechanism and its falsifier before the test; resolve every DOI (digital object identifier)
                          • Timing and agents that run code

                            • Test: first tradable return on \(d+2\); read-only data, logged tool calls, the diff of every script before a result is accepted

                            Agreement beyond Chance: Cohen's \(\kappa\)

                            Slide 73 in the PDF
                            • Accuracy also counts agreement reached by chance, which is large when one class dominates

                            Calibration of Probabilities: ECE and Temperature Scaling

                            Slide 76 in the PDF
                            • The question: when the model gives a class probability 0.9, is it right 90% of the time?

                              • \(n_b\) and \(n\): the number of headlines in bin \(b\) and the total number of headlines
                              • \(\text{conf}_b\): the mean top probability in bin \(b\)
                              • \(\text{acc}_b\): the share of headlines classified correctly in bin \(b\)
                            • Temperature scaling (Guo et al. (2017)): \(p_k(T) \propto p_k^{1/T}\), i.e. logits divided by \(T\)
                              • \(T\) fitted by maximum likelihood on one random half of the Twitter validation set
                              • ECE measured on the other half
                              • The saved probabilities are rounded to 6 decimals; zeros are replaced numerically by \(10^{-12}\)
                              • The most likely class does not change, hence neither does accuracy

                            When Does the Correction Overshoot? Headlines versus Stock-Days

                            Slide 87 in the PDF
                            • The correction \(b/\rho\) rests on three assumptions
                              • Non-differential error: given the true tone, the score error does not predict returns
                              • The Twitter correlation carries over to FNSPID headlines
                              • The same unit: \(\rho\) is measured per headline, but \(b\) uses the daily score of a stock, the mean of on average 5.8 headlines
                            • If headline errors are independent and the headlines of a stock-day share one true tone:
                              • Averaging raises the correlation with the truth
                              • The daily \(\rho\) exceeds the headline \(\rho\), and \(b/\rho\) over-corrects
                            • Numerical example: one headline has reliability \(\lambda = \rho^2 = 0.25\) (\(\rho = 0.50\))
                              • The mean of \(k = 4\) such headlines has \(\lambda_k = \dfrac{k\lambda}{1 + (k-1)\lambda} = \dfrac{4(0.25)}{1 + 3(0.25)} = 0.57\) (Spearman–Brown)
                              • So \(\rho_k = 0.76\)
                              • A daily slope of 10 bp is then corrected to \(10/0.50 = 20\) bp with the headline \(\rho\), but to \(10/0.76 \approx 13\) bp with the daily one
                            • With independent headline errors our \(b/\rho\) values are upper bounds
                              • The conclusion about the LM slope depends on this
                            • Remedy: labelling every headline of a random sample of stock-days measures \(\rho\) at the right level

                            When Annotators Disagree: Agreement and Latent Labels

                            Slide 92 in the PDF
                            • Fleiss' \(\kappa\) (Fleiss (1971)): \(N\) texts, \(m\) raters, \(n_{ij}\) raters giving text \(i\) label \(j\)
                              • \(\bar P = \frac{1}{N}\sum_i \sum_j \frac{n_{ij}(n_{ij} - 1)}{m(m - 1)}\), \(\bar P_e = \sum_j p_j^2\), \(\kappa = \dfrac{\bar P - \bar P_e}{1 - \bar P_e}\); \(p_j\): share of all votes for \(j\)
                              • The formula assumes the same \(m\) for every text; PhraseBank has 5–8 raters per sentence and publishes only agreement levels, not the votes: \(\kappa\) cannot be computed from the release
                            • Dawid–Skene model (Dawid & Skene (1979)): rater \(a\) has a confusion matrix
                              • \(\pi^{(a)}_{jl} = P(\text{says } l \mid \text{true } j)\)
                              • The true class is latent
                              • EM (expectation–maximisation) estimates the \(\pi^{(a)}\) and gives each text a soft label \(P(\text{true} = j \mid \text{votes})\)
                            • Evaluate models by a proper scoring rule (log loss) against soft labels
                              • Not by accuracy against the majority label
                              • The ceiling in the agreement chart is a label-noise ceiling, not a model failure

                            Text Regressions with Statistical Theory

                            Did the Memory Vanish? A Formal Test and Its Power

                            Slide 97 in the PDF
                            • \(H_0: \text{AUC}_{\text{before}} = \text{AUC}_{\text{after}}\), two independent samples
                              • DeLong variance for each AUC (DeLong et al. (1988))
                              • \(z = (\widehat{\text{AUC}}_1 - \widehat{\text{AUC}}_2)/\sqrt{\hat V_1 + \hat V_2}\): 14B \(z\) = 0.73 (\(p\) = 0.47), 7B \(z\) = 1.01 (\(p\) = 0.31)
                            • Under \(H_0\) (AUC \(= 0.5\), no ties): \(SE_0 = \sqrt{\dfrac{n_1 + n_0 + 1}{12\, n_1 n_0}}\)
                              • \(n_1\), \(n_0\): the numbers of up and down months
                              • After release: \(n_1\) = 13, \(n_0\) = 10, so \(SE_0 = \sqrt{24/1\,560}\) = 0.124
                            • Smallest detectable drop (5% two-sided test, 80% power):
                              • \(2.80\sqrt{SE_{\text{before}}^2 + SE_0^2}\) = \(2.80\sqrt{0.03227^2 + 0.124^2}\) = 0.36
                              • \(2.80 = 1.96 + 0.84\), the normal quantiles for the test and for the power; \(SE_{\text{before}}\): DeLong standard error of the 296 pre-release months
                            • A fall from 0.69 to 0.5 (0.19) is below this threshold
                              • Not rejecting \(H_0\) does not show that the memory survives release
                            • Months needed: the smallest \(m\) for which the detectable drop falls to 0.19
                              • With the post-release share of up months kept at 13/23: \(m\) = 101 (7B: 129)

                            Discrete Labels: Misclassification Is Not Classical Error

                            Slide 98 in the PDF
                            • Binary "negative news" dummy \(s^* \in \{0, 1\}\), predicted label \(\hat s\)
                              • \(\alpha_0 = P(\hat s = 1 \mid s^* = 0)\), \(\alpha_1 = P(\hat s = 0 \mid s^* = 1)\)
                              • The error \(u = \hat s - s^*\) is negatively correlated with \(s^*\): the classical formula does not apply (Aigner (1973))
                              • \(\operatorname{plim} \hat b = \beta\,\dfrac{\pi(1 - \pi)(1 - \alpha_0 - \alpha_1)}{p(1 - p)}\), \(\pi = P(s^* = 1)\), \(p = P(\hat s = 1)\)
                            • Twitter validation set, \(\pi\) = 14.5%, from the confusion matrices
                              • FinBERT: \(\alpha_0\) = 13.3%, \(\alpha_1\) = 23.9%, factor 0.45; Qwen2.5-7B: 0.59; LM (\(\alpha_1\) = 50.4%): 0.28
                            • As long as \(\alpha_0 + \alpha_1 < 1\) the sign is kept, but a dummy slope can shrink to a third or less

                            Two Noisy Measures: Factor Model and Instruments

                            Slide 99 in the PDF
                            • One factor: \(z_j = \ell_j f + u_j\), \(\text{Var}(f) = 1\)
                              • \(u_j\) uncorrelated with \(f\) and with each other
                              • Then \(\rho_{jk} = \ell_j\ell_k\), so \(\ell_j^2 = \rho_{jk}\rho_{jl}/\rho_{kl}\)
                              • Daily FNSPID scores: \(\ell_j\) = 0.53 (LM), 0.85 (FinBERT), 0.78 (Qwen)
                            • IV (instrumental variables): with \(r = a + \beta f + \varepsilon\), instrument \(z_Q\) with \(z_F\)
                              • \(\hat b_{IV} = \widehat{\text{Cov}}(r, z_F)/\widehat{\text{Cov}}(z_Q, z_F) \to \beta/\ell_Q\)
                              • If \(\ell_F \neq 0\), \(f\) is uncorrelated with \(\varepsilon\), and \(u_F\) is uncorrelated with \(u_Q\) and \(\varepsilon\)
                              • Day \(d\), Qwen: 59 bp [53; 66] with FinBERT as instrument, 37 bp [28; 46] with LM (bootstrap over days)
                            • If both instruments were valid the two estimates would coincide
                              • Difference 22 bp, \(z\) = 5.4: rejected
                              • The test rejects the joint restrictions, not a named instrument: FinBERT–Qwen errors correlated through overlapping training text are one plausible cause, an LM error related to returns another; the factor loadings \(\ell_j\) are then suspect too

                            Short Answers to the Check Questions (2/2)

                            Slide 106 in the PDF
                            • Release date: before it, the evaluated returns may already be memorised in the weights
                              • News and returns after the weight release could not have been memorised in them
                              • Condition: no tool access to future information
                              • The other sources of look-ahead bias in the backtest still need to be controlled
                            • No-context memory test: it can flag memorisation
                              • Qwen2.5-14B: AUC 0.69 on the months before the release
                              • A non-significant post-release AUC (0.59 on 23 months) does not establish the absence of contamination
                            • Score as a measurement: \(\hat s = s^* + u\) estimates the true tone
                              • Even a classical, zero-mean error can attenuate the slope (in the classical model, slope on \(z\) = effect \(\times\ \rho\))
                              • A random human-labelled sample allows the mean bias \(f - Y\) and the reliability of the score to be estimated
                              • The estimates hold for the unit of analysis at which the sample was measured
                              • PPI corrects the mean score using the mean difference \(f - Y\)
                              • The \(b/\rho\) correction requires the model assumptions and a \(\rho\) measured at the same unit of aggregation as the regression
                              • A \(\rho\) measured on headlines does not automatically justify correcting a slope on daily averages
