Opening: When Does an Empirical Result Deserve Trust?

Slide 1 in the PDF
Albert J. Menkveld. Photo: Me Judice (2015); CC BY 3.0; Wikimedia Commons
“In science, evidence is generated to test hypotheses in an evidence-generating process (EGP). We claim that EGP variation across researchers adds uncertainty—nonstandard errors (NSEs).” — Menkveld et al. (2024), Nonstandard errors

\(\blacktriangleright\) Central result: “A Mini-Multiverse on Course Data: 27 Paths per Index”

The chapter asks when an empirical result about markets deserves trust, and when it only reflects the data, the design or luck. In the experiment of Menkveld et al. (2024), 164 research teams test the same six hypotheses on the same data: all trades in Euro Stoxx 50 index futures from 2002–2018, 720 million records. For market efficiency, the median estimate is −1.1% per year and the interquartile range (IQR) of the estimates across teams is 6.7 percentage points (pp). The median standard error (SE) of 2.5% implies a Gaussian IQR of 3.4%, half the 6.7 pp: the dispersion across teams is twice the one that sampling alone would produce.

The same phenomenon appears on course data. A mini-multiverse combines three decisions, the treatment of outliers, the inefficiency measure and the model, into 27 specifications per index, applied to daily closes from 2002–2018. The IQR of the estimated annual trend is 64.6 pp for the Euro Stoxx 50 and 82.6 pp for the BET, the Bucharest Stock Exchange index, above the benchmark due to sampling alone, 41.3 pp and 43.4 pp, respectively. The choice of model drives the dispersion: for the nine specifications with a linear trend, the IQR is only 2.6 pp and 6.7 pp, respectively.

The first part of the chapter reviews Chapters 0–18 through six pitfalls and an integrated case study, from raw BET prices to a backtested 1% Value-at-Risk (VaR). Over the 5 432 evaluation days, the Normal model is breached on 2.25% of days, more than twice as often as it should, and only filtered historical simulation (FHS), at 1.23%, lies in the 95% binomial range [0.74%, 1.27%]. The second part prepares the team project: replication, pre-registration, the specification grid and the declared use of AI.

Five Questions for Every Model

Slide 6 in the PDF
Before estimating
  • Data: which series, which calendar, which adjustments (dividends, splits, holidays)?
  • Model: which stylised fact does it capture?
  • Model: which does it ignore?
  • Estimation: on which window, with which information at time \(t\)?
After estimating
  • Evaluation: out of sample, with a loss that matches the target?
    • QLIKE (the quasi-likelihood loss) for variance
    • FZ0 (the Fissler–Ziegel score) for a (VaR, ES) pair
  • Uncertainty: robust standard errors, bootstrap, a test against a simple benchmark?

Chapter 0: Financial Markets in 2026

Slide 7 in the PDF
  • Markets combine exchanges, over-the-counter dealers, funds, algorithms and blockchains
    • exchange-traded funds (ETFs)
  • SPY, 2015–2026: CAGR 13.7%, annualised volatility 17.6%
    • SPY: S&P 500 ETF, dividends reinvested
    • CAGR: compound annual growth rate
  • Bitcoin, same period: CAGR 60.6%, annualised volatility 66.7%
  • BET, July 2007 to February 2009: a drawdown of \(-82.5\%\)
  • Standard errors of the mean and the volatility from i.i.d. log returns
    • \(Y\): sample length in years; \(T\): number of daily returns; \(K\): population kurtosis
    \begin{equation*} \mathrm{SE}(\hat\mu_{\text{ann}}) = \frac{\sigma_{\text{ann}}}{\sqrt{Y}}, \qquad \mathrm{SE}(\hat\sigma) \approx \sigma\sqrt{\frac{K - 1}{4T}} \end{equation*}
    • \(\mathrm{SE}(\hat\sigma)\) requires a finite fourth moment, which Chapter 1 questions
  • SPY: \(\mathrm{SE}(\hat\mu_{\text{ann}}) \approx 5.1\%\) for an annualised mean log return of \(12.8\%\)
    • means are barely estimable, volatilities are

Chapter 1: Stylised Facts and Returns

Slide 8 in the PDF
  • Returns are uncorrelated but not independent Cont (2001)
    • their magnitude is predictable, their sign largely is not
  • Tail index \(\alpha\): the probability of a loss above \(x\) decays like \(x^{-\alpha}\)
  • The Hill estimator uses the \(k\) largest losses, ordered \(L_{(1)} \ge \dots \ge L_{(k+1)}\) Hill (1975)
    \begin{equation*} \hat\alpha = \Big[\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\ln\frac{L_{(i)}}{L_{(k+1)}}\Big]^{-1}, \qquad \mathrm{SE}(\hat\alpha) \approx \frac{\hat\alpha}{\sqrt{k}} \ \text{for i.i.d.\ losses} \end{equation*}
  • BET, \(k = 153\): \(\hat\alpha = 2.39\), block-bootstrap 95% CI \([2.12, 3.03]\)
    • CI: confidence interval
  • The i.i.d. interval \(\hat\alpha \pm 1.96\,\hat\alpha/\sqrt{k} = [2.01, 2.77]\) is narrower
    • large losses come in clusters
  • \(\alpha < 4\): the fourth moment is likely infinite
  • Days beyond \(5\sigma\) are more than 1000 times more frequent than under the Normal distribution
    • the Normal distribution gives \(2\Phi(-5) \approx 5.7 \times 10^{-7}\) a day
    • that is, one such day in about 7 000 years

Chapter 2: Market Efficiency

Slide 9 in the PDF
  • The efficient market hypothesis: prices fully reflect the information set
  • A test of abnormal-return predictability is a joint test Fama (1970)
    • of efficiency and of the expected-return model
  • The \(q\)-period variance ratio and its heteroskedasticity-robust statistic Lo & MacKinlay (1988)
    \begin{equation*} VR(q) = \frac{\Var(r_t + \dots + r_{t-q+1})}{q\,\Var(r_t)}, \qquad z^*(q) = \frac{\widehat{VR}(q) - 1}{\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}^{\,*}} \Rightarrow N(0, 1) \end{equation*}
    • \(z^*(q)\) requires a finite fourth moment, which Chapter 1 questions
  • Several horizons at once: \(\max_q |z^*(q)|\) with the Chow–Denning critical values Chow & Denning (1993)
  • Share of rolling windows that reject the random walk
    • S&P 500 6.7%, BET 22.4%, Bitcoin 0.0%
  • Statistical rejection is not exploitable profit

Chapter 3: Factor Models and Asset Pricing

Slide 10 in the PDF
  • Expected returns should reward only exposure to systematic factors
  • The GRS test that all intercepts (pricing errors) \(\hat\alpha\) are zero Gibbons, Ross & Shanken (1989)
    • \(T\) periods, \(N\) assets and \(K\) factors
    \begin{equation*} \mathrm{GRS} = \frac{T - N - K}{N}\,\frac{\hat\alpha^\top\hat\Sigma^{-1}\hat\alpha}{1 + \hat\mu_f^\top\hat\Omega^{-1}\hat\mu_f} \;\sim\; F(N, T - N - K) \end{equation*}
    • \(\hat\mu_f\): factor means; \(\hat\Sigma\), \(\hat\Omega\): residual and factor covariances
    • the \(F\) is exact only under i.i.d. Normal errors
  • 25 size and book-to-market portfolios
    • SML (security market line) slope \(-4.4\%\)
  • GRS \(= 4.18\), against a 5% critical value below 1.6
    • the CAPM (capital asset pricing model) is rejected (\(p < 0.01\))
  • Sector ETFs: the first principal component (the market) explains 66% of the variance

Chapter 4: Portfolio Optimisation

Slide 11 in the PDF
  • Markowitz asks the right question, but estimation error dominates the answer Markowitz (1952)
  • Standard error of the per-period Sharpe ratio, i.i.d. Normal returns over \(T\) periods Lo (2002)
    \begin{equation*} \mathrm{SE}(\widehat{SR}) \approx \sqrt{(1 + \widehat{SR}^2/2)/T} \end{equation*}
    • \(\mathrm{SE}(\widehat{SR})\) requires a finite fourth moment, which Chapter 1 questions
  • 60-month samples: median in-sample Sharpe 1.56
    • median true Sharpe only 0.30
  • 96% of the estimated tangency portfolios are truly worse than 1/N DeMiguel, Garlappi & Uppal (2009)
    • 1/N is a hard benchmark
  • Portfolio A against 1/N: test \(SR_A - SR_B = 0\) with HAC or studentised bootstrap inference Ledoit & Wolf (2008)
    • not by comparing two point estimates

Chapter 5: Conditional Volatility: GARCH

Slide 12 in the PDF
  • Volatility clusters, so today' s shock predicts tomorrow' s variance Engle (1982), Bollerslev (1986)
  • The GARCH(1,1) model and its \(h\)-day forecast
    • \(\varepsilon_t\): the shock; \(\alpha\), \(\beta\): the weights of the last shock and of the last variance
    \begin{gather*} \sigma_{t+1}^2 = \omega + \alpha\,\varepsilon_t^2 + \beta\,\sigma_t^2, \qquad \bar\sigma^2 = \frac{\omega}{1 - \alpha - \beta} \\ \sigma_{T+h|T}^2 = \bar\sigma^2 + (\alpha + \beta)^{h-1}\big(\sigma_{T+1|T}^2 - \bar\sigma^2\big) \end{gather*}
  • The persistence \(\alpha + \beta\) is the rate at which the forecast returns to the long-run level \(\bar\sigma^2\)
    • the half-life is \(h_{1/2} = \ln 0.5/\ln(\alpha + \beta)\)
  • S&P 500, 2000–2026: \(\alpha + \beta = 0.9948\), so \(h_{1/2} = 132\) days
  • The BET persistence cannot be bounded away from 1
    • pitfall “Persistence Near 1”

Chapter 6: Multivariate Volatility and Dependence

Slide 13 in the PDF
  • Correlations move with the macro regime
    • diversification is not guaranteed
  • Crisis correlation rises mechanically with volatility, so test on the adjusted correlation Forbes & Rigobon (2002)
    \begin{equation*} \rho^* = \frac{\rho}{\sqrt{1 + \delta(1 - \rho^2)}} \end{equation*}
    • \(\rho\): crisis correlation
    • \(\delta\): relative rise in the variance of the source market from calm to crisis
    • the test on \(\rho^*\) requires a finite fourth moment, which Chapter 1 questions
  • SPY–TLT: \(\rho\) rose from \(-0.40\) to \(0.11\) after 2022
    • TLT: long US Treasury bond ETF
  • Over the same time a 60/40 portfolio went from 10.4% to 13.2% volatility
  • Bitcoin–SPY DCC correlation: 0.04 before 2020, 0.31 since

Chapter 7: Value-at-Risk and Expected Shortfall

Slide 14 in the PDF
  • VaR is a quantile of the loss
  • ES, the average loss beyond it, is coherent
  • Definitions, with \(q_\alpha\) the \(\alpha\)-quantile of the returns \(r\) (continuous distribution)
    \begin{equation*} \VaR_\alpha = -q_\alpha, \qquad \ES_\alpha = -\E\big[r \mid r \le q_\alpha\big] = \frac{1}{\alpha}\int_0^\alpha \VaR_u\,du \end{equation*}
  • S&P 500, daily: historical \(\VaR_{1\%} = 3.45\%\), \(\ES_{1\%} = 5.03\%\)
  • The Normal distribution gives only \(2.80\%\)
    • heavy tails matter
  • Filtered historical simulation and conditional EVT react to volatility within days McNeil & Frey (2000)

Chapter 8: Backtesting and Evaluating Risk Forecasts

Slide 15 in the PDF
  • Test the number and the timing of breaches: coverage and independence Kupiec (1995), Christoffersen (1998)
  • Kupiec, with \(x\) breaches in \(T\) days and the observed rate \(\hat\pi = x/T\)
    \begin{equation*} LR_{uc} = -2\ln\frac{(1-\alpha)^{T-x}\alpha^{x}}{(1-\hat\pi)^{T-x}\hat\pi^{x}} \;\sim\; \chi^2_1 \end{equation*}
  • Christoffersen: \(LR_{ind} \sim \chi^2_1\) compares the breach rate after a quiet day with the rate after a breach
    • formula: Step 4 of the case study
  • S&P 500, VaR 1%, 2007–2026: breach rate (target 1%)
    • Normal 2.70%
    • HS (historical simulation) 1.67%
    • FHS (filtered historical simulation) 1.23%
    • GARCH-EVT (GARCH filter with a tail from extreme value theory) 1.15%
  • On the S&P 500 and the BET, a GARCH filter plus a flexible tail wins
    • FHS, GARCH-EVT

Chapter 9: Realised Volatility

Slide 16 in the PDF
  • Intraday prices measure daily variance (\(RV\), realised variance)
  • The log-HAR (heterogeneous autoregressive) model approximates its observed persistence over finite horizons Corsi (2009)
    \begin{equation*} \ln RV_{t+1} = b_0 + b_d \ln RV_t + b_w\,\overline{\ln RV}^{(5)}_t + b_m\,\overline{\ln RV}^{(22)}_t + u_{t+1} \end{equation*}
    • \(\overline{\ln RV}^{(5)}_t\), \(\overline{\ln RV}^{(22)}_t\): the mean of \(\ln RV\) over the last 5 and 22 days
    • a stationary HAR is a finite AR: short memory asymptotically
  • SPY: kurtosis 15.73 for open-to-close returns
    • 2.66 after dividing by \(\sqrt{RV_t}\)
  • log-HAR beats GARCH(1,1)-\(t\) in QLIKE
    • by 10% for SPY (\(p = 0.021\))
    • by 22% for Bitcoin
    • the DM test on QLIKE requires finite moments and weak dependence of the loss differential \(d_t\), not of the returns

Chapter 10: Market Microstructure

Slide 17 in the PDF
  • Prices form in limit order books
  • The spread pays for processing, inventory and adverse selection
  • The Roll spread: the bid–ask bounce
    • makes consecutive changes of the transaction price, \(\Delta p_t\), negatively correlated
    \begin{equation*} s_{\text{Roll}} = 2\sqrt{-\Cov(\Delta p_t, \Delta p_{t-1})} \end{equation*}
  • When the sample covariance is positive the estimator is undefined
    • report how often
  • Amihud ratio (basis points, bp, per 1 million US dollars, USD)
    • SPY 0.0016, Banca Transilvania 24.7, Transelectrica 1024.0
  • SPY: the opening 5-minute bar moves 2.06 times as much as a midday bar

Chapter 11: Continuous-Time Models

Slide 18 in the PDF
  • Brownian motion is the limit of random walks with ever smaller steps
  • Itô' s lemma solves the stochastic differential equations of prices
  • Normality of the log returns of geometric Brownian motion (GBM)
    • the Jarque–Bera test, with \(S\) the sample skewness and \(K\) the sample kurtosis
    \begin{equation*} JB = \frac{T}{6}\Big(S^2 + \frac{(K - 3)^2}{4}\Big) \;\sim\; \chi^2_2 \end{equation*}
  • S&P 500 against GBM: 62 days beyond \(\pm 4\) standard deviations, against 0.59 expected
  • The Hill index of S&P 500 losses is below 4 (Chapter 16)
    • the fourth moment is likely infinite
  • Jumps: \((RV - BV)\) standardised \(\Rightarrow N(0, 1)\) Barndorff-Nielsen & Shephard (2006)
    • \(BV\): the bipower variation, robust to jumps

Chapter 12: Options and the Volatility Surface

Slide 19 in the PDF
  • Black–Scholes prices options by hedging Black & Scholes (1973)
    • implied volatility: the value of \(\sigma\) extracted from option prices, a risk-neutral quantity, not a forecast of physical volatility
  • The (ex-ante) variance risk premium: \(E_t^Q[RV_{t,t+21}] - E_t^P[RV_{t,t+21}]\)
    • \(VIX_t^2\) estimates the first term, the risk-neutral expected variance over the next month; the second needs a forecast
    • \(RV_{t,t+21}\): realised variance over the next 21 trading days
  • The ex-post realised gap, known only at \(t + 21\):
    \begin{equation*} VIX_t^2 - RV_{t,t+21} \end{equation*}
  • Consecutive values overlap, so the mean needs Newey–West standard errors with at least 21 lags
    • the standard error of the mean requires a finite fourth moment, which Chapter 1 questions
  • 1990–2026: mean VIX 19.4
    • mean realised volatility over the next month 15.3
  • The ex-post realised gap is positive on 86% of days, but not in crashes
    • its sign does not identify the sign of the ex-ante premium

Chapter 17: Bubbles and Crashes

Slide 24 in the PDF
  • A bubble is a price path that grows faster than fundamentals can justify
  • GSADF: the largest right-tailed ADF (augmented Dickey–Fuller) statistic Phillips, Shi & Yu (2015)
    • over all sample fractions \([r_1, r_2]\) of length at least \(r_0\)
    \begin{equation*} GSADF = \sup_{r_2 \in [r_0, 1]}\,\sup_{r_1 \in [0, r_2 - r_0]} ADF_{r_1}^{r_2} \end{equation*}
  • Volatility shifts distort the size
    • use wild-bootstrap critical values
  • Bitcoin 2014–2026 (weekly): GSADF \(3.85\) against a 95% wild-bootstrap critical value of \(2.82\) (\(2.27\) Monte Carlo)
    • explosive episodes are detected
  • One vs two Markov regimes for Bitcoin: \(LR = 123.2\)
    • against a parametric-bootstrap 95% critical value of \(11.8\) (\(p = 0.005\))

Chapter 18: Systemic Risk and Networks

Slide 25 in the PDF
  • Systemic risk: losses of one institution that spread to the whole system
  • \(\Delta\)CoVaR: the change in the system' s VaR when institution \(i\) moves from its median to distress
    • \(X^i\): the return of institution \(i\)
    \begin{equation*} \Delta\text{CoVaR}^{i} = \text{CoVaR}_{\alpha}^{\,sys \mid X^i = -\VaR_\alpha^i} - \text{CoVaR}_{\alpha}^{\,sys \mid X^i = \text{median}} \end{equation*}
  • \(\Delta\)CoVaR 1% of eleven banks: 4 of 25 within-region pairs differ at 5%
    • 0 after the Holm correction
  • Overlap of intervals alone would have ranked none
  • LRMES, simulated from GJR–GARCH and DCC Brownlees & Engle (2017)
    • LRMES: the expected loss of a bank when the market falls by more than \(10\%\) over 22 days
    • JPMorgan \(10.6\%\) today, \(23.7\%\) with the information of 31 March 2020

Six Pitfalls

Pitfalls of Design: Look-Ahead Bias and Overfitting

Slide 26 in the PDF
  • Look-ahead bias: a full-sample estimate leaks the future
    • any parameter, threshold or scaling estimated on the full sample
    • Examples: full-sample GARCH parameters or standardisation, labels that overlap the test set (Chapter 13; the effect on the BET: case study)
    • Remedy: rolling or expanding windows, purging and embargo, the information set \(\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\) written next to every forecast

Pitfall of Inference: Heavy Tails Ignored

Slide 30 in the PDF
  • Classical standard errors assume independence and finite moments of the quantity analysed
    • variances, autocorrelations and Sharpe ratios: the fourth moment of returns; DM: the moments of the loss differential; Gaussian QMLE: the fourth moment of the standardised innovations
    • BET: \(\hat\alpha = 2.39\), 95% CI \([2.12, 3.03]\) (Chapter 1): under a Pareto-type tail, consistent with an infinite kurtosis, not a proof

  • Remedy: HAC errors, block bootstrap, intervals that respect the parameter boundary
    • HAC: heteroskedasticity and autocorrelation consistent
    • block bootstrap where it is valid
  • The Newey–West HAC standard error of a mean \(\bar d\) Newey & West (1987): \(\mathrm{SE}(\bar d) = \sqrt{\hat\Omega/T}\), with
    \begin{equation*} \hat\Omega = \hat\gamma_0 + 2\sum_{j=1}^{L}\Big(1 - \frac{j}{L + 1}\Big)\hat\gamma_j \end{equation*}

Persistence Near 1: Which Boundary? (1/2)

Slide 31 in the PDF
  • \(p = \alpha + \beta \le 1\) is a restriction we choose, not a property of GARCH
    • its interior gives a finite unconditional variance and a finite half-life
    • equality is the IGARCH (integrated GARCH) boundary
  • Without it, \(p\) slightly above 1 is admissible Nelson (1990)
    • the process can still be strictly stationary, when \(E\ln(\alpha z_t^2 + \beta) < 0\)
    • but it has no finite variance and no half-life
  • BET: \(\hat p = 0.9907\) is inside the region
    • the difficulty is the true \(p\), which may equal 1
  • At \(p = 1\) the estimator is not approximately Normal Andrews (1999)
    • so the Wald interval [0.974, 1.007] is uninformative under our restriction, rather than “wrong”

Persistence Near 1: Which Boundary? (2/2)

Slide 32 in the PDF
  • The likelihood-ratio test of \(H_0\colon p = 1\) has the boundary distribution \(\tfrac12\chi^2_0 + \tfrac12\chi^2_1\)
    • the 5% critical value is 2.71 instead of 3.84
    • BET: \(LR = 1.01\), \(p\)-value 0.16, so IGARCH is not rejected
  • Profile-likelihood CI within \(p \le 1\): \([0.975, 1]\)

  • Boundary critical values apply only when the true parameter can sit on the edge of the space you imposed
    • not whenever an estimate is close to some bound

          The Power of a Backtest: Definition

          Slide 44 in the PDF
          • The question: if the VaR 1% model is wrong, how often does the Kupiec test notice?
          • Power at the true breach rate \(\pi\): an exact binomial probability
            • the probability of the rejection region \(R_T\) of the 5% test
            \begin{equation*} \text{Power}(\pi) = \sum_{x \in R_T}\binom{T}{x}\pi^{x}(1 - \pi)^{T - x}, \qquad R_T = \{x : LR_{uc}(x) > 3.84\} \end{equation*}
            • At \(\pi = 1\%\) the same sum gives the actual size of the nominal 5% test: 9.48% at \(T = 250\)
            • At \(T = 250\), \(R_T\) holds 0 breaches and any number from 7 up
          • Minimum detectable effect (MDE): the smallest true rate \(\pi > 1\%\) with \(\text{Power}(\pi) \ge 80\%\)
          • Power grows with \(T\)
            • the standard error of the observed rate \(\hat\pi\) is \(\sqrt{\pi(1 - \pi)/T}\)

                  How Much Do Researchers' Choices Matter? (1/2)

                  Slide 64 in the PDF

                    • Finance Crowd Analysis Project, #fincap
                  • Standard error
                    • Uncertainty from sampling, the data-generating process
                    • Nonstandard error: uncertainty from the analysis path each researcher picks, the evidence-generating process
                    • Model: team \(j\) reports \(X_j = e_j + \varepsilon_j\), \(e_j\) the researcher fixed effect of its path, \(\varepsilon_j\) the sampling error

                    • The IQR, not the standard deviation, because the \(e_j\) can have fat tails

                    Step 2: the GARCH(1,1)-\(t\) Model

                    Slide 83 in the PDF
                    • The GARCH(1,1) of the Chapter 5 review, with Student-\(t\) innovations Bollerslev (1986)
                      \begin{equation*} r_t = \mu + \varepsilon_t, \qquad \varepsilon_t = \sigma_t z_t, \qquad \sigma_t^2 = \omega + \alpha\,\varepsilon_{t-1}^2 + \beta\,\sigma_{t-1}^2 \end{equation*}
                      • \(\sigma_t\): conditional volatility, known at \(t - 1\)
                      • \(z_t\): i.i.d. Student-\(t\) with \(\nu\) degrees of freedom, scaled to variance 1
                      • \(\omega > 0\), \(\alpha, \beta \ge 0\): \(\alpha\) measures the reaction to news, \(\beta\) the memory
                      • a small \(\nu\) means heavy tails
                    • Persistence \(\alpha + \beta\) and half-life \(h_{1/2}\): the formulas of the Chapter 5 review
                    • Estimated by maximum likelihood, with robust (sandwich) standard errors

                      • \(\hat\sigma_{T+1}\) also uses the last return \(r_T = 2.17\%\) and is the input of tomorrow' s VaR
                      \begin{equation*} \hat\sigma_{T+1}^2 = \hat\omega + \hat\alpha\,(r_T - \hat\mu)^2 + \hat\beta\,\hat\sigma_T^2 = 0.0448 + 0.192\,(2.17 - 0.08)^2 + 0.799 \times 1.67^2 = 3.11 \end{equation*}
                      • So \(\hat\sigma_{T+1} = \sqrt{3.11} = 1.76\%\): a large last return lifts the forecast above \(\hat\sigma_T = 1.67\%\)
                    • Long-run variance implied by the model
                      \begin{equation*} \bar\sigma^2 = \frac{\omega}{1 - \alpha - \beta} = \frac{0.0448}{0.0093} = 4.82 \end{equation*}
                      • Returns are in %: \(\bar\sigma = 2.20\%\) a day (34.7% a year), against a sample standard deviation of 1.40% a day (22.1% a year)

                    Step 2: Today' s Volatility and the Long-Run Level (2/2)

                    Slide 85 in the PDF

                    Step 3: Four VaR 1% Forecasts (1/2)

                    Slide 86 in the PDF
                    • Unconditional, on the last 500 days: one quantile for the whole window
                      • HS (historical simulation): the loss \(L = -r\) exceeded on 1% of the last 500 days (empirical quantile)
                      • Normal: \(\VaR = -(\hat\mu + \hat\sigma z_{0.01})\)
                      • \(\hat\mu\), \(\hat\sigma\): sample mean and standard deviation; \(z_{0.01} = -2.326\): the 1% quantile of the standard Normal distribution
                    • Conditional, GARCH-\(t\) on the last 1000 days
                      • the quantile scales with today' s volatility forecast \(\hat\sigma_t\)
                      \begin{equation*} \textbf{GARCH-}t\colon\; \VaR_{t} = -\Big(\hat\mu + \hat\sigma_t\,t_\nu^{-1}(0.01)\sqrt{(\nu - 2)/\nu}\Big), \qquad \textbf{FHS}\colon\; \VaR_{t} = -\big(\hat\mu + \hat\sigma_t\,\hat q_{0.01}(z)\big) \end{equation*}
                      • \(t_\nu^{-1}(0.01)\): the 1% quantile of a Student-\(t\) with \(\nu\) degrees of freedom
                      • \(\sqrt{(\nu - 2)/\nu}\) rescales it to variance 1
                      • FHS (filtered historical simulation): \(\hat q_{0.01}(z)\), the empirical 1% quantile of the standardised residuals \(z_t = (r_t - \hat\mu)/\hat\sigma_t\), replaces the Student-\(t\) quantile McNeil & Frey (2000)

                    Step 4: the Three Backtests

                    Slide 88 in the PDF
                    • Kupiec coverage test Kupiec (1995): is the breach rate equal to \(\alpha = 1\%\)?
                      • \(LR_{uc}\) as in the Chapter 8 review, rejected at 5% when \(LR_{uc} > 3.84\)
                    • Christoffersen independence test Christoffersen (1998): is a breach today more likely after a breach yesterday?
                      \begin{equation*} LR_{ind} = -2\ln\frac{(1-\hat\pi)^{n_{00}+n_{10}}\,\hat\pi^{\,n_{01}+n_{11}}}{(1-\hat\pi_{01})^{n_{00}}\,\hat\pi_{01}^{\,n_{01}}\,(1-\hat\pi_{11})^{n_{10}}\,\hat\pi_{11}^{\,n_{11}}} \;\sim\; \chi^2_1 \end{equation*}
                      • \(n_{ij}\): number of days in state \(j\) after a day in state \(i\) (1 = breach, 0 = no breach)
                      • \(\hat\pi_{01} = n_{01}/(n_{00} + n_{01})\), \(\hat\pi_{11} = n_{11}/(n_{10} + n_{11})\): breach rates after a quiet day and after a breach
                      • \(\hat\pi = (n_{01} + n_{11})/(n_{00} + n_{01} + n_{10} + n_{11})\): the rate over the \(T - 1\) transitions, within \(1/(T - 1)\) of \(x/T\)
                    • Joint test: \(LR_{cc} = LR_{uc} + LR_{ind} \sim \chi^2_2\), with 5% critical value 5.99

                    Step 5: Three Tools for a Formal Comparison (1/2)

                    Slide 92 in the PDF
                    • Dynamic Quantile (DQ) test Engle & Manganelli (2004): are the breaches predictable?
                      • Regress \(I_t - 0.01\) (\(I_t = 1\) if the loss exceeds \(\VaR_t\), else 0) on a constant, \(I_{t-1}, \dots, I_{t-4}\) and \(\VaR_t\)
                      • \(DQ\), the Wald statistic that all six coefficients are zero, is \(\chi^2_6\) under a correct model
                      • It detects breaches predictable from the past or from the level of VaR, which the Christoffersen test (one lag) can miss
                    • FZ0 score Patton, Ziegel & Chen (2019): one number per day that scores a (VaR, ES) forecast pair
                      \begin{equation*} S(v, e; y) = -\frac{\mathbf{1}\{y \le v\}(v - y)}{\alpha e} + \frac{v}{e} + \ln(-e) - 1 \end{equation*}
                      • lower is better
                      • \(\alpha = 2.5\%\): the ES level; \(y\): the realised return
                      • \(v = -\VaR\), \(e = -\ES\): the forecasts written as return quantiles (\(e \le v < 0\))
                      • Strictly consistent for (VaR, ES) Fissler & Ziegel (2016): on average the true pair has the lowest score

                    Power and Robustness without p-Hacking (1/2)

                    Slide 99 in the PDF
                    • Power: before the main test, compute the minimum detectable effect (MDE)
                      • MDE: the smallest effect the design detects with 80% power at 5%
                      • For an approximately Normal estimate with standard error SE, a two-sided 5% test and 80% power
                        \begin{equation*} \mathrm{MDE} = (z_{0.975} + z_{0.80})\,\mathrm{SE} = 2.80\,\mathrm{SE}, \qquad T_{\min} = \Big(\frac{2.80\,\sigma}{\delta}\Big)^2 \ \text{when } \mathrm{SE} = \sigma/\sqrt{T} \end{equation*}
                      • \(z_p\): the \(p\)-quantile of the standard Normal distribution; \(\delta\): the effect you look for; \(\sigma\): the standard deviation of one observation
                      • Deliverable: a simulated power curve of the main test at your \(T\)
                      • Short crypto, ETF or post-event samples make many questions underpowered (Kupiec at \(T = 250\): MDE \(3.65\%\))

                    Slide 104 in the PDF

                    Why Relative Changes Explode: a Toy Example

                    Slide 107 in the PDF
                    • A measure that returns to its start: \(m = (1,\ 0.01,\ 1)\)
                      • Relative changes \(m_t/m_{t-1} - 1\): \(-99\%\), then \(+9\,900\%\); their mean is \(+4\,900.5\%\)
                      • Log changes: \(\ln 0.01\), then \(\ln 100\); their mean is exactly 0, the right answer for a measure with no trend
                      • For a positive measure Jensen' s inequality pushes the mean relative change up (Appendix): the course multiverse explodes upwards
                    • A positive measure can never fall by more than \(100\%\)

                      • or daily changes summed rather than compounded (250 changes of \(-1\%\) add up to \(-250\%\))

                      • pre-specify the transformation (log differences for positive measures, or a linear trend)

                    Inference by Chapter: Further Formulas

                    Slide 111 in the PDF
                    • Chapter 3: Fama–MacBeth standard errors ignore that the betas are estimated
                      • Shanken' s covariance, with \(\hat\gamma\) the risk premia, \(\hat\Sigma_f\) the factor covariance and \(\hat V_{FM}\) the Fama–MacBeth (FM) covariance Shanken (1992)
                      \begin{equation*} \hat V_{\text{Sh}} = (1 + c)\big(\hat V_{FM} - \hat\Sigma_f/T\big) + \hat\Sigma_f/T, \qquad c = \hat\gamma^\top\hat\Sigma_f^{-1}\hat\gamma \end{equation*}
                      • valid for a correctly specified model; with an intercept, \(\hat\Sigma_f\) is bordered by zeros in the row and column of the intercept
                      • the empirical reference of Chapter 3: Kan–Robotti–Shanken errors, robust to misspecification, with Newey–West lags and a sensitivity to their number
                    • Chapter 5: QMLE (quasi-maximum likelihood) of GARCH Bollerslev & Wooldridge (1992)
                      • \(A\): the expected Hessian (sign changed); \(B\): the variance of the score
                      \begin{equation*} \sqrt{T}(\hat\theta - \theta) \Rightarrow N\big(0, A^{-1}BA^{-1}\big) \end{equation*}
                      • in the block of the variance parameters, \(A = B\) when \(\kappa_z = 3\): Normal innovations, but not only
                      • otherwise Hessian-only standard errors (\(A^{-1}\)) are wrong; the factor \(\sqrt{(\kappa_z - 1)/2}\) applies to the Gaussian QMLE only, not to the Student-\(t\) estimator
                    • Chapter 7: the empirical quantile, for i.i.d. returns with density \(f(q_\alpha) > 0\)
                      • in the tail \(f\) is small, so historical VaR is noisy
                      \begin{equation*} \sqrt{T}(\hat q_\alpha - q_\alpha) \Rightarrow N\big(0,\ \alpha(1 - \alpha)/f(q_\alpha)^2\big) \end{equation*}
