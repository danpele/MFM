The chapter asks whether past prices, news or other public information can predict returns, after adjusting for risk and costs. The definition of Fama (1970) depends on the information set: the weak form of efficiency concerns past prices and returns, the semi-strong form all public information, and the strong form private information as well. Every test also needs a model of the expected return, so a rejection does not by itself prove inefficiency: this is the joint hypothesis problem.

Across 16 series of daily log returns, the lag-1 autocorrelation is positive and significant for the BET (0.109), IPC (0.071), Nifty 50 (0.048) and EUR/RON (0.170), and negative and significant for the S&P 500 (−0.099). For Bitcoin (−0.022) and Ethereum (−0.012) it stays inside the robust test band. The effect is small: yesterday's return explains 0.98% of the variance of today's return for the S&P 500 and 1.19% for the BET. The proposed mechanisms, infrequent trading, the managed-float regime and overreaction, are not tested here.

Out-of-sample forecasts and multiple testing call for the same caution. With the dividend–price ratio, the 680 monthly forecasts for the S&P 500 have an out-of-sample \(R^2\) of −0.51%, and the Clark–West test gives \(t = 0.42\), so we do not reject the absence of predictability. Of 2,000 random trading rules, 5.2% are “significant” at 5%, as the test level implies, and the best one has \(|t| = 3.30\), although it is pure chance. The number of rules tried matters as much as the threshold chosen.

    The Random Walk: the Model

    Slide 9 in the PDF
    \[ p_t = \mu + p_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t, \qquad p_t = \ln P_t \]
    • \(P_t\): the index level at the close of day \(t\).
      • \(p_t\): the log price.
      • \(\mu\): the drift (mean growth per day).
      • \(\varepsilon_t\): the day's shock, with mean zero and variance \(\sigma^2\).
    • By repeated substitution: \(p_t = p_0 + \mu t + \sum_{s=1}^{t} \varepsilon_s\).
      • the log price is a linear trend plus the sum of all past shocks.
    • Equivalently, the log return \(r_t = p_t - p_{t-1} = \mu + \varepsilon_t\).
    • The best forecast: \(\E[p_{t+h} \mid \mathcal{I}_t] = p_t + \mu h\), with \(\mathcal{I}_t = \{p_t, p_{t-1}, \dots\}\).
      • it holds if the shocks have conditional mean zero, \(\E[\varepsilon_{t+1} \mid \mathcal{I}_t] = 0\): today's price plus the drift.
      • RW3 (zero autocorrelations only) is not enough.
      • Under RW3, the past does not, in general, improve the best linear forecast.
    • With uncorrelated shocks of variance \(\sigma^2\): \(\Var(p_{t+h} - p_t) = h\,\sigma^2\).
      • the variance grows linearly with the horizon.
      • the basis of the variance ratio in section 2.
    • RW1, RW2 and RW3 differ only in what they assume about the shocks \(\varepsilon_t\) Campbell, Lo & MacKinlay (1997).

    RW1, RW2, RW3: the restrictions on returns

    Slide 10 in the PDF
    • i.i.d.: independent and identically distributed.
    • GARCH: Generalized AutoRegressive Conditional Heteroskedasticity.
    • \(z_t\): a shock with the standard Normal distribution \(N(0,1)\).
      • in GARCH(1,1), \(\omega > 0\) and \(\alpha, \beta \ge 0\).
    Condition on the shocksIllustrative return model
    RW1\(\varepsilon_t\) i.i.d., mean 0, variance \(\sigma^2\)\(r_t=\mu+0.01z_t\), \(z_t\) i.i.d. \(N(0,1)\)
    RW2\(\varepsilon_t\) independent; the variance \(\sigma_t^2\) may vary over time\(r_t=\mu+\sigma_t z_t\), \(\sigma_t=1\%\) then \(3\%\), fixed in advance
    RW3\(\operatorname{Cov}(\varepsilon_t, \varepsilon_{t-k}) = 0\) for every \(k \ge 1\)GARCH: \(\varepsilon_t = \sigma_t z_t\), \(\sigma_t^2 = \omega + \alpha\varepsilon_{t-1}^2 + \beta\sigma_{t-1}^2\)
    • RW1 implies RW2: i.i.d. returns are in particular independent.
    • RW2 implies RW3: independent returns are in particular uncorrelated, provided their variance exists.
    • A GARCH model keeps zero correlation but violates RW2.
      • the size of today's move depends on past shocks.
    • RW3 does not make tomorrow's mean unpredictable: a nonlinear function of the past can forecast it.

          The Variance Ratio: Why and What It Measures

          Slide 19 in the PDF
          VR: Variance Ratio.
          • Why another test: \(\hat\rho_1\) looks at a single lag.
            • small autocorrelations at many lags can each pass the test yet accumulate over longer horizons.
          • Variance ratio over a horizon of \(q\) days Lo & MacKinlay (1988): \(\text{VR}(q) = \dfrac{\Var(r_t + \dots + r_{t-q+1})}{q\,\Var(r_t)}\).
            • equal to 1 without autocorrelation.
            • \(q = 5\): one trading week.
          • Without autocorrelation, \(\widehat{\text{VR}}(q)\) tends to 1 even when volatility varies over time (RW2, RW3).
          • For a covariance-stationary series, \(\text{VR}(q) = 1 + 2 \sum_{k=1}^{q-1} \big(1 - \tfrac{k}{q}\big) \rho_k\).
            • a weighted sum of autocorrelations, with weights that fall with the lag \(k\).
          • \(\text{VR}(q) > 1\) signals persistence, \(\text{VR}(q) < 1\) reversal (mean reversion).
          • For \(q = 2\), \(\text{VR}(2) = 1 + \rho_1\): on the S&P 500, \(1 - 0.099 = 0.901\), equal to \(\widehat{\text{VR}}(2)\) estimated directly.

          The robust variance-ratio band

          Slide 20 in the PDF
          • The robust 95% band is centred at 1, the value under the null.
            • if \(\widehat{\text{VR}}(q)\) falls outside it, we reject the null at 5%.
          • The band is not a confidence interval.
            • a confidence interval would be centred at the estimate \(\widehat{\text{VR}}(q)\), not at 1.
          • The robust null band is \(1 \pm 1.96\sqrt{\hat\theta(q)/T}\) (test construction).
            • \(T\): the number of daily returns.
            • \(\hat\theta(q)\): the estimated variance of \(\sqrt{T}\,(\widehat{\text{VR}}(q) - 1)\), robust to heteroskedasticity.
          • When large moves follow large moves (volatility clustering), \(\hat\theta(q)\) grows and the band widens.
          • \(\hat\theta(q)\) also grows with \(q\), so the band widens at long horizons.
            • a sample contains fewer independent \(q\)-day periods.
          Appendix: a numerical check

          Which variance-ratio horizons do we test?

          Slide 21 in the PDF
          • Why several horizons: reversal or persistence can appear at one day, a week or a month.
            • we do not know in advance at which.
          • We test four horizons, \(q = 2, 5, 10, 20\) trading days.
            • two days, about one week, two weeks and one trading month.
          • The choice follows Lo & MacKinlay (1988), who use \(q = 2, 4, 8, 16\) on weekly data.
            • other horizons can give a different result.
          • The robust null band has level 5% at each horizon separately.
            • with four tests, a false rejection occurs more often than 5% of the time.
          • The Chow–Denning test Chow & Denning (1993) corrects this.
            • it uses the largest \(|z^*(q)|\) across the four horizons.
            • The critical value is \(2.49\) instead of \(1.96\).
          • The Chow–Denning test rejects RW3 for 5 of the 16 series, including the BET and the S&P 500.
          • For Bitcoin, the Chow–Denning test does not reject RW3 (p-value \(= 0.835\)).
          • \(z^*\) and the Chow–Denning test require a finite fourth moment, which Chapter 1 questions.

            Out-of-sample predictability

            3. Out-of-sample predictability

            Slide 26 in the PDF
            • The question: does a variable known today forecast next month's market return?
            • The classic candidate is \(dp\), the log dividend–price ratio.
              • dividends over the last 12 months divided by the average index level in that month.
            • In sample, such regressions appear to work.
              • on new data, most published predictors fail Welch & Goyal (2008).
            • The proper test: every forecast uses only the data available when it is made.
            Appendix: why the dividend–price ratio

            How we build the forecasts

            Slide 27 in the PDF
            OS: Out-of-Sample.
            • \(r\): the monthly S&P 500 log return, with dividends, minus the 3-month US Treasury bill rate.
            • Each month \(t\) we estimate \(r_{s+1} = \alpha + \beta\, dp_s + u_{s+1}\) on all months up to \(t\).
              • at the next step the window grows by one month (expanding window).
            • The \(dp\) forecast for month \(t+1\): \(\hat r_{t+1} = \hat\alpha + \hat\beta\, dp_t\).
            • The competing forecast: the historical mean \(\bar r_{t+1}\).
              • The mean of returns up to month \(t\).
              • it corresponds to no predictability.
            • The first forecast is for January 1970, giving 680 monthly forecasts.
            • Monthly dividends are interpolated from quarterly totals.
              • so \(dp_t\) was not known exactly in month \(t\).
              • the exercise is pseudo-out-of-sample.

            How we score the result: \(R^2_{OS}\)

            Slide 28 in the PDF
            • \(R^2_{OS} = 1 - \sum_t (r_t - \hat r_t)^2 \big/ \sum_t (r_t - \bar r_t)^2\), with the sums over the forecast months.

            • A positive \(R^2_{OS}\) can also arise by chance.
              • we test its significance separately (the Clark–West test, in the results).

              Event studies

              4. Event studies

              Slide 35 in the PDF
              • The question: how fast and how fully does the price absorb a precisely dated piece of public news?
              • To answer it, we need the return the stock would have earned without the news.
              • The difference between the observed return and this expected return is the abnormal return (AR).
              • An event study measures AR around the news and so tests semi-strong efficiency Fama, Fisher, Jensen & Roll (1969).
              • The section's example: the bank tax announced in December 2018.
                • unanticipated news that hit the listed banks directly.

              The Event-Study Method (1/2)

              Slide 36 in the PDF

              The Event-Study Method (2/2)

              Slide 37 in the PDF
              • Abnormal return: \(AR_{i,t} = r_{i,t} - \hat a_i - \hat b_i r_{m,t}\), with \(\hat a_i\), \(\hat b_i\) estimated in the estimation window.
              • Cumulative abnormal return (CAR): \(CAR_i[\tau_1, \tau_2] = \sum_{t=\tau_1}^{\tau_2} AR_{i,t}\), where \([\tau_1, \tau_2]\) are the days summed.
              • First-pass test: \(t = CAR_i / (\hat\sigma_{\varepsilon} \sqrt{L})\) MacKinlay (1997).
                • \(\hat\sigma_\varepsilon\): the standard deviation of the residuals in the estimation window.
                • \(L\): the number of days summed.
              • The formula ignores the uncertainty in \(\hat a_i\) and \(\hat b_i\).
                • its correction appears in the worked example.
              • For unanticipated news, efficiency predicts a jump on day 0 and no predictable drift or reversal afterwards
              • Daily data can only show absorption within a day.
                • speed at the microsecond scale needs intraday data (Chapter 10).
                • literature: Appendix.
              • Application: the price response of Ethereum to network development milestones
                • Quantinar: Market Responses to ETH Development Milestones

                Slide 44 in the PDF
                • The question: does a calendar anomaly remain significant?
                  • once we account for how many patterns were searched
                • The literature has tested thousands of patterns on the same data, and some pass the test by chance alone Harvey, Liu & Zhu (2016).

                Calendar effects and data snooping

                Slide 45 in the PDF
                • A calendar effect is a difference in mean returns associated with a day or a month.
                • Day-of-the-week effect: mean returns differ across Monday, Tuesday, …, Friday French (1980).
                • January effect: the mean January return differs from that of the other months Rozeff & Kinney (1976).
                • Turn of the month (TOM): the last trading day of a month and the first three of the next Ariel (1987).
                • Data snooping: selecting a pattern after searching many alternatives on the same data.

                How Many “Anomalies” Does Pure Chance Produce? (1/3)

                Slide 46 in the PDF
                • Why we simulate: here every null hypothesis is true, so every rejection is false.
                • Experiment: 2 000 trading rules with no economic content, on daily S&P 500 returns.
                • Each rule picks 20% of the days at random and calls them “good days”.
                • For each rule, a \(t\) test compares the mean return on “good days” with the mean on the other days.
                • The chart: the histogram of the 2 000 \(t\) statistics, over the standard Normal density.
                • The vertical lines: the thresholds \(|t| = 1.96\) (5%) and \(|t| = 3.0\), the bar proposed by Harvey, Liu & Zhu (2016).

                How Many “Anomalies” Does Pure Chance Produce? (2/3)

                Slide 47 in the PDF
                  Question for the room For 2,000 tests with valid p-values, which individual Bonferroni cutoff guarantees at most a 5% probability of any false rejection, regardless of dependence between tests?
                  Answer

                  The cutoff is 0.05/2,000 = 0.000025. The value 0.025 divides the level by two rather than by the number of tests; a two-sided p-value already includes both tails. The value 0.05 retains the individual level without controlling the family. Bonferroni's guarantee does not require independent tests. In the simulation, of 2,000 rules with no economic content, 104 (5.2%) have |t| > 1.96, six have |t| > 3.0, and the best has |t| = 3.30. Adding rules to the same family cannot lower the largest |t|.

                  Multiple Testing: Bonferroni and Holm

                  Slide 51 in the PDF
                  FWER: Family-Wise Error Rate — the probability of at least one false rejection in the family of tests.
                  • Why FWER: we want every anomaly we report to be real, not only most of them.
                  • With \(M\) independent tests at level \(\alpha\): \(\text{FWER} = 1 - (1 - \alpha)^M\).
                  • For \(M = 15\) and \(\alpha = 5\%\), FWER would be \(53.7\%\) if the tests were independent.
                  • Bonferroni: reject only if \(p_i < \alpha / M\).
                    • it guarantees \(\text{FWER} \leq \alpha\) under any dependence between the tests.
                  • Holm: sort \(p_{(1)} \leq \dots \leq p_{(M)}\) and reject while \(p_{(j)} < \alpha/(M - j + 1)\), stopping at the first failure.
                  • Holm has the same guarantee and rejects at least everything Bonferroni rejects.
                  • Bootstrap methods that use the correlation between tests (StepM): Appendix.

                  Moments, tails and asymptotics

                  Slide 58 in the PDF
                  • The question: which assumptions make an efficiency test valid, and which alternatives can it detect?
                  • Robust standard errors of autocorrelations (Further Topics) need a finite fourth moment, \(\E[r^4]<\infty\)
                    • heavy tails: Student \(t\) with four degrees of freedom has finite variance but \(\E[r^4]=\infty\), and then the robust standard error no longer converges

                    • correct as the sample grows
                    • possibly inaccurate in a short sample such as a 500-day window
                  • Limit theorems also need weak dependence (mixing)
                    • observations far enough apart in time become nearly independent
                    • mixing allows volatility clustering and dependence between neighbouring days

                  Long memory and changing efficiency

                  Short memory, long memory and scaling

                  Slide 60 in the PDF
                  • Does dependence persist across horizons and market regimes?
                  AR: AutoRegressive model.
                  • Short memory has absolutely summable autocovariances
                    • an AR(1) with coefficient 0.5 has correlations \(0.5^k\) at lag \(k\).
                  • Long memory here means positive dependence decaying so slowly that the autocovariance sum diverges
                    • power-law decay can produce this.
                  • Scaling: a measure of fluctuations over \(n\) days, \(F(n)\), grows like \(n^H\)
                    • e.g. the standard deviation of a sum of \(n\) returns
                    • \(H\) is the Hurst exponent, estimated in Further Topics
                    • doubling the horizon multiplies fluctuations by \(2^H\): about 1.41 at \(H = 0.5\) (no long memory) and 1.68 at \(H = 0.75\)
                  • Anti-persistence describes offsetting increments, as in fractional Gaussian noise (fGn) with \(H<0.5\)
                    • fGn is a stationary Gaussian increment model with partial-sum variance proportional to \(n^{2H}\).

                  Variance Ratio and Hurst Exponent: Long or Short Memory?

                  Slide 61 in the PDF
                  • For fGn, the variance of a sum of \(q\) returns is \(\Var(r_t + \dots + r_{t-q+1}) = q^{2H} \Var(r_t)\).
                    • fGn: the exactly self-similar process defined above.
                  • Hence \(\text{VR}(q) = q^{2H-1}\), and an observed \(\text{VR}(q)\) implies a Hurst exponent \(H(q) = 0.5 + \ln \text{VR}(q) / (2 \ln q)\).
                  • At \(q = 20\) days, the implied exponent is \(0.553\) for the BET and \(0.453\) for the S&P 500.
                  • The problem: a process without long memory can give \(H(q) \neq 0.5\)
                    • Example: an AR(1) with \(\rho_1 = -0.1\) has autocorrelations \(\rho_k = (-0.1)^k\), so only short memory.
                    • For this AR(1), \(\text{VR}(20) = 0.826\), so \(H(20) = 0.468\): the process looks “anti-persistent” without any long memory.
                    • At \(q = 250\), the same process gives \(H(250) = 0.482\): for short memory, \(H(q)\) approaches \(0.5\) as \(q\) grows.
                  • An AR(1) with BET's autocorrelation, \(\hat\rho_1 = 0.109\), gives \(H(20) = 0.535\).
                  • So most of the gap between BET's \(H(20) = 0.553\) and \(0.5\) is short memory.
                  • Separating long from short memory needs a statistic built for it: Lo's modified R/S (Further Topics).

                    Why the robust band is wider: a numerical check

                    Slide 74 in the PDF
                    • For \(q = 2\), the classical statistic is \(z = \sqrt{T}\,(\widehat{\text{VR}}(2) - 1)\).

                    • For the S&P 500, \(z = -8.15\) and \(z^* = -3.66\).

                    Fair games and martingales

                    Slide 78 in the PDF
                    • Fair game for excess returns: \(\E[r_{t+1} - \mu_t \mid \mathcal{I}_t] = 0\)
                      • \(\mathcal{I}_t\): the information available at the end of day \(t\); \(\E[\cdot \mid \mathcal{I}_t]\): the expectation given that information
                      • \(\mu_t\): the required (equilibrium) return, the compensation for risk
                      • in words: the return beyond \(\mu_t\) cannot be forecast from \(\mathcal{I}_t\)
                    • Martingale: a process whose expected next value, given the past, equals its current value
                      • with a constant required simple return \(m\), the value \(V_t\) of a position with dividends reinvested, discounted at \(m\), \(V_t/(1 + m)^t\), is a martingale Campbell, Lo & MacKinlay (1997)
                      • discounting at the risk-free rate gives a martingale only under the risk-neutral measure (slide on the risk-neutral measure)
                      • the price \(P_t\) itself is a martingale only if no dividends are paid and \(m = 0\); the expected log return is then negative (the Jensen term, next slide)

                    The Jensen term: why averages differ

                    Slide 79 in the PDF
                    • The logarithm is concave.
                      • averaging log outcomes gives less than taking the log of their average, unless outcomes are constant.
                    • Equally likely simple returns of \(+10\%\) and \(-10\%\) have mean zero.
                    • The mean log return is \(\tfrac12\ln(1.1)+\tfrac12\ln(0.9)=\tfrac12\ln(0.99)\approx-0.00503\).
                    • Along a path with one rise and one fall, wealth 100 becomes \(100(1.1)(0.9)=99\).
                      • a zero arithmetic mean does not preserve compounded wealth.

                    Prices, log prices and information

                    Slide 80 in the PDF
                    • A martingale in prices and a constant mean log return are different restrictions
                      • a martingale in prices: \(\E[P_{t+1} \mid \mathcal{I}_t] = P_t\)
                      • a constant mean log return: \(\E[\ln(P_{t+1}/P_t) \mid \mathcal{I}_t] = \mu\)
                      • the difference is the Jensen term: \(\E[\ln(1 + R)] \approx \E[R] - \Var(R)/2\), with \(R = P_{t+1}/P_t - 1\) the simple return
                    • Consequence of the fair game: \(\text{corr}(\varepsilon_{t+1}, g(\mathcal{I}_t)) = 0\) for any function \(g\) of past information
                      • where \(\varepsilon_{t+1} = r_{t+1} - \mu_t\) is the unexpected part of the return
                    • If \(\mu_t = \mu\) is constant, all autocorrelations \(\rho_k = \text{corr}(r_t, r_{t-k})\), \(k \geq 1\), are zero
                      • this is the hypothesis tested in section 2
                    • The converse fails: zero autocorrelations do not rule out a nonlinearly predictable mean
                      • the MDH, next topic

                    Discounting and the risk-neutral measure

                    Slide 81 in the PDF
                    • Why: the martingale above uses the actual probabilities and the required return \(m\).
                      • pricing theory uses, equivalently, the risk-free rate and risk-adjusted probabilities.
                    • A risk-neutral measure uses pricing probabilities, in an arbitrage-free model.
                      • under which discounted traded prices, including dividends, are martingales.
                    • Example: a stock without dividends costs 100 today.
                      • in one year it is worth 120 or 80.
                      • the risk-free rate is 5%.
                      • The pricing probability \(q=0.625\) gives \(q\,120+(1-q)80=105\), and \(105/1.05 = 100\), today's price.
                    • This \(q\) need not equal the actual probability of an increase.
                      • pricing probabilities are not forecasts of frequencies.

                    Fundamental value, rational bubbles and fads

                    Slide 83 in the PDF
                    • With a constant required simple return \(m\), the fair game is \(\E[P_{t+1} + D_{t+1} \mid \mathcal{I}_t] = (1 + m)\,P_t\)
                      • \(D_{t+1}\): the cash flow (dividend) paid in \(t + 1\)
                      • Fundamental value \(P^*_t = \E\big[\sum_{j \geq 1} D_{t+j}/(1 + m)^j \mid \mathcal{I}_t\big]\); pricing error \(B_t = P_t - P^*_t\)
                      • The fundamental value \(P^*_t\) satisfies the same fair-game equation as the price
                      • Subtracting the two equations, the gap \(B_t\) satisfies \(\E[B_{t+1} \mid \mathcal{I}_t] = (1 + m)\,B_t\)
                    • A fair game ties the price to the fundamental value only up to a rational bubble
                      • A gap \(B_t > 0\) sustained by the expectation of resale
                      • A gap \(B_t \neq 0\) is admissible only if it is expected to grow at the required rate \(m\) (Chapter 17)
                      • A mean-reverting gap (fad) makes \(\varepsilon_{t+1}\) predictable and violates the fair game, although short-horizon tests have little power against it Summers (1986)
                    • The restriction is on the conditional mean only
                      • The variance is not restricted
                      • volatility may be predictable (Chapter 1)

                    Rational bubble: what happens if it can burst?

                    Slide 85 in the PDF
                    • Initial bubble \(B_t=20\) RON and required return \(m=10\%\).
                      • Survival probability \(\pi=0.8\); crash probability \(1-\pi=0.2\).
                      • A crash makes the bubble zero; investors know the probabilities.
                    • The fair game for the bubble, \(\E[B_{t+1}\mid\mathcal I_t]=(1+m)B_t\), requires an expected bubble of \((1+m)B_t=22\) RON.
                      • Conditional on survival: \(B_{t+1}=22/0.8=27.5\) RON.
                      • Conditional growth is \(27.5/20-1=37.5\%\).

                    • The purchase price is 120 RON.

                    Rational bubble and the no-bubble condition

                    Slide 86 in the PDF
                    • The fair game restricts only the expected return over the next period.
                      • it does not pin down the price level.
                    • No-bubble condition: \(\displaystyle\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{\E[P_{t+k}\mid\mathcal I_t]}{(1+m)^k}=0\)
                      • also called the transversality condition
                      • \(k\): the resale horizon; the discounted expected resale price vanishes
                    • Under this condition the price equals the fundamental value \(P^*_t\).
                      • it is supported entirely by dividends.
                    • A rational bubble violates the condition.
                      • from \(\E[B_{t+k}\mid\mathcal I_t]=(1+m)^kB_t\), the discounted value of the bubble stays \(B_t\neq 0\) at every horizon.
                    • In the numerical example, the 20 RON bubble is still worth 20 RON in today's money, however distant the resale.
                    • Tirole (1985): in overlapping-generations models, a bubble can exist only if:
                      • the interest rate is below the growth rate of the economy.

                    Autocorrelation and Its Robust Standard Error

                    Slide 89 in the PDF
                    • Sample autocorrelation: \(\hat\rho_k = \sum_{t=k+1}^T e_t e_{t-k} / \sum_{t=1}^T e_t^2\), with \(e_t = r_t - \bar r\)
                      • \(T\): number of daily returns; \(\bar r\): their mean; \(k\): the lag; \(\hat\rho_k > 0\): persistence, \(\hat\rho_k < 0\): reversal
                    • Under RW1 (i.i.d.): \(\text{SE}(\hat\rho_k) \approx 1/\sqrt{T}\); 95% band \(\pm 1.96/\sqrt{T}\)
                    • Unpredictable mean with volatility clustering (finite fourth moments): \(\text{SE}(\hat\rho_k) \approx \sqrt{\hat\tau_k}\)
                      • \(\hat\tau_k = \sum_t e_t^2 e_{t-k}^2 \,/\, \big(\sum_t e_t^2\big)^2\) (robust variance under \(H_0\); for test bands centred at 0)
                      • When large moves cluster, \(\hat\tau_k > 1/T\): the i.i.d. band is too narrow and classical tests reject too often; the robust statistics \(\tilde Q\) (Ljung–Box) and \(z^*\) (variance ratio) use \(\hat\tau_k\) instead of \(1/T\)

                    Intervals for \(\rho_k\): the HAC Standard Error

                    Slide 90 in the PDF
                    • \(\sqrt{\hat\tau_k}\) is derived under \(H_0\): valid for a test band centred at 0, not for an interval around \(\hat\rho_k \neq 0\).
                    • Around the estimate we use the HAC variance of the estimator Newey & West (1987):
                      • \(\hat\psi_t = (e_t e_{t-k} - \hat\rho_k e_t^2)/\hat\gamma_0\), with \(\hat\gamma_0 = \tfrac{1}{T}\sum_t e_t^2\): the contribution of day \(t\) to \(\hat\rho_k - \rho_k\).
                      • \(\widehat{\Var}(\hat\rho_k) = \tfrac{1}{T}\big[\hat c_0 + 2\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}\big(1 - \tfrac{j}{\ell+1}\big)\hat c_j\big]\), with \(\hat c_j\) the lag-\(j\) autocovariance of \(\hat\psi_t\).
                      • bandwidth \(\ell = \lfloor 4 (T/100)^{2/9} \rfloor\): 9–10 lags for the full samples, 4–5 for one calendar year, 6 for the ETF windows.
                    • Use: intervals around \(\hat\rho_k\) and comparisons between periods (crypto years, before/after the ETFs).
                    • Check: a moving-block bootstrap (blocks of \(T^{1/3}\) days) gives similar values.
                      • S&P 500: HAC \(0.030\), block bootstrap \(0.031\), \(\text{SE}_0 = 0.027\).

                    Portmanteau Tests: Ljung–Box and Its Robust Version

                    Slide 92 in the PDF
                    • Ljung & Box (1978): \(Q(m) = T(T+2) \sum_{k=1}^m \hat\rho_k^2/(T-k) \sim \chi^2_m\) under i.i.d. returns
                      • Tests jointly \(H_0\): \(\rho_1 = \dots = \rho_m = 0\)
                      • \(m\): the number of lags
                    • Robust version Lobato, Nankervis & Savin (2001): \(\tilde Q(m) = \sum_{k=1}^m \hat\rho_k^2/\hat\tau_k \to \chi^2_m\) under RW3
                      • if the \(\hat\rho_k\) at different lags are asymptotically uncorrelated
                      • the condition, \(\E[\varepsilon_t^2\varepsilon_{t-j}\varepsilon_{t-k}] = 0\) for \(j \neq k\), holds for a GARCH with symmetric innovations
                    • Three markets at \(m = 10\) (5% critical value \(18.31\))
                      • S&P 500: \(Q = 97.1\) but \(\tilde Q = 20.0\) (\(p = 0.029\))
                      • BET: \(Q = 108.0\), \(\tilde Q = 24.4\) (\(p = 0.007\))
                      • Bitcoin: \(Q = 20.2\) (\(p = 0.028\)) but \(\tilde Q = 11.9\) (\(p = 0.294\))
                    • The robust test keeps the rejections for S&P 500 and BET, but not for Bitcoin
                      • \(\tilde Q\) requires a finite fourth moment, which Chapter 1 questions

                    The Runs Test

                    Slide 93 in the PDF
                    • The runs test Wald & Wolfowitz (1940) uses only the signs of returns (zero-return days dropped), so heavy tails do not affect it
                    • A run is a maximal block of equal signs: ++|-|+++ has 3 runs
                    • With \(n_1\) positive days, \(n_2\) negative days and \(n = n_1 + n_2\), if the signs are independent:
                      • \(\E[R] = \dfrac{2 n_1 n_2}{n} + 1\),   \(\Var[R] = \dfrac{2 n_1 n_2 (2 n_1 n_2 - n)}{n^2 (n-1)}\),   \(z = \dfrac{R - \E[R]}{\sqrt{\Var[R]}} \approx N(0,1)\)
                      • \(R\): the observed number of runs; \(|z| > 1.96\) rejects independence at 5%
                      • the test assumes a constant probability of a positive day

                      • too many (\(z > 0\)) mean signs that alternate

                    • BET: \(z = -6.05\), signs persist, as \(\hat\rho_1 > 0\)

                    The Lo–MacKinlay Test

                    Slide 94 in the PDF
                    • Lo & MacKinlay (1988) estimate \(\text{VR}(q)\) from overlapping sums of \(q\) daily returns and correct the small-sample bias
                      • \(\hat\sigma_a^2 = \frac{1}{T-1}\sum_t (r_t - \hat\mu)^2\),   \(\hat\sigma_c^2(q) = \frac{1}{m}\sum_{t=q}^{T} \big(\textstyle\sum_{j=0}^{q-1} r_{t-j} - q\hat\mu\big)^2\)
                      • \(m = q(T - q + 1)(1 - q/T)\);   \(\widehat{\text{VR}}(q) = \hat\sigma_c^2(q) / \hat\sigma_a^2\)
                      • \(\hat\mu\): mean daily return; \(\hat\sigma_a^2\): variance of one-day returns; \(\hat\sigma_c^2(q)\): variance of the overlapping \(q\)-day sums divided by \(q\); \(m\): a divisor that removes the small-sample bias
                    • Test statistic under RW1 (homoskedastic returns, i.e. with constant variance)
                      • \(z(q) = \dfrac{\widehat{\text{VR}}(q) - 1}{\sqrt{2(2q-1)(q-1)/(3qT)}} \approx N(0,1)\)
                      • the denominator is the standard error of \(\widehat{\text{VR}}(q)\) under RW1; \(|z| > 1.96\) rejects \(\text{VR}(q) = 1\) at 5%
                    • This \(z\) rejects too often under volatility clustering (next slide)

                    The Heteroskedasticity-Robust Statistic \(z^*\)

                    Slide 95 in the PDF
                    • Under RW3 plus the conditions of Lo & MacKinlay (1988), the \(\hat\rho_k\) are asymptotically uncorrelated with variances \(\hat\delta_k / T\)
                      • finite moments, weak dependence (mixing), \(\E[\varepsilon_t^2 \varepsilon_{t-j} \varepsilon_{t-k}] = 0\) for \(j \neq k\)
                      • \(\hat\delta_k = T\hat\tau_k\), with \(\hat\tau_k\) of the autocorrelation slide: the robust variance of \(\sqrt{T}\hat\rho_k\), equal to 1 without volatility clustering
                    • Asymptotic variance of \(\widehat{\text{VR}}(q)\) and the test statistic
                      • \(\hat\theta(q) = \sum_{k=1}^{q-1} \Big(\dfrac{2(q-k)}{q}\Big)^2 \hat\delta_k, \qquad z^*(q) = \dfrac{\sqrt{T}\,(\widehat{\text{VR}}(q) - 1)}{\sqrt{\hat\theta(q)}} \approx N(0,1)\)
                      • \(2(q-k)/q\): the weight of \(\hat\rho_k\) in \(\widehat{\text{VR}}(q)\); \(|z^*| > 1.96\) rejects \(\text{VR}(q) = 1\) at 5%
                    • RW3 alone is not enough
                      • the conditions hold for GARCH with symmetric innovations
                      • but can fail with a leverage effect
                      • Without the condition \(\E[\varepsilon_t^2 \varepsilon_{t-j} \varepsilon_{t-k}] = 0\), \(\hat\theta(q)\) needs the full covariance matrix of the \(\hat\rho_k\)
                    • At \(q = 5\), the S&P 500 has \(z^*(5) = -2.78\)
                      • the reversal stays significant with the robust standard error
                      • the comparison of \(z\) with \(z^*\) at \(q = 2\): section 2

                    Beyond Linear Predictability: the Martingale Difference Hypothesis

                    Slide 98 in the PDF
                    • MDH (Martingale Difference Hypothesis): \(\E[r_t \mid \mathcal{I}_{t-1}] = \mu\)
                      • no function of the past predicts the mean, linear or not
                    • RW3 (all \(\rho_k = 0\)) is weaker. Counterexample: \(r_t = \varepsilon_t + \theta(\varepsilon_{t-1}^2 - \sigma^2)\), \(\varepsilon_t\) i.i.d. symmetric
                      • \(\sigma^2 = \Var(\varepsilon_t)\), so the extra term has mean 0; \(\theta \neq 0\): how strongly yesterday's squared shock moves today's mean
                      • all \(\rho_k = 0\), yet \(\E[r_t \mid \varepsilon_{t-1}] = \theta(\varepsilon_{t-1}^2 - \sigma^2)\) is predictable: autocorrelation tests cannot see it
                      • The MDH tests on the next slide look for exactly this kind of dependence (results: Appendix)
                    • BDS (Brock–Dechert–Scheinkman) Brock, Dechert, Scheinkman & LeBaron (1996) compares the frequency of nearby return sequences
                      • with that implied by i.i.d. observations
                      • it tests i.i.d., not the MDH
                      • GARCH alone makes it reject
                      • A BDS rejection is evidence against RW1, not against weak-form efficiency

                    Slide 99 in the PDF

                    Slide 100 in the PDF

                    Predictive Regressions and the Stambaugh Bias (2/2)

                    Slide 104 in the PDF
                    • \(x_t\) is predetermined, not strictly exogenous: OLS is consistent but biased Stambaugh (1999)
                      • Project \(u\) on \(v\): \(u = (\sigma_{uv}/\sigma_v^2)\, v + e\), so \(\hat\beta - \beta = (\sigma_{uv}/\sigma_v^2)(\hat\rho - \rho) + \text{noise}\); \(e\): the part of \(u\) unrelated to \(v\)
                      • Both regressions use the same months and the same \(x_t\), so the estimation errors \(\hat\beta - \beta\) and \(\hat\rho - \rho\) are linked through the correlation of the shocks \(u\) and \(v\); the noise term has mean zero when \(e\) is independent of the whole path of \(x\), e.g. \((u, v)\) i.i.d. with a bivariate Normal distribution
                      • \(\hat\rho\) is biased downward in small samples Kendall (1954), and this error passes into \(\hat\beta\)
                      • With \(\E[\hat\rho - \rho] \approx -(1 + 3\rho)/T\):   \(\E[\hat\beta - \beta] \approx -\dfrac{\sigma_{uv}}{\sigma_v^2}\,\dfrac{1 + 3\rho}{T}\)
                    • Price up \(\Rightarrow\) \(dp\) down: \(\sigma_{uv} < 0\), so the bias is upward: predictability looks stronger than it is
                    • Near a unit root the \(t\)-statistic is not \(N(0,1)\) either
                      • valid inference by a Bonferroni \(Q\)-test over a confidence interval for \(\rho\) Campbell & Yogo (2006)

                    Comparing Forecasts: Diebold–Mariano and Clark–West

                    Slide 107 in the PDF

                    The Rescaled Range (R/S) Statistic

                    Slide 109 in the PDF
                    • Using the rescaled range, Hurst (1951) found that years of high Nile floods followed other high-flood years
                      • more often than chance allows
                      • history: Appendix
                    • Construction for a window of \(n\) returns with mean \(\bar r_n\) and standard deviation \(s_n\)
                      • Cumulative deviations \(Y_k = \sum_{i=1}^k (r_i - \bar r_n)\), \(k = 1, \dots, n\)
                      • Range \(R_n = \max_k Y_k - \min_k Y_k\); rescaled range \(R_n / s_n\)
                    • Scaling law: \(\E[R_n/s_n] \approx c\, n^H\), where \(H\) is the Hurst exponent
                      • \(c\): a constant; taking logs, \(\ln(R/S) \approx \ln c + H \ln n\), so \(H\) is a slope
                      • Estimate: slope of \(\ln(R/S)\) on \(\ln n\), averaging \(R/S\) over non-overlapping windows
                    • \(H = 0.5\): no long memory (short-run dependence allowed)
                      • \(H > 0.5\): persistence, long memory with \(\rho_k \sim k^{2H-2}\)
                      • \(H < 0.5\): anti-persistence

                    Detrended Fluctuation Analysis (DFA) (1/2)

                    Slide 113 in the PDF
                    • Peng et al. (1994): DFA measures how the fluctuation of cumulative returns grows with the window length.
                    • Removing a linear trend from the profile removes a constant mean of returns in each box.
                    • If the mean of returns itself trends, higher-order DFA is needed.
                    • The algorithm
                      • The profile \(y_k = \sum_{t \leq k} (r_t - \bar r)\) is the running sum of deviations from the mean, over the full sample.
                      • The profile is split into boxes of length \(n\).
                      • In each box a linear trend is fitted and the residuals are kept.
                      • \(F(n)\) is the root mean square of all residuals; the calculation is repeated for many \(n\).
                    • \(F(n) \propto n^{\alpha_{DFA}}\): the DFA exponent \(\alpha_{DFA}\) plays the role of \(H\) (\(\alpha_{DFA} = 0.5\): no long memory).

                    Detrended Fluctuation Analysis (DFA) (2/2)

                    Slide 114 in the PDF

                    Event Studies: Correlation Across Firms

                    Slide 127 in the PDF
                    • The question: what happens to the test of \(\overline{AR}_0\) when all firms have the event on the same day?
                    • Let \(N\) be the number of firms and \(\bar\varrho\) the average correlation of their abnormal returns.
                      • estimated in the estimation window.
                      • for TLV and BRD, \(\bar\varrho = 0.153\).

                    Validation, leakage and shrinkage

                    Slide 137 in the PDF
                    • Flexible models, such as trees and neural networks, can also fit accidental sample patterns (overfitting).
                    • Walk-forward validation: the model is estimated only on data before the test period.
                    • Leakage: a forecast uses information unavailable at its date.
                      • such as an accounting figure revised later.
                    • Ridge: \(\hat g = \arg\min_g \sum_t (r_t - x_t^\prime g)^2 + \lambda \sum_j g_j^2 = (X^\prime X + \lambda I)^{-1} X^\prime r\).

                      • reduces estimation variance.
                    • With \(N(0, \sigma^2)\) errors and a prior \(g \sim N(0, \tau^2 I)\), ridge with \(\lambda = \sigma^2/\tau^2\) is the posterior mean of the coefficients.
                      • Bayesian learning.
                    • Cross-validation: leave out one block of data at a time (e.g. a year).
                      • estimate on the rest.
                      • pick the \(\lambda\) with the smallest error on the blocks left out.

                    Difference-in-differences: example and assumptions

                    Slide 149 in the PDF
                    • The before/after test cannot separate the effect of the ETFs from other changes in 2024.

                      • It subtracts the control group's change from the treated group's change.
                    • Parallel trends:
                      • without treatment, the treated and control groups would have changed in the same way.
                    • Similar pre-treatment trends support this assumption but cannot prove it.
                    • No anticipation: the treated group does not react before treatment.
                    • No spillover: treatment does not affect the control group.
                      • for example, a Bitcoin ETF must not change Ethereum trading.

                      • \(d > 0\): the density explodes at low frequencies, long memory

                      • \(\hat d = \arg\min_d \Big[\ln\Big(\tfrac1m\sum_{j=1}^m \lambda_j^{2d} I(\lambda_j)\Big) - \tfrac{2d}{m}\sum_{j=1}^m \ln\lambda_j\Big]\) , \(I\): the periodogram (sample spectral density)
                      • Robinson (1995): for \(|d| < 1/2\), \(m \to \infty\), \(m/T \to 0\), a smooth \(f\) near 0 and a linear process with conditionally homoskedastic martingale-difference innovations, \(\sqrt m(\hat d - d) \to N(0, 1/4)\) without Gaussianity: a confidence interval instead of a band
                    • Estimates with \(m = T^{0.65}\) and the 95% CI \(\hat d \pm 1.96/(2\sqrt m)\)
                      • S&P 500 \(-0.013\) \([-0.069, 0.043]\); Bitcoin \(0.063\) \([-0.002, 0.127]\)
                      • BET \(0.118\) \([0.062, 0.174]\); Ethereum \(0.112\) \([0.045, 0.179]\)
                    • Bandwidth trade-off: a large \(m\) reaches frequencies where short-run autocorrelation biases \(\hat d\) upward
                      • BET \(\hat\rho_1 = 0.109\)
                      • with \(m = T^{0.5}\) BET gives \(\hat d = 0.135\) (SE \(0.056\)): a sensitivity check supporting, not proving, that its \(d > 0\) is not only short memory
                      • GARCH returns also strain the conditions above

                    Appendix: Case Study, Model and Method (1/2)

                    Slide 168 in the PDF
                    • Model (Martin & Nagel (2022), Sec. 2)
                      • cash-flow growth \(\Delta y_t = Xg + e_t\), with \(e_t \sim N(0, I)\) and a zero interest rate
                      • \(\Delta y_t\): the \(N\) cash-flow growth rates; \(X\): the \(N \times J\) matrix of characteristics; \(g\): the \(J\) unknown coefficients; \(I\): identity matrix
                    • Learning (Prop. 1–2)
                      • prior \(g \sim N(0, (\theta/J)\,I)\), the true distribution from which \(g\) is drawn; \(\theta\): signal strength
                      • schematically, one period: the price is \(X\hat g_t\), with \(\hat g_t = (tX^\prime X + (J/\theta)I)^{-1}X^\prime\sum_{s \le t}\Delta y_s\), so \(\E[\hat g_t \mid g] = Ag\), with \(A\) “smaller” than \(I\)
                      • \(r_{t+1} = X(I - A)g\) (underreaction from shrinkage) \(- X(\hat g_t - Ag)\) (estimation noise) \(+ e_{t+1}\) (unpredictable shock)
                    • Asymptotics and simulations (Sec. 4)
                      • \(N, J \to \infty\) with \(J/N \to \psi > 0\)
                      • simulations with \(N = 1000\), entries of \(X\) drawn from the standard Normal distribution and \(\theta = 1\)

                    Slide 169 in the PDF

                    • Tests
                      • in sample: a cross-sectional OLS of \(r_{t+1}\) on \(X\) (requires \(J < N\)) with a joint \(\chi^2\) test

                    Appendix: Case Study, In Sample vs Out of Sample in Detail

                    Slide 172 in the PDF
                    • \(r_{\text{IS}} \geq 0\) in every 20-year window by construction: \(r_{\text{IS}} = b^{\prime} G^{-1} b / 240\)
