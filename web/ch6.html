Central chart: Stocks and Bonds: A Correlation That Changed Sign (1/2)

Forbes & Rigobon (2002) show that in a crisis the correlation coefficient can rise even when the link between markets stays the same, simply because volatility has risen. The chapter's question is when markets move together and how sure we can be of it. Diversification depends on the correlation through a single cross term of the portfolio variance. A 60/40 portfolio of SPY and TLT, exchange-traded funds on the S&P 500 and on US Treasury bonds with maturities above 20 years, has a volatility of 10.4% with the 2002–2021 correlation (−0.40) and 13.2% with the 2022–2026 one (0.11). The 252-day correlation of the two funds was positive on 6% of days before 2022 and on 81% of days since January 2022. Its sign depends on the type of shock: growth shocks move stocks and bond prices in opposite directions, inflation shocks hit both.

The first problem is measurement. The Fisher interval assumes i.i.d. data and is too narrow on daily returns, which have fat tails and volatility clustering; the HAC (heteroskedasticity and autocorrelation consistent) interval from the delta method accounts for both. Across time zones, asynchronous closes bias the daily correlation towards zero, and 2-day or weekly returns reduce this bias. For today's covariance, the chapter compares rolling windows, EWMA and the DCC (dynamic conditional correlation) model. For SPY and TLT, DCC(1,1) has \(a+b = 0.986\), and the constant conditional correlation (CCC) is rejected (LR 533.4, bootstrap p 0.005).

In crises, the Forbes–Rigobon bias appears. In 2008 the variance of 2-day S&P 500 returns was 7.5 times its calm-year level; at this level, with a constant \(\beta\), a calm-period correlation of 0.4 is measured as 0.77 in the crisis. After the adjustment, the crisis rises in the correlation with the Euro Stoxx 50 and with the BET are no longer significant, although the adjustment may over-correct when a common shock hits every market. Correlation does not describe dependence in the tails, however: when JPM has one of its 5% worst days, BAC is in its own worst 5% about 61% of the time. Copulas separate the marginal distributions from the dependence structure and allow tail dependence to be measured.

Why Dependence Matters (1/2): Portfolio Diversification

Slide 4 in the PDF
  • Diversification (Markowitz (1952))
    • The portfolio variance depends on the correlation through a single cross term
    • \(\sigma_p^2=w^\top\Sigma w=w^2\sigma_1^2+(1-w)^2\sigma_2^2+2w(1-w)\rho\,\sigma_1\sigma_2\)
    • \(w\): weight of asset 1 (\(1-w\) in asset 2)
    • \(\Sigma\): covariance matrix
    • \(\sigma_1\), \(\sigma_2\): volatilities
    • \(\rho\): correlation

Why Dependence Matters (2/2): Hedges, Contagion and Moving Covariances

Slide 5 in the PDF
  • Hedging
    • How much value of the hedge \(F\) to sell per unit of value held in the position \(S\) to minimise the variance
    • Minimum-variance hedge ratio \(h^*=\rho\,\sigma_S/\sigma_F\): the slope of the regression of \(r_S\) on \(r_F\). Estimated on returns, it is a ratio of values: units of \(F\) per unit of \(S\) are \(h^*P_S/P_F\) (\(P_S\), \(P_F\): prices, contract size included)
    • \(r_S\), \(r_F\): returns of the position and of the hedge
    • \(\sigma_S\), \(\sigma_F\): their volatilities
    • Effectiveness \(\rho^2\): the share of the variance removed
    • If the correlation moves, yesterday's hedge is wrong today: a dynamic hedge needs a dynamic \(\rho_t\)
  • Contagion and interdependence (Forbes & Rigobon (2002))
    • Contagion: a significant increase in cross-market linkages after a shock
    • Interdependence: links present in all states (trade, common investors, common macro factors)
    • Correlations tend to rise exactly when diversification is needed most
  • New in this chapter: \(\Sigma_t\), the covariance matrix at date \(t\), moves

    Measuring Covariance and Its Uncertainty

    How Precise Is an Estimated Correlation?

    Slide 8 in the PDF
    • The question of this section: how do we estimate \(\Sigma\), and how uncertain is each estimated correlation?
    • First trap: closing times differ, so the data frequency changes the correlation
    • Second trap: fat tails and volatility clustering can change the uncertainty substantially, and the Fisher interval ignores them
    • Third trap: the window length decides how fast we see a regime change

    Asynchronous Trading: A Trap for Daily Correlations

    Slide 9 in the PDF
    • Closing times differ
      • the Bucharest Stock Exchange and the euro-area exchanges close hours before New York
      • US news after the European close enters European prices only the next day
    • Consequence, under positive dependence (as between equity markets)
      • the same-day correlation is biased towards zero
      • the lagged correlation is positive
    • Remedies
      • Lower frequency: 2-day or weekly returns on common trading days
      • Add the lead–lag term: \(\rho_{0}+\rho_{+1}\) (\(\rho_0\): same-day correlation, \(\rho_{+1}\): US yesterday with Europe today)
      • A rough approximation of the synchronous correlation, valid only for a one-day lag, no autocorrelation and equal variances
    • Rule used throughout
      • first align the prices on common trading days
      • then compute returns

      Sample Covariance and Inference on a Correlation (1/2): Estimator and Fisher Interval

      Slide 13 in the PDF
      • Sample covariance matrix and Pearson correlation of \(N\) assets over \(T\) days
        • \(\hat\Sigma=\frac{1}{T-1}\sum_t (r_t-\bar r)(r_t-\bar r)^\top\), \(\quad\hat\rho_{ij}=\hat\Sigma_{ij}/\sqrt{\hat\Sigma_{ii}\hat\Sigma_{jj}}\)
        • \(r_t\in\R^N\): vector of the \(N\) returns on day \(t\), \(t=1,\dots,T\)
        • \(\bar r\): vector of sample means
        • \(\hat\Sigma_{ij}\): element \((i,j)\)
        • \(N(N+1)/2\) distinct elements to estimate: 125 250 for \(N=500\)
      • Fisher transform: \(z=\operatorname{artanh}\hat\rho=\tfrac12\ln\frac{1+\hat\rho}{1-\hat\rho}\)
        • approximately Normal with a variance that does not depend on \(\rho\)
        • \(z\approx N\big(\operatorname{artanh}\rho,\,1/(T-3)\big)\) for i.i.d. pairs with the bivariate Normal distribution
        • 95% interval \(\tanh(z\pm1.96/\sqrt{T-3})\)
        • \(T=252\), \(\rho=0.5\): half-width about \(0.09\)

      Sample Covariance and Inference on a Correlation (2/2): Delta Method and HAC

      Slide 14 in the PDF
      • The delta method writes the estimation error \(\hat\rho-\rho\) as a sample mean of daily contributions \(\psi_t\)
      • Volatility clustering does not change this form, only the variance of \(\psi_t\)
        • \(\hat\rho-\rho\approx\frac1T\sum_t\psi_t\), \(\quad\psi_t=\tilde x_t\tilde y_t-\frac\rho2(\tilde x_t^2+\tilde y_t^2)\)
        • \(\tilde x_t\), \(\tilde y_t\): the two returns standardised to mean 0 and variance 1
        • \(\psi_t\): the contribution of day \(t\) to the error
      • Standard error (SE) from the long-run variance of \(\psi_t\)
        • HAC (heteroskedasticity and autocorrelation consistent): Bartlett kernel, \(L\) lags (Newey & West (1987))
        • Needs stationarity, finite fourth moments (questioned in Chapter 1) and weak dependence
        • Lags by the usual rule of thumb \(L=\lfloor4(T/100)^{2/9}\rfloor\): \(L=9\) for \(T=4\,891\), \(L=6\) for \(T=1\,182\), \(L=4\) for a 252-day window
        • i.i.d. Normal pairs give \(\Var(\psi_t)=(1-\rho^2)^2\), the Fisher variance
        • Fat tails and GARCH can change it substantially, in either direction (derivation in the Appendix)
      • SPY–TLT daily, 2002–2021 (\(T=4\,891\), \(\hat\rho=-0.403\)): the SE of \(\hat\rho\)
        • \(0.0120\) (Fisher)
        • \(0.0217\) (fourth moments, \(L=0\))
        • \(0.0227\) (HAC, \(L=9\))

      Fisher versus HAC Intervals: SPY and TLT (1/2)

      Slide 15 in the PDF
        Question for the room If we choose L = 0 (no lags), does the HAC interval for the SPY–TLT correlation coincide with the Fisher interval?
        Answer

        No. On the 252-day window, the median ratio of the HAC width to the Fisher width is 1.17 at L = 0, 1.24 at L = 4 and 1.39 at L = 20. The Fisher formula assumes i.i.d. pairs from the bivariate Normal distribution; L = 0 removes only the lagged terms, not the fourth moments in the standard error. Over 2002–2021 the standard error of the correlation is 0.0120 by Fisher and 0.0217 without lags. With L = 4, the conclusion of the test changes in 8.2% of the windows. The HAC interval itself needs finite fourth moments, which Chapter 1 puts in doubt: HAC handles the dependence but does not compensate for missing moments.

        Rolling Windows

        Slide 17 in the PDF
        • Rolling window of length \(m\): \(\hat\Sigma_t=\frac1m\sum_{s=t-m}^{t-1} r_s r_s^\top\)
          • mean set to zero for daily data
          • \(r_s\): return vector of day \(s\)
          • \(m\): number of past days used
        • Positive semidefinite
          • for any weight vector \(v\), \(v^\top\hat\Sigma_tv=\frac1m\sum_s(v^\top r_s)^2\ge0\)
          • so every portfolio variance is \(\geq0\)
        • Singular if \(m<N\)
          • positive definite only if the \(m\) return vectors span \(\R^N\)
        • Ghost effect: an extreme day enters with weight \(1/m\) and leaves abruptly \(m\) days later
        • The trade-off
          • a short window reacts fast but is noisy
          • a long one is stable but detects a regime change late

        EWMA (RiskMetrics)

        Slide 18 in the PDF
        • EWMA (exponentially weighted moving average, J.P. Morgan/Reuters (1996))
          • \(\Sigma_t=\lambda\,\Sigma_{t-1}+(1-\lambda)\,r_{t-1}r_{t-1}^\top=(1-\lambda)\sum_{k\ge1}\lambda^{k-1} r_{t-k}r_{t-k}^\top\)
          • \(\lambda\in(0,1)\): decay factor
          • \(k\): age of the observation in periods, weight \((1-\lambda)\lambda^{k-1}\)
        • Usual values: \(\lambda=0.94\) for daily and \(\lambda=0.97\) for monthly data
        • Half-life \(\ln0.5/\ln\lambda\)
          • 11.2 periods for \(\lambda=0.94\) and 22.8 for \(\lambda=0.97\)
        • Effective sample \((1+\lambda)/(1-\lambda)\)
          • about 32 days for \(\lambda=0.94\)
        • Positive semidefinite, no ghost effect
        • Limitation: one \(\lambda\) for all elements and no mean reversion
          • i.e. an integrated GARCH(1,1) without a constant (Chapter 5)
        • \(\lambda\) plays the role of \(m\): the same trade-off between reaction speed and noise

          Multivariate GARCH and DCC

          From Univariate to Multivariate Volatility (1/2): the Model

          Slide 20 in the PDF
          • The question of this section: how do we model a covariance matrix that changes every day?
            • the matrix must stay positive definite and have few parameters
          • Chapter 5: one asset, \(r_t=\sigma_t z_t\), \(\sigma_t^2=\omega+\alpha r_{t-1}^2+\beta\sigma_{t-1}^2\)
          • Now \(N\) assets and a covariance matrix
            • \(r_t=H_t^{1/2} z_t\), \(\quad\E(z_t\mid\mathcal F_{t-1})=0\), \(\quad\Var(z_t\mid\mathcal F_{t-1})=I_N\), \(\quad\)so \(\Var(r_t\mid\mathcal F_{t-1})=H_t\)
            • \(r_t\), \(z_t\): vectors of \(N\) returns and innovations
            • \(H_t\): \(N\times N\) conditional covariance matrix, known at \(t-1\) (\(\Sigma_t\) in the previous section)
            • \(H_t^{1/2}\): a matrix square root, \(H_t^{1/2}(H_t^{1/2})^\top=H_t\)
            • \(I_N\): identity matrix
            • \(\mathcal F_{t-1}\): information up to \(t-1\)
          • Any model for \(H_t\) must deliver
            • positive definiteness at every \(t\) (every portfolio has a positive variance)
            • Few parameters
            • Interpretable dynamics
          • Gaussian quasi-log-likelihood, maximised over the parameters of \(H_t\)
            • \(\ell=-\tfrac12\sum_t\big(N\ln 2\pi+\ln|H_t|+r_t^\top H_t^{-1}r_t\big)\)
            • \(|H_t|\): determinant of \(H_t\)
            • Quasi: the estimates stay consistent if \(z_t\) is not Normal but \(H_t\) is right

          From Univariate to Multivariate Volatility (2/2): the Curse of Dimension

          Slide 21 in the PDF
          • VEC (Bollerslev, Engle & Wooldridge (1988)): \(\operatorname{vech}(H_t)=c+A\operatorname{vech}(r_{t-1}r_{t-1}^\top)+B\operatorname{vech}(H_{t-1})\)
            • \(\operatorname{vech}\): the vector of the \(N(N+1)/2\) distinct elements of a symmetric matrix; \(A,B\): square matrices of that size
            • Positive definiteness not guaranteed
          • BEKK (Baba–Engle–Kraft–Kroner, Engle & Kroner (1995)): \(H_t=CC^\top+A^\top r_{t-1}r_{t-1}^\top A+B^\top H_{t-1}B\)
            • \(C\): lower triangular; \(A,B\): \(N\times N\) matrices
            • Positive definite by construction (a sum of quadratic forms), but the elements of \(A\), \(B\) have no direct interpretation (Appendix)
          • DCC: \(3N\) univariate GARCH parameters plus \(a,b\)
            • the target \(\bar Q\) is estimated separately (next slide)
          • The number of parameters grows like \(N^4\) (VEC) and \(N^2\) (BEKK)
            • infeasible for large portfolios

          Constant and Dynamic Conditional Correlation

          Slide 22 in the PDF
          • Decomposition: covariance = volatilities \(\times\) correlations \(\times\) volatilities
            • \(H_t=D_t R_t D_t\), \(\quad D_t=\operatorname{diag}(\sigma_{1t},\dots,\sigma_{Nt})\)
            • \(\sigma_{it}\): GARCH volatility of asset \(i\); \(R_t\): conditional correlation matrix; \(\varepsilon_{it}=r_{it}/\sigma_{it}\): standardised residuals
          • CCC (constant conditional correlation, Bollerslev (1990)): \(R_t=R\) constant
          • DCC (Engle (2002)): a GARCH-type recursion for a proxy matrix \(Q_t\)
            • \(Q_t=(1-a-b)\,\bar Q+a\,\varepsilon_{t-1}\varepsilon_{t-1}^\top+b\,Q_{t-1}\), \(\quad a,b\ge0\), \(a+b<1\)
            • \(R_t=\operatorname{diag}(Q_t)^{-1/2}\,Q_t\,\operatorname{diag}(Q_t)^{-1/2}\)
            • \(\bar Q\): the intercept matrix of the recursion, with \(N(N-1)/2\) free elements. Correlation targeting replaces it by the sample covariance \(\hat{\bar Q}=\frac1T\sum_t\varepsilon_t\varepsilon_t^\top\) (when this is consistent: slide “Two-Step Estimation (2/2)”)
            • \(a\): reaction to yesterday's product of shocks; \(b\): memory; \(a+b\): persistence
            • The second line rescales \(Q_t\) so that its diagonal is 1: \(R_t\) is a valid correlation matrix
          • Two parameters govern all \(N(N-1)/2\) correlations
            • parsimonious, but every pair has the same persistence
          • \(Q_t\) is a covariance proxy, not a correlation: \(\E_{t-1}[\varepsilon_t\varepsilon_t^\top]=R_t\ne Q_t\)

          Two-Step Estimation (1/2): Splitting the Likelihood

          Slide 23 in the PDF
          • The Gaussian log-likelihood splits into a volatility part and a correlation part
            • \(\ell=\underbrace{-\tfrac12\sum_t\big(N\ln2\pi+2\ln|D_t|+r_t^\top D_t^{-2}r_t\big)}_{\ell_V}\;\underbrace{-\tfrac12\sum_t\big(\ln|R_t|+\varepsilon_t^\top R_t^{-1}\varepsilon_t-\varepsilon_t^\top\varepsilon_t\big)}_{\ell_C}\)
            • \(\ell_V\): depends only on the GARCH parameters (volatility part)
            • \(\ell_C\): depends on \((a,b)\) given the volatilities (correlation part)
            • \(|D_t|\), \(|R_t|\): determinants
          • Where the split comes from
            • substitute \(H_t=D_tR_tD_t\) and \(\varepsilon_t=D_t^{-1}r_t\) into the quasi-log-likelihood of the previous section
            • Determinant: \(|H_t|=|D_t|^2\,|R_t|\), so \(\ln|H_t|=2\ln|D_t|+\ln|R_t|\)
            • Quadratic form: \(r_t^\top H_t^{-1}r_t=r_t^\top D_t^{-1}R_t^{-1}D_t^{-1}r_t=\varepsilon_t^\top R_t^{-1}\varepsilon_t\)
            • Add and subtract \(\varepsilon_t^\top\varepsilon_t=r_t^\top D_t^{-2}r_t=\sum_i r_{it}^2/\sigma_{it}^2\): then \(\ell_V=\sum_i\big[-\tfrac12\sum_t(\ln2\pi+\ln\sigma_{it}^2+r_{it}^2/\sigma_{it}^2)\big]\), the sum of the \(N\) univariate Gaussian GARCH log-likelihoods
          • Step 1: fit \(N\) univariate GARCH(1,1) models, keep \(\sigma_{it}\) and \(\varepsilon_{it}\)
          • Step 2: fix \(\bar Q\) at the sample covariance of \(\varepsilon_t\) and maximise \(\ell_C\) over \((a,b)\)

              Crisis Correlations and the Forbes–Rigobon Bias

              Contagion or Interdependence?

              Slide 30 in the PDF
              Lehman Brothers headquarters, New York, 15 September 2008, the day of the bankruptcy filing Photo: Robert Scoble (2008); CC BY 2.0; Wikimedia Commons
              • Common finding: correlations measured during crises are higher than in calm periods
              • A high crisis correlation is not yet contagion
                • it must exceed the calm one
              • Two very different explanations
                • Contagion: the transmission mechanism itself changes
                • Volatility bias: the mechanism is unchanged, but a more volatile source market mechanically raises the correlation
              • Forbes & Rigobon (2002): after correcting the bias, most “contagion” became interdependence
                • in the 1987, 1994 and 1997 crises
              • Longin & Solnik (2001), Ang & Chen (2002): correlations rise in bear markets, not in bull markets
                • an asymmetry the Normal distribution cannot produce

              The Heteroskedasticity Bias (1/2): Correlation in a Calm Period

              Slide 31 in the PDF
              • Model: \(y_t=\alpha+\beta x_t+\varepsilon_t\), \(\Cov(x_t,\varepsilon_t)=0\), \(\beta\) and \(\sigma_\varepsilon^2\) constant
                • \(x_t\): return of the source market
                • \(y_t\): return of the other market
                • \(\beta\): transmission coefficient
                • \(\varepsilon_t\): own shock of \(y\), variance \(\sigma_\varepsilon^2\)
                • \(\sigma_x^2\): variance of \(x\)
              • Two moments of the model
                • \(\Cov(x_t,y_t)=\beta\sigma_x^2\) and \(\Var(y_t)=\beta^2\sigma_x^2+\sigma_\varepsilon^2\), because \(\varepsilon_t\) is uncorrelated with \(x_t\)
              • Correlation: \(\rho^2=\dfrac{\beta^2\sigma_x^2}{\beta^2\sigma_x^2+\sigma_\varepsilon^2}=\Big(1+\dfrac{\sigma_\varepsilon^2}{\beta^2\sigma_x^2}\Big)^{-1}\)
              • The same in one number: the signal-to-noise ratio \(k=\beta^2\sigma_x^2/\sigma_\varepsilon^2\)
                • \(\rho^2=\dfrac{k}{1+k}\), equivalently \(k=\dfrac{\rho^2}{1-\rho^2}\): the correlation depends on \(\sigma_x^2\) only through \(k\)

              The Heteroskedasticity Bias (2/2): From Calm to Crisis

              Slide 32 in the PDF
              • Crisis (subscript \(c\)): \(\sigma_{x,c}^2=(1+\delta)\,\sigma_{x}^2\)
                • \(\delta>0\): the relative rise of the source variance
                • nothing else changes
              • Only \(\sigma_x^2\) grows, so \(k_c=(1+\delta)\,k\) and
                • \(\rho_c^2=\dfrac{k_c}{1+k_c}=\dfrac{(1+\delta)\rho^2/(1-\rho^2)}{1+(1+\delta)\rho^2/(1-\rho^2)}=\dfrac{(1+\delta)\rho^2}{1+\delta\rho^2}\)
              • Then \(\rho_c=\rho\sqrt{\dfrac{1+\delta}{1+\delta\rho^2}}\), so \(|\rho_c|>|\rho|\) for \(0<|\rho|<1\)
                • the measured correlation rises in absolute value although \(\beta\) is the same
              • Inverting: \(\rho^*=\dfrac{\rho_c}{\sqrt{1+\delta(1-\rho_c^2)}}\) is the crisis correlation adjusted for the volatility increase
              • Quick check: the source variance doubles (\(\delta=1\)) and \(\rho=0.5\)
                • \(k=0.25/0.75=1/3\), \(k_c=2/3\), \(\rho_c^2=(2/3)/(5/3)=0.4\), so \(\rho_c=0.632\): a rise of \(0.13\) with no change in \(\beta\)
                • Adjusting: \(\rho^*=0.632/\sqrt{1+1\cdot(1-0.4)}=0.632/1.265=0.5\), the calm value is recovered

                The Forbes–Rigobon Test and Its Inference

                Slide 34 in the PDF
                • Procedure
                  • Choose the source market \(x\), a calm window and a crisis window
                  • Use 2-day returns (closing times differ)
                  • Compute \(\delta=\hat\sigma^2_{x,c}/\hat\sigma^2_{x,calm}-1\) and \(\rho^*\)
                  • Test \(H_0: \rho^*\le\rho_{calm}\) with Fisher \(z\) statistics, the convention of Forbes & Rigobon (2002)
                • Inference
                  • With \(\delta\) fixed, \(\Var(\operatorname{artanh}\rho^*)\approx1/[n_c(1+\delta(1-\rho_c^2))]<1/n_c\) (\(n_c\): crisis observations): the Fisher test is conservative
                  • \(\delta\) is estimated and 2-day returns overlap: resample the calm and crisis windows jointly (stationary bootstrap, mean block 10 days, \(B=1\,999\) draws) and test \(D=\rho^*-\rho_{calm}\)
                  • Stationary bootstrap: it resamples blocks of consecutive days with random starts and geometric random lengths, which keeps the serial dependence inside each block
                Assumptions to remember
                • No omitted common shock
                  • a global factor hitting both markets raises \(\sigma_\varepsilon\) as well as \(\sigma_x\)
                  • and the adjustment over-corrects (Corsetti, Pericoli & Sbracia (2005); Appendix)
                • No feedback from \(y\) to \(x\) (no endogeneity)
                • The choice of windows matters: results can flip with a few weeks
                • The residual variance \(\sigma_\varepsilon^2\) does not change between periods

                    Why Correlation Is Not Enough

                    Slide 38 in the PDF
                    • Embrechts, McNeil & Straumann (2002): three fallacies about linear correlation
                    • Correlation measures only linear dependence
                      • it is not invariant to monotone transformations: \(\Corr(x,y)\ne\Corr(e^x,e^y)\)
                    • Margins and correlation do not determine the joint distribution
                      • Two portfolios with identical margins and \(\rho\) can have very different Value-at-Risk (Chapter 7)
                    • Attainable correlations: for given margins, \(\rho\in[\rho_{\min},\rho_{\max}]\)
                      • reached by countermonotone and comonotone pairs
                      • Lognormal \(X=e^{\sigma_1Z}\), \(Y=e^{\pm\sigma_2Z}\), \(Z\) standard Normal (\(+\): comonotone, \(-\): countermonotone): \(\rho_{\max}=\dfrac{e^{\sigma_1\sigma_2}-1}{\sqrt{(e^{\sigma_1^2}-1)(e^{\sigma_2^2}-1)}}\), \(\rho_{\min}\) with \(e^{-\sigma_1\sigma_2}\) in the numerator
                      • \(\sigma_1=1\), \(\sigma_2=3\): \(\rho_{\max}=0.162\) and \(\rho_{\min}=-0.008\): perfectly dependent, yet almost uncorrelated

                    Sklar's Theorem: Margins plus Copula

                    Slide 39 in the PDF
                    • A copula is a joint distribution function on \([0,1]^d\) with uniform margins
                    • Sklar (1959): for any joint distribution \(F\) with margins \(F_1,\dots,F_d\) there is a copula \(C\)
                      • with \(F(x_1,\dots,x_d)=C\big(F_1(x_1),\dots,F_d(x_d)\big)\)
                      • \(d\): number of variables
                      • \(F_i\): marginal distribution function of \(x_i\)
                      • \(u_i=F_i(x_i)\in[0,1]\): its probability level
                      • \(C\) is unique if the margins are continuous
                      • Conversely, any copula and any margins give a valid joint distribution
                      • With densities: \(f(x)=c\big(F_1(x_1),\dots,F_d(x_d)\big)\prod_i f_i(x_i)\), \(c\) the copula density, \(f_i\) the marginal densities (the comonotone copula has none)
                    • Margins carry volatility, skewness and fat tails of each asset (Chapters 1 and 5)
                    • The copula carries all the dependence
                      • it is invariant to increasing transformations of each variable
                    • Three reference copulas and the Fréchet–Hoeffding bounds \(W\le C\le M\) (Nelsen (2006))
                      • Independence \(\Pi(u,v)=uv\)
                      • Comonotonicity \(M(u,v)=\min(u,v)\)
                      • Countermonotonicity \(W(u,v)=\max(u+v-1,0)\)

                      • \((X_1,Y_1)\), \((X_2,Y_2)\): two independent draws of the pair
                      • Concordant: both coordinates move in the same direction

                    • Both lie in \([-1,1]\) and depend only on the copula: 1 for \(M\), 0 for \(\Pi\), \(-1\) for \(W\)

                        • Efficient, but computationally demanding and sensitive to misspecification
                      • IFM (inference functions for margins, Joe (2005)): two steps
                        • Fit parametric margins (e.g. GARCH with Student t)
                        • Set \(\hat u_{it}=\hat F_i(x_{it})\) (\(\hat F_i\): fitted marginal distribution function)
                        • Maximise \(\sum_t\ln c(\hat u_{1t},\hat u_{2t};\theta)\), \(c\) the copula density
                      • CML (canonical maximum likelihood, Genest, Ghoudi & Rivest (1995)): no parametric margins
                        • Pseudo-observations \(\hat u_{it}=\operatorname{rank}(x_{it})/(T+1)\)
                        • Then maximise the copula likelihood
                        • Robust to misspecified margins
                        • The \(T+1\) keeps \(\hat u\) away from 0 and 1
                      • For returns: filter each series with GARCH first
                        • the static CML inference shown here assumes i.i.d. innovations; GARCH filtering alone does not guarantee this
                        • without filtering, volatility clustering would mix dependence with changing volatility
                        • then use the ranks of the standardised residuals (CML, the method used from here on)
                      • Model choice: most of the families compared are not nested
                        • for Gaussian against t we use an LR test calibrated by bootstrap at \(\nu=\infty\) (Seminar 6, B2)
                        • we use AIC \(=2k-2\ell\) (Akaike information criterion), BIC \(=k\ln T-2\ell\) (Bayesian information criterion)
                        • \(k\): number of parameters, \(\ell\): maximised log-likelihood

                                    DCC in Vast Dimensions: Does the Estimation Survive Many Assets?

                                    Slide 61 in the PDF
                                    • The question of Pakel, Shephard, Sheppard & Engle (2021): can the dynamic DCC parameters \((a,b)\) be estimated on hundreds of assets?
                                      • the VaR of a large portfolio and hedging need the conditional correlations of all assets
                                    • The claim of the paper, for scalar BEKK and cDCC: with correlation targeting, the full likelihood gives strongly biased \((\hat a,\hat b)\) as \(K\) grows
                                      • \(K\): the number of assets; the target \(\bar Q\) has \(K(K-1)/2\) correlations, estimated before \((a,b)\)
                                      • the number of these incidental parameters grows with \(K\), and their errors accumulate in the likelihood
                                      • every evaluation of the likelihood inverts \(T\) matrices of size \(K\times K\): the cost grows with \(K^3\)
                                    • The remedy: the composite likelihood (CL), the sum of the bivariate likelihoods of pairs of assets
                                      • \(\ell_{CL}(a,b)=\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal P}\ell_{ij}(a,b)\); each pair uses its own \(2\times2\) block of \(\hat{\bar Q}\)
                                      • \(\mathcal P\): all \(K(K-1)/2\) pairs or only the contiguous pairs \((1,2),(2,3),\dots,(K-1,K)\)
                                      • no large matrix is inverted, and the theory of the paper lets \(K\) grow with \(T\)
                                    • Data of the paper: the stocks of the S&P 100 and of the S&P 500, daily, 1997–2006

                                    DCC Estimates as the Number of Assets Grows

                                    Slide 62 in the PDF
                                      Question for the room Why does the composite likelihood (CL) depend less than the full likelihood on the errors of the target Q̄ as the number of assets K grows?
                                      Answer

                                      Each CL term uses only a 2 × 2 block of the target. The target Q̄ has K(K − 1)/2 correlations estimated before (a, b), i.e. 1,128 at K = 48, and the full likelihood uses the whole matrix with all its errors. The CL pairs are not independent samples: the bivariate terms are computed from the same data. The estimates do not move monotonically: the full-likelihood â is 0.0099 at K = 2, 0.0157 at K = 5 and 0.0077 at K = 48, while the all-pairs CL â rises to 0.0362. With the full-likelihood estimates the mean correlation of the 48 assets has a standard deviation over time of 0.034, against 0.058 with CL, and the heuristic persistence measure ln 0.5 / ln(a + b) is 99 days, against 31. The difference is consistent with the mechanism above, but the data alone do not show which estimate is closer to the truth. The simulations of Pakel, Shephard, Sheppard and Engle (2021), for scalar BEKK and cDCC, support the advantage of CL in the models and settings they study; they do not show the absence of bias in this adaptation on the standard DCC.

                                        Inference on a Correlation, the Delta Method

                                        Slide 90 in the PDF
                                        • \(\hat\rho=g(\hat m)\), with \(\hat m\) the sample mean of \(m_t=(x_t,\,y_t,\,x_t^2,\,y_t^2,\,x_ty_t)\)
                                          • \(g\): the function that turns these five moments into the correlation; \(\nabla g\): its gradient
                                        • Delta method: \(\sqrt T(\hat\rho-\rho)\to N\big(0,\ \nabla g^\top\Omega\,\nabla g\big)\)
                                          • \(\Omega\): the long-run covariance of \(m_t\), estimated by HAC with a Bartlett kernel and \(L\) lags (Newey & West (1987))
                                        • The equivalent influence-function form, \(\hat\rho-\rho\approx\frac1T\sum_t\psi_t\), is the one of the main text
                                        • Conditions: stationarity, finite moments of order \(4+\delta\) of \((x_t,y_t)\) and weak dependence (mixing)
                                          • with infinite fourth moments the Normal limit fails
                                          • nor does the ordinary bootstrap automatically fix missing moments: this applies to the Forbes–Rigobon bootstrap and to tests on squared losses too
                                        • The HAC interval, built on the Fisher scale: \(\tanh\big(\operatorname{artanh}\hat\rho\pm1.96\,\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}_{\mathrm{HAC}}/(1-\hat\rho^2)\big)\)
                                          • with \(\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}_{\mathrm{HAC}}=\sqrt{\nabla\hat g^\top\hat\Omega\,\nabla\hat g/T}\)
                                        • The classic Fisher interval replaces \(\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}_{\mathrm{HAC}}/(1-\hat\rho^2)\) by \(1/\sqrt{T-3}\)
                                        • The last 252-day window (the 5-day series) ends on 17 September 2026
                                          • \(\hat\rho=0.245\), Fisher interval [\(0.13\), \(0.36\)], HAC interval [\(0.07\), \(0.41\)]
                                          • largest HAC-to-Fisher width ratio: \(2.02\)

                                        Two-Step Estimators, the Correct Covariance

                                        Slide 92 in the PDF
                                        • Stacked estimating equations
                                          • \(\sum_t s_{1t}(\theta_1)=0\) (step 1: GARCH and, with correlation targeting, \(\operatorname{vech}\bar Q\))
                                          • \(\sum_t s_{2t}(\theta_1,\theta_2)=0\) (step 2, \(\theta_2=(a,b)\))
                                        • \(s_{jt}\): score of step \(j\) on day \(t\); \(A_{jk}=\E\big[\partial s_{jt}/\partial\theta_k^\top\big]\)
                                          • \(A_{21}\) measures how the correlation score reacts to the GARCH parameters
                                        • Newey & McFadden (1994), Section 6: \(\sqrt T(\hat\theta_2-\theta_2)\to N(0,V_2)\)
                                          • \(V_2=A_{22}^{-1}\,\Omega\,A_{22}^{-1\top}\), with \(\Omega\) the long-run variance of the adjusted score \(u_t=s_{2t}-A_{21}A_{11}^{-1}s_{1t}\)
                                        • Step-2 standard errors keep only \(s_{2t}\)
                                          • they drop \(A_{21}A_{11}^{-1}s_{1t}\), the GARCH estimation error carried into step 2
                                        • The target moment is not a martingale difference: \(\E_{t-1}[\varepsilon_t\varepsilon_t^\top-\bar Q]=R_t-\bar Q\)
                                          • so \(\Omega\) is estimated by HAC
                                          • the contemporaneous variance of \(u_t\) suffices only if the whole system, the target moment included, is a martingale difference
                                        • Engle & Sheppard (2001) derive \(V_2\) for DCC
                                        • A simple alternative bootstraps the whole two-step procedure (next slide)
