For twenty years, when United States (US) stocks fell, long-term US Treasury bonds tended to rise. That is why a portfolio of 60% stocks and 40% bonds was calmer than either part on its own. In 2022 this link reversed: since then, the two have often fallen together.

The difference shows up directly in risk. With the 2002–2021 link, the 60/40 portfolio has an annual volatility of 10.4%. With the 2022–2026 link, the same portfolio has 13.2%. The risk of each asset is the same in both computations; only the way they move together has changed.

The computation uses two exchange-traded funds (ETFs) of the United States (US). SPY (SPDR S&P 500 ETF Trust, where SPDR stands for Standard & Poor's Depositary Receipts) tracks the S&P 500 (Standard & Poor's 500) index of 500 large US companies. TLT (iShares 20+ Year Treasury Bond ETF) holds US Treasury bonds with maturities above 20 years.

The chapter studies this joint movement, measured first of all by the correlation. It answers four questions:

  1. How precisely can we measure a correlation from daily returns?
  2. How do we track a correlation that changes from one day to the next?
  3. Do correlations really rise in crises, or do they only seem to rise because markets become more turbulent?
  4. What happens on extreme days, which the correlation does not describe well?

The last question has a clear example. When the bank JPMorgan Chase & Co. (stock ticker JPM) has one of its 5% worst days, Bank of America Corporation (stock ticker BAC) is also in its own worst 5% about 61% of the time. Large losses come together far more often than an ordinary correlation would suggest.

Motivation: Dependence between Markets

Chapter 5 modelled the risk of a single asset. This chapter looks at several assets at once and asks how they move together. The object of study is the covariance matrix \(\Sigma\) of returns: the variances sit on the diagonal, the covariances off it, and the correlations are obtained from them.

Correlation matters in three situations: when we compute the risk of a portfolio, when we hedge a position and when we judge whether a crisis spreads from one market to another. In all three, the correlation is not observed directly. It is estimated from data and it changes over time.

Why Dependence Matters (1/2): Portfolio Diversification

Slide 4 in the PDF

Diversification reduces risk only if the assets do not move perfectly together (Markowitz, 1952). For two assets with weights \(w\) and \(1-w\), volatilities \(\sigma_1\), \(\sigma_2\) and correlation \(\rho\), the portfolio variance is

\[\sigma_p^2 = w^\top\Sigma w = w^2\sigma_1^2+(1-w)^2\sigma_2^2+2w(1-w)\rho\,\sigma_1\sigma_2 .\]
(6.1)

The correlation appears in a single term of (6.1), the last one. With positive weights, a higher correlation means a riskier portfolio. A negative correlation makes the term negative, so the two assets partly offset each other's losses.

The chapter's example uses two exchange-traded funds (ETFs): SPY, on the S&P 500 stock index, and TLT, on US Treasury bonds with maturities above 20 years. Over July 2002 – September 2026, the annual volatility is 18.8% for SPY and 14.3% for TLT.

A 60/40 portfolio of the two has a volatility of 10.4% if we use the 2002–2021 correlation, equal to \(-0.40\). With the 2022–2026 correlation, equal to \(0.11\), the volatility rises to 13.2%. The volatilities of the two funds are the same in both computations; only the correlation differs.

Splitting the sample in January 2022, at the start of the inflation shock, is a convention for the illustration. A formal test, presented in Further Topics, places the change earlier, in August 2020.

In brief

  • Diversification (Markowitz (1952))
    • The portfolio variance depends on the correlation through a single cross term
    • \(\sigma_p^2=w^\top\Sigma w=w^2\sigma_1^2+(1-w)^2\sigma_2^2+2w(1-w)\rho\,\sigma_1\sigma_2\)
    • \(w\): weight of asset 1 (\(1-w\) in asset 2)
    • \(\Sigma\): covariance matrix
    • \(\sigma_1\), \(\sigma_2\): volatilities
    • \(\rho\): correlation
    • Portfolio 60/40 of SPY (S&P 500 exchange-traded fund) and TLT (20+ year US Treasury bond fund)

    • We split at January 2022, the start of the inflation shock; the break test in Further Topics dates the change earlier, to August 2020

Why Dependence Matters (2/2): Hedges, Contagion and Moving Covariances

Slide 5 in the PDF

The second use of the correlation is hedging. We hold a position \(S\) and want to reduce its risk by selling another instrument \(F\), for example a futures contract. The question is how much of \(F\) to sell. The answer that makes the variance of the hedged position as small as possible is the minimum-variance hedge ratio:

\[h^* = \rho\,\frac{\sigma_S}{\sigma_F},\]
(6.2)

that is, the slope of the regression of the return \(r_S\) on the return \(r_F\). The ratio in (6.2) is in value: how many units of value of \(F\) per unit of value of \(S\). To obtain the number of contracts, multiply by \(P_S/P_F\), the ratio of the prices, contract size included.

With the optimal ratio, the hedge removes the share \(\rho^2\) of the variance: the larger the correlation in absolute value, the more risk the hedge removes. If the correlation changes, yesterday's ratio is no longer optimal today. A hedge that adjusts over time needs a correlation that adjusts over time, \(\rho_t\).

The third use concerns crises. Forbes & Rigobon (2002) call contagion a significant increase in the links between markets after a shock. Interdependence means the links that are always present, through trade, common investors or common macroeconomic factors. Correlations tend to rise exactly when diversification would be most useful. The chapter asks how much of this rise is genuine.

The three uses share one point, which is also the new idea of the chapter: the covariance matrix is not fixed. It takes a different value at each date \(t\), written \(\Sigma_t\).

In brief

  • Hedging
    • How much value of the hedge \(F\) to sell per unit of value held in the position \(S\) to minimise the variance
    • Minimum-variance hedge ratio \(h^*=\rho\,\sigma_S/\sigma_F\): the slope of the regression of \(r_S\) on \(r_F\). Estimated on returns, it is a ratio of values: units of \(F\) per unit of \(S\) are \(h^*P_S/P_F\) (\(P_S\), \(P_F\): prices, contract size included)
    • \(r_S\), \(r_F\): returns of the position and of the hedge
    • \(\sigma_S\), \(\sigma_F\): their volatilities
    • Effectiveness \(\rho^2\): the share of the variance removed
    • If the correlation moves, yesterday's hedge is wrong today: a dynamic hedge needs a dynamic \(\rho_t\)
  • Contagion and interdependence (Forbes & Rigobon (2002))
    • Contagion: a significant increase in cross-market linkages after a shock
    • Interdependence: links present in all states (trade, common investors, common macro factors)
    • Correlations tend to rise exactly when diversification is needed most
  • New in this chapter: \(\Sigma_t\), the covariance matrix at date \(t\), moves

    Measuring Covariance and Its Uncertainty

    Before modelling a covariance matrix that changes over time, we need to know how to measure it. This section shows why the daily correlation between distant markets comes out too low, how large the error of an estimated correlation is, and how to estimate today's correlation from recent days.

    How Precise Is an Estimated Correlation?

    Slide 8 in the PDF

    Estimating \(\Sigma\) and its uncertainty has three traps:

    1. Closing times differ from one market to another, so the measured correlation depends on the frequency of the data.
    2. Daily returns have fat tails, and turbulent periods come in clusters (volatility clustering). Both features change the uncertainty of the estimate, and the classical interval ignores them.
    3. The length of the estimation period decides how quickly we notice a change of regime.

    In brief

    • The question of this section: how do we estimate \(\Sigma\), and how uncertain is each estimated correlation?
    • First trap: closing times differ, so the data frequency changes the correlation
    • Second trap: fat tails and volatility clustering can change the uncertainty substantially, and the Fisher interval ignores them
    • Third trap: the window length decides how fast we see a regime change

    Asynchronous Trading and the Bias of Daily Correlations

    Slide 9 in the PDF

    The Bucharest Stock Exchange and the euro-area exchanges close several hours before the New York exchange. A US news item released after the European close enters European prices only the next day. This lag is called asynchronous trading.

    When two markets generally move in the same direction, the lag has two effects. The same-day correlation comes out lower than the true link. The lagged correlation, between the US yesterday and Europe today, comes out positive, because part of the European reaction arrives one day late.

    There are two remedies:

    1. Use returns over longer periods, 2-day or weekly. Over a longer period, a one-day delay matters less.
    2. Add the lagged correlation to the same-day one: \(\rho_0+\rho_{+1}\). Here \(\rho_0\) is the same-day correlation and \(\rho_{+1}\) the correlation between yesterday's US returns and today's European returns. The sum is only a rough approximation. It assumes a one-day lag, no autocorrelation and equal variances.

    The rule used throughout the chapter: first align prices on the days when both exchanges traded, and only then compute returns.

    In brief

    • Closing times differ
      • the Bucharest Stock Exchange and the euro-area exchanges close hours before New York
      • US news after the European close enters European prices only the next day
    • Consequence, under positive dependence (as between equity markets)
      • the same-day correlation is biased towards zero
      • the lagged correlation is positive
    • Remedies
      • Lower frequency: 2-day or weekly returns on common trading days
      • Add the lead–lag term: \(\rho_{0}+\rho_{+1}\) (\(\rho_0\): same-day correlation, \(\rho_{+1}\): US yesterday with Europe today)
      • The sum \(\rho_0+\rho_{+1}\) is a rough approximation of the synchronous correlation, valid only for a one-day lag, no autocorrelation and equal variances
    • Rule used throughout
      • first align the prices on common trading days
      • then compute returns

      Sample Covariance and Inference on a Correlation (1/2): Estimator and Fisher Interval

      Slide 13 in the PDF

      Let \(r_t\in\mathbb{R}^N\) be the vector of the \(N\) returns on day \(t\), \(t=1,\dots,T\), and \(\bar r\) the vector of means. The sample covariance matrix and the Pearson correlation of assets \(i\) and \(j\) are

      \[\hat\Sigma=\frac{1}{T-1}\sum_t (r_t-\bar r)(r_t-\bar r)^\top, \qquad \hat\rho_{ij}=\frac{\hat\Sigma_{ij}}{\sqrt{\hat\Sigma_{ii}\hat\Sigma_{jj}}} .\]

      The matrix has \(N(N+1)/2\) distinct elements. For \(N=500\) assets, that means 125,250 quantities to estimate: their number grows with the square of the number of assets.

      How reliable is a single estimated correlation? The classical answer uses the Fisher transformation, \(z=\operatorname{artanh}\hat\rho=\tfrac12\ln\frac{1+\hat\rho}{1-\hat\rho}\). It puts the correlation on a scale on which the error has roughly the same size, whatever \(\rho\) is. If days are independent and returns are Normal,

      \[z\approx N\!\big(\operatorname{artanh}\rho,\ 1/(T-3)\big), \qquad 95\%\text{ interval: } \tanh\!\big(z\pm1.96/\sqrt{T-3}\big).\]
      (6.3)

      For one year of data, \(T=252\), and \(\rho=0.5\), the interval (6.3) has a half-width of about \(0.09\). The interval is not symmetric around the estimated correlation, because the function \(\tanh\) compresses values close to \(\pm1\).

      In brief

      • Sample covariance matrix and Pearson correlation of \(N\) assets over \(T\) days
        • \(\hat\Sigma=\frac{1}{T-1}\sum_t (r_t-\bar r)(r_t-\bar r)^\top\), \(\quad\hat\rho_{ij}=\hat\Sigma_{ij}/\sqrt{\hat\Sigma_{ii}\hat\Sigma_{jj}}\)
        • \(r_t\in\R^N\): vector of the \(N\) returns on day \(t\), \(t=1,\dots,T\)
        • \(\bar r\): vector of sample means
        • \(\hat\Sigma_{ij}\): element \((i,j)\)
        • \(N(N+1)/2\) distinct elements to estimate: 125 250 for \(N=500\)
      • Fisher transform: \(z=\operatorname{artanh}\hat\rho=\tfrac12\ln\frac{1+\hat\rho}{1-\hat\rho}\)
        • approximately Normal with a variance that does not depend on \(\rho\)
        • \(z\approx N\big(\operatorname{artanh}\rho,\,1/(T-3)\big)\) for i.i.d. pairs with the bivariate Normal distribution
        • 95% interval \(\tanh(z\pm1.96/\sqrt{T-3})\)
        • \(T=252\), \(\rho=0.5\): half-width about \(0.09\)

      Sample Covariance and Inference on a Correlation (2/2): Delta Method and HAC

      Slide 14 in the PDF

      Daily returns are neither independent nor Normal, so we need another formula for the error. The delta method writes the error of the estimated correlation as an average of daily contributions. With \(\tilde x_t\), \(\tilde y_t\) the two returns standardised to mean 0 and variance 1,

      \[\hat\rho-\rho\approx\frac1T\sum_t\psi_t, \qquad \psi_t=\tilde x_t\tilde y_t-\frac\rho2\big(\tilde x_t^2+\tilde y_t^2\big),\]
      (6.4)

      where \(\psi_t\) is the contribution of day \(t\) to the error. The standard error (SE) of the correlation comes from the variance of these contributions.

      Two features of the data change this variance. Fat tails make the contributions more variable. Clustered turbulent periods make the contributions of nearby days depend on each other. The HAC (heteroskedasticity and autocorrelation consistent) estimator accounts for both (Newey & West, 1987). It adds up the covariances of contributions up to \(L\) days apart, with Bartlett weights. The usual rule \(L=\lfloor4(T/100)^{2/9}\rfloor\) gives \(L=9\) for \(T=4{,}891\) and \(L=4\) for a 252-day window.

      For independent Normal data, the variance of the contributions is \((1-\rho^2)^2\), exactly the Fisher variance; the derivation is in the Appendix. The HAC standard error, however, requires finite fourth moments, and Chapter 1 casts doubt on this condition.

      For SPY–TLT in 2002–2021, with \(T=4{,}891\) and \(\hat\rho=-0.403\), the standard error is 0.0120 by the Fisher formula. If we account only for fat tails (\(L=0\)), it rises to 0.0217. With HAC and \(L=9\), it is 0.0227. Almost all of the increase therefore comes from fat tails, not from dependence between days.

      In brief

      • Delta method: linearise \(\hat\rho\), a smooth function of sample moments, and write the error \(\hat\rho-\rho\) as a mean of daily contributions \(\psi_t\)
        • \(\hat\rho-\rho\approx\frac1T\sum_t\psi_t\), \(\quad\psi_t=\tilde x_t\tilde y_t-\frac\rho2(\tilde x_t^2+\tilde y_t^2)\)

        Rolling Windows

        Slide 17 in the PDF

        How do we estimate today's covariance matrix? The simplest answer uses the last \(m\) days, with equal weights. With the mean set to zero, as is usual for daily data,

        \[\hat\Sigma_t=\frac1m\sum_{s=t-m}^{t-1} r_s r_s^\top ,\]
        (6.5)

        where \(r_s\) is the vector of returns on day \(s\). This estimator never gives a portfolio a negative variance: for any weight vector \(v\), \(v^\top\hat\Sigma_tv=\frac1m\sum_s(v^\top r_s)^2\ge0\). The matrix is singular, however, whenever the window has fewer days than assets, \(m<N\).

        Equal weights have a defect called the ghost effect. An extreme day stays in the estimate with the same weight for \(m\) days, then drops out abruptly, and the estimate jumps although the market has not changed. The window length sets a trade-off. A short window reacts fast but is noisy. A long one is stable but notices a change of regime late.

        In brief

        • Rolling window of length \(m\): \(\hat\Sigma_t=\frac1m\sum_{s=t-m}^{t-1} r_s r_s^\top\)
          • mean set to zero for daily data
          • \(r_s\): return vector of day \(s\)
          • \(m\): number of past days used
        • Positive semidefinite
          • for any weight vector \(v\), \(v^\top\hat\Sigma_tv=\frac1m\sum_s(v^\top r_s)^2\ge0\)
          • so every portfolio variance is \(\geq0\)
        • Singular if \(m<N\)
          • positive definite only if the \(m\) return vectors span \(\R^N\)
          • a singular matrix cannot be inverted, so it gives no minimum-variance weights
        • Ghost effect: an extreme day enters with weight \(1/m\) and leaves abruptly \(m\) days later
          • the estimate then jumps although the market has not changed
        • The trade-off
          • a short window reacts fast but is noisy
          • a long one is stable but detects a regime change late

        EWMA (RiskMetrics)

        Slide 18 in the PDF

        The exponentially weighted moving average (EWMA), used in RiskMetrics (J.P. Morgan/Reuters, 1996), replaces the equal weights. Each day counts slightly less than the day after it:

        \[\Sigma_t=\lambda\,\Sigma_{t-1}+(1-\lambda)\,r_{t-1}r_{t-1}^\top=(1-\lambda)\sum_{k\ge1}\lambda^{k-1} r_{t-k}r_{t-k}^\top ,\]
        (6.6)

        where \(\lambda\in(0,1)\) is the decay factor. The observation from \(k\) days ago has weight \((1-\lambda)\lambda^{k-1}\). The usual values are \(\lambda=0.94\) for daily data and \(\lambda=0.97\) for monthly data. With \(\lambda=0.94\), the weights halve every 11.2 days, and the estimate effectively uses about 32 days.

        Because the weights decline gradually, EWMA has no ghost effect. Its limitations: a single \(\lambda\) governs all variances and covariances, and the estimate does not revert to a long-run mean: (6.6) is an integrated GARCH(1,1) without a constant (Chapter 5). The factor \(\lambda\) plays the role of the window length \(m\), with the same trade-off between speed and noise.

        In brief

        • EWMA (exponentially weighted moving average, J.P. Morgan/Reuters (1996))
          • \(\Sigma_t=\lambda\,\Sigma_{t-1}+(1-\lambda)\,r_{t-1}r_{t-1}^\top=(1-\lambda)\sum_{k\ge1}\lambda^{k-1} r_{t-k}r_{t-k}^\top\)
          • \(\lambda\in(0,1)\): decay factor
          • \(k\): age of the observation in periods, weight \((1-\lambda)\lambda^{k-1}\)
        • Usual values: \(\lambda=0.94\) for daily and \(\lambda=0.97\) for monthly data
        • Half-life \(\ln0.5/\ln\lambda\): the number of periods after which an observation's weight halves
          • 11.2 periods for \(\lambda=0.94\) and 22.8 for \(\lambda=0.97\)
        • Effective sample \((1+\lambda)/(1-\lambda)\)
          • about 32 days for \(\lambda=0.94\)
        • Positive semidefinite, no ghost effect
        • Limitation: one \(\lambda\) for all elements and no mean reversion (forecasts do not converge to a long-run level)
          • i.e. an integrated GARCH(1,1) without a constant (Chapter 5)
        • \(\lambda\) plays the role of \(m\): the same trade-off between reaction speed and noise

          Multivariate GARCH and DCC

          This section builds a model in which the covariance matrix changes every day by a rule estimated from the data. The GARCH model of Chapter 5 extends directly to a matrix, but two problems appear: the number of parameters explodes, and the resulting matrix can give some portfolios a negative variance.

          The DCC (dynamic conditional correlation) model of Engle (2002) solves both problems. Its idea is to separate the volatilities from the correlations. Each asset has its own GARCH model for volatility, and all correlations are driven by only two parameters.

          From Univariate to Multivariate Volatility (1/2): the Model

          Slide 20 in the PDF

          In Chapter 5, the return of a single asset was \(r_t=\sigma_t z_t\), with \(\sigma_t^2=\omega+\alpha r_{t-1}^2+\beta\sigma_{t-1}^2\). With \(N\) assets, the volatility \(\sigma_t\) becomes a covariance matrix \(H_t\), and the vector of returns is written

          \[r_t=H_t^{1/2} z_t, \qquad \mathbb{E}(z_t\mid\mathcal F_{t-1})=0, \quad \operatorname{Var}(z_t\mid\mathcal F_{t-1})=I_N,\]

          so that \(\operatorname{Var}(r_t\mid\mathcal F_{t-1})=H_t\). Here \(z_t\) are the standardised shocks, \(\mathcal F_{t-1}\) is the information available up to yesterday, and \(I_N\) is the identity matrix. \(H_t\) is the \(\Sigma_t\) of the previous section, and \(H_t^{1/2}\) is a square root of it: \(H_t^{1/2}(H_t^{1/2})^\top=H_t\).

          A good model for \(H_t\) must meet three requirements:

          1. \(H_t\) is positive definite at every \(t\), so every portfolio has a positive variance.
          2. The number of parameters stays small.
          3. The parameters have a clear interpretation.

          The parameters are estimated by maximising the Gaussian quasi-log-likelihood:

          \[\ell=-\tfrac12\sum_t\big(N\ln 2\pi+\ln|H_t|+r_t^\top H_t^{-1}r_t\big),\]
          (6.7)

          where \(|H_t|\) is the determinant of \(H_t\). The prefix "quasi" says that the Normal formula can be used even when the shocks are not Normal: the estimates remain consistent if the model for \(H_t\) is correct.

          Constant and Dynamic Conditional Correlation

          Slide 22 in the PDF

          DCC starts from an identity: a covariance matrix can be written from volatilities and correlations,

          \[H_t=D_t R_t D_t, \qquad D_t=\operatorname{diag}(\sigma_{1t},\dots,\sigma_{Nt}).\]

          Here \(\sigma_{it}\) is the GARCH volatility of asset \(i\) and \(R_t\) is the correlation matrix. Dividing each return by its volatility gives the standardised residuals \(\varepsilon_{it}=r_{it}/\sigma_{it}\).

          The simplest model, the constant conditional correlation (CCC) model of Bollerslev (1990), assumes that \(R_t=R\) does not change. The DCC model of Engle (2002) lets the correlations vary too. It applies a GARCH-type recursion to an auxiliary matrix \(Q_t\) and then turns it into a correlation matrix \(R_t\):

          \[Q_t=(1-a-b)\,\bar Q+a\,\varepsilon_{t-1}\varepsilon_{t-1}^\top+b\,Q_{t-1},\]
          (6.8)
          \[R_t=\operatorname{diag}(Q_t)^{-1/2}\,Q_t\,\operatorname{diag}(Q_t)^{-1/2},\]

          with \(a,b\ge0\) and \(a+b<1\). The parameters read as in GARCH. \(a\) measures how much the correlation reacts to yesterday's joint shocks, \(b\) how much it remembers of the past, and \(a+b\) how persistent it is. The second equation divides \(Q_t\) by its own diagonal, so that \(R_t\) has ones on the diagonal and is a valid correlation matrix.

          The matrix \(\bar Q\) is the level to which the recursion reverts. It has \(N(N-1)/2\) free elements. In practice it is replaced by the sample covariance of the standardised residuals, \(\hat{\bar Q}=\frac1T\sum_t\varepsilon_t\varepsilon_t^\top\); this is called correlation targeting. Two parameters then drive all \(N(N-1)/2\) correlations. The model is very economical, but it imposes the same persistence on every pair of assets.

          One subtlety matters further on: \(Q_t\) is not the expected correlation matrix. The expected correlation is \(R_t\), so \(\mathbb{E}_{t-1}[\varepsilon_t\varepsilon_t^\top]=R_t\ne Q_t\). The slide Two-Step Estimation (2/2) shows why this matters.

          In brief

          • Decomposition: covariance = volatilities \(\times\) correlations \(\times\) volatilities
            • \(H_t=D_t R_t D_t\), \(\quad D_t=\operatorname{diag}(\sigma_{1t},\dots,\sigma_{Nt})\)
            • \(\sigma_{it}\): GARCH volatility of asset \(i\); \(R_t\): conditional correlation matrix; \(\varepsilon_{it}=r_{it}/\sigma_{it}\): standardised residuals
          • CCC (constant conditional correlation, Bollerslev (1990)): \(R_t=R\) constant
          • DCC (Engle (2002)): a GARCH-type recursion for a proxy matrix \(Q_t\)
            • \(Q_t=(1-a-b)\,\bar Q+a\,\varepsilon_{t-1}\varepsilon_{t-1}^\top+b\,Q_{t-1}\), \(\quad a,b\ge0\), \(a+b<1\)
            • \(R_t=\operatorname{diag}(Q_t)^{-1/2}\,Q_t\,\operatorname{diag}(Q_t)^{-1/2}\)
            • \(\bar Q\): the intercept matrix of the recursion, with \(N(N-1)/2\) free elements. Correlation targeting replaces it by the sample covariance \(\hat{\bar Q}=\frac1T\sum_t\varepsilon_t\varepsilon_t^\top\) (when this is consistent: slide “Two-Step Estimation (2/2)”)
            • \(a\): reaction to yesterday's product of shocks; \(b\): memory; \(a+b\): persistence, i.e. how slowly the correlation returns to the target
            • The second line rescales \(Q_t\) so that its diagonal is 1: \(R_t\) is a valid correlation matrix
          • Two parameters govern all \(N(N-1)/2\) correlations
            • parsimonious, but every pair has the same persistence
          • \(Q_t\) is a covariance proxy, not a correlation: \(\E_{t-1}[\varepsilon_t\varepsilon_t^\top]=R_t\ne Q_t\)

          Two-Step Estimation (1/2): Splitting the Likelihood

          Slide 23 in the PDF

          Substitute \(H_t=D_tR_tD_t\) and \(\varepsilon_t=D_t^{-1}r_t\) into (6.7). The determinant factors as \(|H_t|=|D_t|^2|R_t|\), and the quadratic form becomes \(r_t^\top H_t^{-1}r_t=\varepsilon_t^\top R_t^{-1}\varepsilon_t\). Adding and subtracting \(\varepsilon_t^\top\varepsilon_t=r_t^\top D_t^{-2}r_t\), the likelihood splits in two:

          \[\ell=\underbrace{-\tfrac12\sum_t\big(N\ln2\pi+2\ln|D_t|+r_t^\top D_t^{-2}r_t\big)}_{\ell_V}\;\underbrace{-\tfrac12\sum_t\big(\ln|R_t|+\varepsilon_t^\top R_t^{-1}\varepsilon_t-\varepsilon_t^\top\varepsilon_t\big)}_{\ell_C} .\]
          (6.9)

          The first part, \(\ell_V\), contains only the volatilities. Since \(D_t\) is diagonal, it is the sum of the \(N\) univariate GARCH log-likelihoods, \(\sum_i\big[-\tfrac12\sum_t(\ln2\pi+\ln\sigma_{it}^2+r_{it}^2/\sigma_{it}^2)\big]\). The second part, \(\ell_C\), depends on \((a,b)\) once the volatilities are given.

          This gives the two-step estimator:

          1. Step 1 estimates a separate GARCH(1,1) model for each asset and keeps the volatilities \(\sigma_{it}\) and the standardised residuals \(\varepsilon_{it}\).
          2. Step 2 sets \(\bar Q\) to the sample covariance of the residuals and maximises \(\ell_C\) over \((a,b)\).

          In brief

          • The Gaussian log-likelihood splits into a volatility part and a correlation part
            • \(\ell=\underbrace{-\tfrac12\sum_t\big(N\ln2\pi+2\ln|D_t|+r_t^\top D_t^{-2}r_t\big)}_{\ell_V}\;\underbrace{-\tfrac12\sum_t\big(\ln|R_t|+\varepsilon_t^\top R_t^{-1}\varepsilon_t-\varepsilon_t^\top\varepsilon_t\big)}_{\ell_C}\)
            • \(\ell_V\): depends only on the GARCH parameters (volatility part)
            • \(\ell_C\): depends on \((a,b)\) given the volatilities (correlation part)
            • \(|D_t|\), \(|R_t|\): determinants
          • Where the split comes from
            • substitute \(H_t=D_tR_tD_t\) and \(\varepsilon_t=D_t^{-1}r_t\) into the quasi-log-likelihood of the previous section
            • Determinant: \(|H_t|=|D_t|^2\,|R_t|\), so \(\ln|H_t|=2\ln|D_t|+\ln|R_t|\)
            • Quadratic form: \(r_t^\top H_t^{-1}r_t=r_t^\top D_t^{-1}R_t^{-1}D_t^{-1}r_t=\varepsilon_t^\top R_t^{-1}\varepsilon_t\)
            • Add and subtract \(\varepsilon_t^\top\varepsilon_t=r_t^\top D_t^{-2}r_t=\sum_i r_{it}^2/\sigma_{it}^2\): then \(\ell_V=\sum_i\big[-\tfrac12\sum_t(\ln2\pi+\ln\sigma_{it}^2+r_{it}^2/\sigma_{it}^2)\big]\), the sum of the \(N\) univariate Gaussian GARCH log-likelihoods
          • Step 1: fit \(N\) univariate GARCH(1,1) models, keep \(\sigma_{it}\) and \(\varepsilon_{it}\)
          • Step 2: fix \(\bar Q\) at the sample covariance of \(\varepsilon_t\) and maximise \(\ell_C\) over \((a,b)\)
          • The gain: instead of one joint maximisation, \(N\) univariate ones and one with two parameters; the cost: harder inference (next slide)

          Two-Step Estimation (2/2): Targeting and Inference

          Slide 24 in the PDF

          Correlation targeting has a cost: the standard DCC estimator is, in general, inconsistent. The reason is the subtlety above. Taking expectations in (6.8), stationarity gives \((1-b)\,\mathbb{E}[Q_t]=(1-a-b)\,\bar Q+a\,\mathbb{E}[R_t]\). The sample covariance of the residuals estimates \(\mathbb{E}[R_t]\). It equals \(\bar Q\) only if \(\mathbb{E}[Q_t]=\mathbb{E}[R_t]\), and in general this equality fails, because \(R_t\) divides \(Q_t\) by a random diagonal.

          The corrected DCC (cDCC) of Aielli (2013) fixes the problem. It uses \(\operatorname{diag}(Q_{t-1})^{1/2}\varepsilon_{t-1}\) in the recursion instead of \(\varepsilon_{t-1}\). Then \(\mathbb{E}[Q_t]=\bar Q\), and targeting becomes consistent.

          The second difficulty concerns the standard errors. Those of step 2 treat the step-1 estimates and \(\bar Q\) as known exactly, so they ignore their uncertainty. There are two corrections: the sandwich matrix of two-step estimators (Newey & McFadden, 1994), given in the Appendix, and a bootstrap that redoes both steps, described there as well.

          The third difficulty concerns the test. The CCC model is the case \(a=0\). Under the hypothesis \(H_0\colon a=0\), the parameter \(b\) has no effect and cannot be estimated. For this reason, the usual \(\chi^2\) distribution of the likelihood ratio (LR) test is only indicative. The chapter obtains the critical values from a bootstrap that simulates data under CCC.

          In brief

              Crisis Correlations and the Forbes–Rigobon Bias

              Correlations measured during crises are usually higher than in calm periods. This section asks whether the link between markets has changed, or whether only the market where the crisis started has become more turbulent. Forbes & Rigobon (2002) show how to separate the two explanations.

              Contagion or Interdependence?

              Slide 30 in the PDF

              A high crisis correlation must be compared with the calm one, and even a rise has two explanations. The first is contagion: the mechanism by which one market affects another changes. The second is the volatility bias: the mechanism stays the same, but the source market becomes much more volatile.

              After correcting this bias, Forbes & Rigobon (2002) find that most of the apparent contagion in the 1987, 1994 and 1997 crises is in fact interdependence, the ordinary link between markets. A related finding concerns asymmetry. Longin & Solnik (2001) and Ang & Chen (2002) find that correlations rise in falling markets (bear markets) but not in rising ones (bull markets). The Normal distribution cannot produce such an asymmetry. The photograph shows the headquarters of Lehman Brothers in New York on 15 September 2008, the day of the bankruptcy filing.

              The Heteroskedasticity Bias (1/2): Correlation in a Calm Period

              Slide 31 in the PDF

              The idea can be seen without formulas. A market's return has a part that comes from the source market and a part of its own, the local noise. The correlation measures how much of the movement comes from the common part. If the source market becomes more turbulent, the common part grows and the local noise does not. The correlation rises, although the link has not changed.

              Formally, let \(x_t\) be the return of the source market and \(y_t\) the return of the other market:

              \[y_t=\alpha+\beta x_t+\varepsilon_t, \qquad \operatorname{Cov}(x_t,\varepsilon_t)=0.\]

              The constant \(\beta\) measures how much is transmitted from \(x\) to \(y\). The own shock \(\varepsilon_t\) has constant variance \(\sigma_\varepsilon^2\), and \(\sigma_x^2\) is the variance of the source market. Because \(\varepsilon_t\) is uncorrelated with \(x_t\), \(\operatorname{Cov}(x_t,y_t)=\beta\sigma_x^2\) and \(\operatorname{Var}(y_t)=\beta^2\sigma_x^2+\sigma_\varepsilon^2\).

              The squared correlation is then

              \[\rho^2=\frac{\beta^2\sigma_x^2}{\beta^2\sigma_x^2+\sigma_\varepsilon^2}=\frac{k}{1+k}, \qquad k=\frac{\beta^2\sigma_x^2}{\sigma_\varepsilon^2}.\]
              (6.10)

              The ratio \(k\) compares the part transmitted from the source market with the own noise; it is called the signal-to-noise ratio. Equivalently, \(k=\rho^2/(1-\rho^2)\). (6.10) shows that the variance of the source market affects the correlation only through \(k\). And \(k\) rises with \(\sigma_x^2\) even when \(\beta\) does not change.

              In brief

              • Model: \(y_t=\alpha+\beta x_t+\varepsilon_t\), \(\Cov(x_t,\varepsilon_t)=0\), \(\beta\) and \(\sigma_\varepsilon^2\) constant
                • \(x_t\): return of the source market
                • \(y_t\): return of the other market
                • \(\beta\): transmission coefficient
                • \(\varepsilon_t\): own shock of \(y\), variance \(\sigma_\varepsilon^2\)
                • \(\sigma_x^2\): variance of \(x\)
              • Two moments of the model
                • \(\Cov(x_t,y_t)=\beta\sigma_x^2\) and \(\Var(y_t)=\beta^2\sigma_x^2+\sigma_\varepsilon^2\), because \(\varepsilon_t\) is uncorrelated with \(x_t\)
              • Correlation: \(\rho^2=\dfrac{\beta^2\sigma_x^2}{\beta^2\sigma_x^2+\sigma_\varepsilon^2}=\Big(1+\dfrac{\sigma_\varepsilon^2}{\beta^2\sigma_x^2}\Big)^{-1}\)
              • The same in one number: the signal-to-noise ratio \(k=\beta^2\sigma_x^2/\sigma_\varepsilon^2\)
                • \(\rho^2=\dfrac{k}{1+k}\), equivalently \(k=\dfrac{\rho^2}{1-\rho^2}\): the correlation depends on \(\sigma_x^2\) only through \(k\)
                • a more volatile source market (larger \(\sigma_x^2\)) raises \(k\), hence \(|\rho|\), with \(\beta\) unchanged

              The Heteroskedasticity Bias (2/2): From Calm to Crisis

              Slide 32 in the PDF

              In a crisis, denoted by the subscript \(c\), the only change is that the variance of the source market rises: \(\sigma_{x,c}^2=(1+\delta)\,\sigma_x^2\), with \(\delta>0\) the relative increase. Then \(k_c=(1+\delta)k\), and (6.10) gives \(\rho_c^2=(1+\delta)\rho^2/(1+\delta\rho^2)\). The correlation measured in the crisis follows:

              \[\rho_c=\rho\sqrt{\frac{1+\delta}{1+\delta\rho^2}} .\]
              (6.11)

              For \(0<|\rho|<1\), \(|\rho_c|>|\rho|\): the measured correlation rises, although \(\beta\) is the same. Inverting the formula gives the crisis correlation adjusted for volatility,

              \[\rho^*=\frac{\rho_c}{\sqrt{1+\delta(1-\rho_c^2)}} .\]
              (6.12)

              An example: take a calm correlation \(\rho=0.5\) and a source variance that doubles (\(\delta=1\)). Then \(k=1/3\), \(k_c=2/3\) and \(\rho_c^2=0.4\), so \(\rho_c=0.632\). The correlation has risen by \(0.13\) with no change in \(\beta\). (6.12) gives back \(0.632/\sqrt{1+1\cdot(1-0.4)}=0.5\), the calm value.

              In brief

              • Crisis (subscript \(c\)): \(\sigma_{x,c}^2=(1+\delta)\,\sigma_{x}^2\)
                • \(\delta>0\): the relative rise of the source variance
                • nothing else changes
              • Only \(\sigma_x^2\) grows, so \(k_c=(1+\delta)\,k\) and
                • \(\rho_c^2=\dfrac{k_c}{1+k_c}=\dfrac{(1+\delta)\rho^2/(1-\rho^2)}{1+(1+\delta)\rho^2/(1-\rho^2)}=\dfrac{(1+\delta)\rho^2}{1+\delta\rho^2}\)
              • Then \(\rho_c=\rho\sqrt{\dfrac{1+\delta}{1+\delta\rho^2}}\), so \(|\rho_c|>|\rho|\) for \(0<|\rho|<1\)
                • the measured correlation rises in absolute value although \(\beta\) is the same
              • Inverting: \(\rho^*=\dfrac{\rho_c}{\sqrt{1+\delta(1-\rho_c^2)}}\) is the crisis correlation adjusted for the volatility increase
              • Quick check: the source variance doubles (\(\delta=1\)) and \(\rho=0.5\)
                • \(k=0.25/0.75=1/3\), \(k_c=2/3\), \(\rho_c^2=(2/3)/(5/3)=0.4\), so \(\rho_c=0.632\): a rise of \(0.13\) with no change in \(\beta\)
                • Adjusting: \(\rho^*=0.632/\sqrt{1+1\cdot(1-0.4)}=0.632/1.265=0.5\), the calm value is recovered

                The Forbes–Rigobon Test and Its Inference

                Slide 34 in the PDF

                The test asks whether the crisis correlation, net of volatility, exceeds the calm one. It has four steps:

                1. Choose the source market \(x\), a calm window and a crisis window.
                2. Use 2-day returns, to absorb the different closing times.
                3. Compute the rise in variance, \(\delta=\hat\sigma^2_{x,c}/\hat\sigma^2_{x,calm}-1\), and the adjusted correlation \(\rho^*\) of (6.12).
                4. Test \(H_0\colon\rho^*\le\rho_{calm}\) with Fisher \(z\) statistics, as in Forbes & Rigobon (2002).

                The Fisher test has two problems here: it treats \(\delta\) as a known number although it is estimated, and the 2-day returns overlap. With \(\delta\) fixed, \(\operatorname{Var}(\operatorname{artanh}\rho^*)\approx1/[n_c(1+\delta(1-\rho_c^2))]<1/n_c\), where \(n_c\) is the number of crisis days, so the Fisher test is conservative.

                For both problems, the chapter uses a stationary bootstrap: samples built from blocks of consecutive days of random length, which keep the dependence between neighbouring days. The mean block length is 10 days, and the number of samples is \(B=1{,}999\). The calm and crisis windows are resampled jointly, and the test looks at the difference \(D=\rho^*-\rho_{calm}\).

                The adjustment holds only under three assumptions:

                • There is no omitted common shock. A global factor that hits both markets raises \(\sigma_\varepsilon\) as well, not only \(\sigma_x\); the adjustment then over-corrects (Corsetti, Pericoli & Sbracia, 2005).
                • The market \(y\) does not feed back into the source market \(x\).
                • The variance of the own noise, \(\sigma_\varepsilon^2\), is the same in the two periods.

                The choice of windows matters too: the conclusion can flip when the windows move by a few weeks.

                In brief

                • Procedure
                  • Choose the source market \(x\), a calm window and a crisis window
                  • Use 2-day returns (closing times differ)
                  • Compute \(\delta=\hat\sigma^2_{x,c}/\hat\sigma^2_{x,calm}-1\) and \(\rho^*\)
                  • Test \(H_0: \rho^*\le\rho_{calm}\) (no contagion) with Fisher \(z\) statistics, the convention of Forbes & Rigobon (2002)
                • Inference
                  • With \(\delta\) fixed, \(\Var(\operatorname{artanh}\rho^*)\approx1/[n_c(1+\delta(1-\rho_c^2))]<1/n_c\) (\(n_c\): crisis observations): the Fisher test is conservative (it rejects \(H_0\) too rarely)
                  • But \(\delta\) is estimated and 2-day returns overlap: use a stationary bootstrap of the calm and crisis windows, resampled jointly, and test \(D=\rho^*-\rho_{calm}\)
                  • Stationary bootstrap: resamples blocks of consecutive days (random starts, geometric lengths, mean 10 days; \(B=1\,999\) draws), keeping the serial dependence inside each block
                Assumptions to remember
                • No omitted common shock
                  • a global factor hitting both markets raises \(\sigma_\varepsilon\) as well as \(\sigma_x\)
                  • and the adjustment over-corrects (Corsetti, Pericoli & Sbracia (2005); Appendix)
                • No feedback from \(y\) to \(x\) (no endogeneity)
                • The choice of windows matters: results can flip with a few weeks
                • The residual variance \(\sigma_\varepsilon^2\) does not change between periods

                  Copulas and Tail Dependence

                  The large risks come from extreme days: how likely is it that two assets fall sharply together? Correlation, a single number, answers this poorly.

                  The section first measures the link on extreme days, then introduces copulas. Their idea: separate how each asset behaves on its own from how the assets move together. The application uses two US banks, JPMorgan Chase (JPM) and Bank of America (BAC).

                  Exceedance Correlations

                  Slide 36 in the PDF

                  Longin & Solnik (2001) compute the correlation only on the days, or weeks, when both returns are extreme. This is the exceedance correlation. For the loss tail, at the threshold \(q\),

                  \[\rho^-(q)=\operatorname{Corr}\big(x,y \mid x\le F_x^{-1}(q),\ y\le F_y^{-1}(q)\big),\]
                  (6.13)

                  where \(F_x^{-1}(q)\) is the \(q\)-quantile of \(x\). At \(q=0.10\), we keep only the weeks in which both returns are among their own weakest 10%. The correlation in the gain tail, \(\rho^+(q)\), is defined in the same way, with the best weeks.

                  Keeping only extreme values lowers the correlation by itself, even if the link does not change. For a Normal distribution, \(\rho^\pm(q)\to0\) as \(q\to0\). The exceedance correlation of the data is therefore compared with that of a Normal distribution with the same correlation, not with the \(\rho\) of the whole sample.

                    Why Correlation Is Not Enough

                    Slide 38 in the PDF

                    Embrechts, McNeil & Straumann (2002) point out three traps of ordinary (Pearson) correlation:

                    1. It measures only the linear link. If we transform the variables, for example from log returns to simple returns, the correlation changes: in general \(\operatorname{Corr}(x,y)\ne\operatorname{Corr}(e^x,e^y)\).
                    2. Knowing the distribution of each asset and the correlation does not give the joint distribution. Two portfolios with the same assets and the same \(\rho\) can have very different Value-at-Risk (Chapter 7).
                    3. Not every value in \([-1,1]\) is possible. For given distributions, the correlation can only take values in an interval \([\rho_{\min},\rho_{\max}]\), reached when one variable rises, or falls, perfectly with the other.

                    An example of the third trap: let \(X=e^{\sigma_1Z}\) and \(Y=e^{\pm\sigma_2Z}\), with \(Z\) standard Normal. With the plus sign, \(Y\) rises perfectly with \(X\); with the minus sign, it falls perfectly. The largest possible correlation is

                    \[\rho_{\max}=\frac{e^{\sigma_1\sigma_2}-1}{\sqrt{(e^{\sigma_1^2}-1)(e^{\sigma_2^2}-1)}},\]

                    and for \(\rho_{\min}\) the numerator becomes \(e^{-\sigma_1\sigma_2}-1\). For \(\sigma_1=1\) and \(\sigma_2=3\), \(\rho_{\max}=0.162\) and \(\rho_{\min}=-0.008\). The two variables are perfectly linked, one being a function of the other, and yet their correlation is almost zero.

                    The chart shows two ways in which the Pearson correlation misleads. On the left, \(Y\) is computed exactly from \(X\), but the link is U-shaped: large values of \(Y\) occur both for very negative and for very positive \(X\). The correlation, which looks for a straight line, comes out close to zero. On the right, the two variables always rise together. The correlation is still small, because a few huge values of \(Y\) dominate the computation. A measure based on order, such as Kendall's tau, sees the perfect link: 1.

                    In brief

                    • Embrechts, McNeil & Straumann (2002): three fallacies about linear correlation
                    • Correlation measures only linear dependence
                      • it is not invariant to monotone transformations: \(\Corr(x,y)\ne\Corr(e^x,e^y)\)
                    • Margins and correlation do not determine the joint distribution
                      • Two portfolios with identical margins and \(\rho\) can have very different Value-at-Risk (Chapter 7)
                    • Attainable correlations: for given margins, \(\rho\in[\rho_{\min},\rho_{\max}]\)
                      • reached by countermonotone (one is a decreasing function of the other) and comonotone (increasing function) pairs
                      • Lognormal \(X=e^{\sigma_1Z}\), \(Y=e^{\pm\sigma_2Z}\), \(Z\) standard Normal (\(+\): comonotone, \(-\): countermonotone): \(\rho_{\max}=\dfrac{e^{\sigma_1\sigma_2}-1}{\sqrt{(e^{\sigma_1^2}-1)(e^{\sigma_2^2}-1)}}\), \(\rho_{\min}\) with \(e^{-\sigma_1\sigma_2}\) in the numerator
                      • \(\sigma_1=1\), \(\sigma_2=3\): \(\rho_{\max}=0.162\) and \(\rho_{\min}=-0.008\): perfectly dependent, yet almost uncorrelated
                    • The remedy: separate the margins from the dependence structure (next slide)

                    Sklar's Theorem: Margins plus Copula

                    Slide 39 in the PDF

                    For each day, we ask where today's return sits in its own history: a weak day, an ordinary one or a very good one? The answer is a probability level between 0 and 1. The copula describes only how these levels move together.

                    Formally, a copula is a joint distribution function on \([0,1]^d\) with uniform margins, where \(d\) is the number of variables. The theorem of Sklar (1959) says that any joint distribution \(F\), with individual distributions (margins) \(F_1,\dots,F_d\), can be written as

                    \[F(x_1,\dots,x_d)=C\big(F_1(x_1),\dots,F_d(x_d)\big),\]
                    (6.14)

                    where \(u_i=F_i(x_i)\in[0,1]\) is the probability level of \(x_i\). If the margins are continuous, the copula \(C\) is unique. Conversely, any copula combined with any margins gives a valid joint distribution. With densities, \(f(x)=c\big(F_1(x_1),\dots,F_d(x_d)\big)\prod_i f_i(x_i)\), where \(c\) is the copula density; the copula of two variables that rise perfectly together has no density.

                    (6.14) splits the modelling in two. The margins carry the volatility, skewness and fat tails of each asset (Chapters 1 and 5). The copula carries all the dependence. The copula is unchanged by increasing transformations of each variable, such as replacing returns by their ranks.

                    The chart reads in two directions. Across a row, the copula stays the same and only the margins change: the cloud changes shape, but the dark points are exactly the same pairs, because the ranks do not change. Down a column, the margins stay the same and the copula changes: under the t copula, the lower-left corner is more crowded. Out of 1,500 pairs, 40 have both variables in their worst 5% under the t copula and 34 under the Gaussian copula.

                    For a risk manager the separation has a practical advantage. The model of each asset (volatility, fat tails) can be chosen and checked on its own. The copula is then chosen only for the question "how often do bad days come together?".

                    Three copulas serve as benchmarks. Independence is \(\Pi(u,v)=uv\). Moving perfectly together (comonotonicity) is \(M(u,v)=\min(u,v)\), and moving perfectly in opposite directions (countermonotonicity) is \(W(u,v)=\max(u+v-1,0)\). Every copula lies between the last two, \(W\le C\le M\) (the Fréchet–Hoeffding bounds (Nelsen, 2006)).

                    In brief

                    • A copula is a joint distribution function on \([0,1]^d\) with uniform margins: it describes only how the variables move together, once each is put on the probability scale \([0,1]\)
                    • Sklar (1959): for any joint distribution \(F\) with margins \(F_1,\dots,F_d\) there is a copula \(C\)
                      • with \(F(x_1,\dots,x_d)=C\big(F_1(x_1),\dots,F_d(x_d)\big)\)
                      • \(d\): number of variables
                      • \(F_i\): marginal distribution function of \(x_i\)
                      • \(u_i=F_i(x_i)\in[0,1]\): its probability level
                      • \(C\) is unique if the margins are continuous
                      • Conversely, any copula and any margins give a valid joint distribution
                      • With densities: \(f(x)=c\big(F_1(x_1),\dots,F_d(x_d)\big)\prod_i f_i(x_i)\), \(c\) the copula density, \(f_i\) the marginal densities (the comonotone copula has none)
                    • Margins carry volatility, skewness and fat tails of each asset (Chapters 1 and 5)
                    • The copula carries all the dependence
                      • it is invariant to increasing transformations of each variable, unlike \(\rho\)
                    • Three reference copulas and the Fréchet–Hoeffding bounds \(W\le C\le M\) (Nelsen (2006))
                      • Independence \(\Pi(u,v)=uv\)
                      • Comonotonicity \(M(u,v)=\min(u,v)\)

                    Rank Correlations: Kendall and Spearman

                    Slide 40 in the PDF

                    Rank correlations depend only on the copula: they use the order of the observations, not their values.

                    Kendall's tau (Kendall, 1938) compares pairs of days. A pair of days is concordant if both assets rose, or both fell, from one day to the other. For two independent draws \((X_1,Y_1)\) and \((X_2,Y_2)\),

                    \[\tau=P\big[(X_1-X_2)(Y_1-Y_2)>0\big]-P\big[(X_1-X_2)(Y_1-Y_2)<0\big]=4\,\mathbb{E}[C(U,V)]-1,\]
                    (6.15)

                    that is, the share of concordant pairs minus the share of discordant ones. Spearman's rho (Spearman, 1904) is the Pearson correlation computed on ranks, \(\rho_S=12\iint (C(u,v)-uv)\,du\,dv\). Both are 1 for \(M\), 0 for independence and \(-1\) for \(W\).

                    A worked example with five days. The returns, in per cent, are \(-2;\ 1;\ 3;\ -1;\ 0.5\) for the first asset and \(-3;\ 2;\ 1;\ -0.5;\ -1\) for the second. Five days give 10 pairs of days. For each pair we ask: on which of the two days does the first asset have the higher return, and is it the same day for the second asset? If yes, the pair is concordant; if not, it is discordant.

                    • Day 1 against day 2: both assets have the lower return on day 1 (\(-2<1\) and \(-3<2\)), so the pair is concordant.
                    • Day 2 against day 3: the first asset has more on day 3 (\(3>1\)), the second less (\(1<2\)), so the pair is discordant.
                    • All other pairs are checked the same way. Only the pairs (2, 3) and (4, 5) are discordant; the other 8 are concordant.

                    The result is \(\hat\tau=(8-2)/10=0.6\). The computation uses only the order of the returns, not their size. If day 3 had a return of 30% instead of 3%, \(\hat\tau\) would stay 0.6, while the Pearson correlation would change a lot.

                    For the families of the chapter, (6.15) has closed forms in the copula parameter \(\theta\): \(\tau=\frac2\pi\arcsin\rho\) for the Gaussian and t copulas, \(\theta/(\theta+2)\) for Clayton, \(1-1/\theta\) for Gumbel, and an expression with the Debye function \(D_1\) for Frank. For the Gaussian copula, \(\rho_S=\frac6\pi\arcsin(\rho/2)\). With \(\rho=0.5\), \(\tau=1/3\) and \(\rho_S=0.483\). Inverting these relations gives a simple estimator of \(\theta\) from the sample \(\hat\tau\).

                    In brief

                    Tail Dependence

                    Slide 43 in the PDF

                    Tail dependence answers the question: if one asset has an extremely bad day, how likely is it that the other has one too? The lower-tail coefficient is the limit of this probability as the threshold becomes ever more extreme:

                    \[\lambda_L=\lim_{q\downarrow0}P(V\le q\mid U\le q)=\lim_{q\downarrow0}\frac{C(q,q)}{q},\]
                    (6.16)

                    where \(U=F_1(X_1)\), \(V=F_2(X_2)\), and \(q\) is the tail probability. The upper-tail coefficient, \(\lambda_U\), is defined in the same way, with the best days.

                    If \(\lambda>0\), joint extremes remain likely however extreme the threshold. If \(\lambda=0\), they become rare in the limit (asymptotic independence). Tail dependence is a property of the copula and cannot be seen from \(\rho\). At a finite threshold, \(C(q,q)/q\) is an ordinary probability, not yet the limit.

                    The closed forms are in the table on the slide. The Gaussian and Frank copulas have \(\lambda_L=\lambda_U=0\). The t copula has the same value in both tails, \(2\,t_{\nu+1}\big(-\sqrt{(\nu+1)(1-\rho)/(1+\rho)}\big)\), where \(t_{\nu+1}\) is the Student t distribution function with \(\nu+1\) degrees of freedom. It rises when \(\nu\) falls or \(\rho\) rises. Clayton has \(\lambda_L=2^{-1/\theta}\) and \(\lambda_U=0\), and Gumbel \(\lambda_L=0\) and \(\lambda_U=2-2^{1/\theta}\).

                    The chart shows what the limit in (6.16) means. Take the 1% threshold, the worst day in a hundred. Under the Gaussian copula, if the first asset has such a day, the second has one too with probability 0.273. Under the t copula the probability is 0.432, and under Clayton 0.707. At the 0.1% threshold (one day in a thousand), the Gaussian copula falls to 0.165, while the t copula stays at 0.408.

                    The difference matters exactly where the risk is. The Gaussian copula says that as a crisis gets worse, the chance that the other asset also falls sharply declines. The t copula says that this chance stays almost constant, around 0.4. The JPM–BAC data below are closer to the second picture.

                      Estimating Copulas

                      Slide 45 in the PDF

                      A copula can be estimated in three ways. The most direct is full maximum likelihood: we estimate the margins and the copula at once. It is efficient but numerically heavy, and wrong margins spoil the copula.

                      The IFM method (inference functions for margins) of Joe (2005) works in two steps. First, we estimate a model for each asset, for example GARCH with Student t innovations. Then we turn each return into its probability level, \(\hat u_{it}=\hat F_i(x_{it})\), and estimate the copula on these levels, maximising \(\sum_t\ln c(\hat u_{1t},\hat u_{2t};\theta)\).

                      The third method, canonical maximum likelihood (CML) (Genest, Ghoudi & Rivest, 1995), no longer needs a model for each asset. The probability level comes straight from the rank: where today's return sits among all the returns of the series. These values are called pseudo-observations:

                      \[\hat u_{it}=\frac{\operatorname{rank}(x_{it})}{T+1}.\]
                      (6.17)

                      The denominator \(T+1\) keeps the values away from 0 and 1, where the density of many copulas becomes infinite. For example, with \(T=4{,}202\) days, the day with the 42nd lowest return gets \(\hat u=42/4{,}203\), about 1%: one of the worst 1% of days. Because it assumes no form for the margins, CML cannot get them wrong.

                      For returns, time-varying volatility would mix turbulent periods with the link between the assets. Each series is therefore first filtered with GARCH, and CML is applied to the ranks of the standardised residuals. This assumes that the residuals are i.i.d., which the GARCH filter does not guarantee on its own.

                      The families are mostly not special cases of one another, so they are compared by the information criteria AIC \(=2k-2\ell\) (Akaike) and BIC \(=k\ln T-2\ell\) (Bayesian), with \(k\) the number of parameters and \(\ell\) the log-likelihood. For the Gaussian against the t copula, the seminar uses a likelihood-ratio (LR) test calibrated by bootstrap. The Gaussian copula is the limit of the t copula as \(\nu\to\infty\), so the bootstrap simulates at \(\nu=\infty\).

                      In brief

                      • Full maximum likelihood: margins and copula estimated jointly
                        • Efficient, but computationally demanding and sensitive to misspecification
                      • IFM (inference functions for margins, Joe (2005)): two steps
                        • Fit parametric margins (e.g. GARCH with Student t)
                        • Set \(\hat u_{it}=\hat F_i(x_{it})\) (\(\hat F_i\): fitted marginal distribution function)
                        • Maximise \(\sum_t\ln c(\hat u_{1t},\hat u_{2t};\theta)\), \(c\) the copula density
                      • CML (canonical maximum likelihood, Genest, Ghoudi & Rivest (1995)): no parametric margins
                        • Pseudo-observations (rescaled ranks): \(\hat u_{it}=\operatorname{rank}(x_{it})/(T+1)\)
                        • Then maximise the copula likelihood
                        • Robust to misspecified margins
                        • The \(T+1\) keeps \(\hat u\) away from 0 and 1
                      • For returns: filter each series with GARCH first
                        • without filtering, volatility clustering would mix dependence with changing volatility
                        • then use the ranks of the standardised residuals (CML, the method used from here on)
                        • the static CML inference shown here assumes i.i.d. innovations; GARCH filtering alone does not guarantee this
                      • Model choice: most of the families compared are not nested (none is a special case of another)
                        • exception: the Gaussian is the t copula with \(\nu=\infty\); for it we use an LR test calibrated by bootstrap (Seminar 6, B2)
                        • for the others we use AIC \(=2k-2\ell\) (Akaike information criterion), BIC \(=k\ln T-2\ell\) (Bayesian information criterion)
                        • \(k\): number of parameters, \(\ell\): maximised log-likelihood

                            The Gaussian Copula in the 2008 Crisis

                            Slide 50 in the PDF

                            Li (2000) modelled joint defaults in credit portfolios with a Gaussian copula, with one correlation parameter calibrated to market prices. The model became the standard for pricing the tranches of collateralised debt obligations (CDO).

                            It had two weaknesses, both discussed in this chapter. First, the Gaussian copula has no tail dependence, so it underestimates the probability that very many loans default at once. Second, the correlation was fixed and calibrated in calm years, while the DCC and Forbes–Rigobon sections show that correlations change between regimes.

                            The example shows why the first weakness mattered. Both models have the same mean, 2 defaults out of 100, and the same correlation. Only the copula differs. Under the t copula most scenarios are even calmer: 71% of them have no default at all, against 48% under the Gaussian copula. The bad scenarios, however, are much more likely. The probability of at least 20 defaults is 0.026 under the t copula and 0.0086 under the Gaussian copula, about three times smaller. For at least 30 defaults the values are 0.012 and 0.002.

                            A senior tranche loses money only in such extreme scenarios. Priced with the Gaussian copula, it looked much safer than it was. This is, in short, the model error discussed after 2008.

                            In brief

                            • Li (2000) modelled joint default times with a Gaussian copula
                              • One correlation parameter, calibrated to market spreads
                              • It priced whole credit portfolios and became the standard for tranches of collateralised debt obligations (CDO)
                            • Zero asymptotic tail dependence (\(\lambda_L=\lambda_U=0\))
                              • for the most extreme joint defaults it gives lower probabilities than tail-dependent models calibrated to the same data
                            • A static correlation calibrated in calm years
                              • exactly the Forbes–Rigobon and DCC lessons of this chapter
                            • The lesson: joint extremes need a copula with tail dependence, re-estimated when the regime changes

                            Section summary

                            Large losses of US and European stocks come together far more often than a Normal distribution with the same correlation would predict. Copulas separate the behaviour of each asset from the link between assets. The same rank correlation can hide very different behaviour on extreme days. For JPM and BAC, the t copula describes the data best, but no static copula passes the goodness-of-fit test, and the difference between the loss tail and the gain tail is not significant.

                                    Case Study (1/2): Which Events Shake All Markets at Once?

                                    Slide 57 in the PDF

                                    In a correctly specified DCC model, the residuals \(e_t\) satisfy \(\mathbb{E}_{t-1}(e_te_t^\top)=I\), where \(e_t\) is the vector of the \(N\) residuals of day \(t\) and \(\mathbb{E}_{t-1}\) is the expectation given yesterday's information. In other words, the residuals are uncorrelated and have variance 1.

                                    Uncorrelated does not mean independent: the residuals can be large on the same days. The paper studies the covariance of their squares:

                                    \[\Psi_t=\mathbb{E}_{t-1}\big[(e_t^2-\iota)(e_t^2-\iota)^\top\big],\]

                                    where \(e_t^2\) squares each element and \(\iota\) is the vector of ones. If an off-diagonal element \(\Psi_{ij,t}\) is not zero, assets \(i\) and \(j\) have large shocks on the same days.

                                    This is the idea of the ARCH effect (autoregressive conditional heteroskedasticity) of Chapter 5, seen across assets instead of over time: a turbulent day for one asset is turbulent for the others too. Herskovic, Kelly, Lustig & Van Nieuwerburgh (2016) find such a common factor in the idiosyncratic volatility of US stocks.

                                    Case Study (2/2): One Common Factor Scales Every Variance

                                    Slide 58 in the PDF

                                    The model assumes that one common factor scales the variance of every residual:

                                    \[e_{it}=\sqrt{s_ix_t+1-s_i}\;\varepsilon_{it},\]
                                    (6.18)

                                    with \(\varepsilon_{it}\) i.i.d. \(\mathcal N(0,1)\), \(x_t>0\) and \(\mathbb{E}_{t-1}(x_t)=1\). The factor \(x_t\) is the squared global COVOL of day \(t\); on a common shock day, \(x_t>1\). The loading \(s_i\in[0,1]\) says how sensitive asset \(i\) is to the factor: its conditional variance is \(g_{it}=1+s_i(x_t-1)\).

                                    From (6.18) only the products \(s_i(x_t-1)\) can be recovered, so the scale must be fixed by a convention. The simplest case has equal loadings, \(s_i=1\). The estimate of \(x_t\) is then the mean of the squared residuals of that day, \(\hat x_t=\frac1N\sum_ie_{it}^2\).

                                    With unequal loadings, the estimation alternates two maximum-likelihood steps. The first estimates \(x_t\) for each day, from all assets. The second estimates \(s_i\) for each asset, from all days. The convention is \(s^\top s=1\) and a mean of \(x_t\) equal to 1. Under this convention the loadings are only relative sizes; equal loadings would be \(s_i=1/\sqrt N\).

                                    The test for a common shock uses the statistic \(\xi\), the standardised mean of the products \((e_{it}^2-1)(e_{jt}^2-1)\) over days and pairs of assets. If the squares are unrelated, that is \(H_0\colon\Psi_{ij}=0\) for \(i\neq j\), the statistic is standard Normal. The data of the paper are 47 country ETFs listed on the New York Stock Exchange (NYSE), 2000–2021, and 23 US asset-class series; details are in the Appendix.

                                    DCC in Vast Dimensions: Estimation with Many Assets

                                    Slide 61 in the PDF

                                    The second study asks whether DCC works with hundreds of assets, as the VaR and the hedging of a large portfolio require. Pakel, Shephard, Sheppard & Engle (2021) show, for scalar BEKK and cDCC, that the usual method gives strongly biased estimates of the dynamic parameters \((a,b)\) as the number of assets \(K\) grows. The usual method is the full likelihood, with the correlation target fixed in advance (correlation targeting).

                                    The cause is the target itself. The matrix \(\bar Q\) holds \(K(K-1)/2\) correlations, estimated before \((a,b)\). Their number grows with \(K\), and their errors add up in the likelihood. It is also slow: every evaluation of the full likelihood inverts \(T\) matrices of size \(K\times K\), so the cost grows with \(K^3\).

                                    The remedy is the composite likelihood (CL), the sum of the likelihoods of pairs of assets:

                                    \[\ell_{CL}(a,b)=\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal P}\ell_{ij}(a,b),\]
                                    (6.19)

                                    where each pair uses only its own \(2\times2\) block of \(\hat{\bar Q}\). The set \(\mathcal P\) contains either all \(K(K-1)/2\) pairs or only the neighbouring pairs \((1,2),(2,3),\dots,(K-1,K)\). No large matrix is inverted, and the theory of the paper lets \(K\) grow with the sample length \(T\). The paper uses the stocks of the S&P 100 and of the S&P 500, daily, 1997–2006.

                                        Testing Constant Correlation at an Unknown Date

                                        Slide 72 in the PDF

                                        If we pick the break date from a chart and then test it with a Fisher \(z\) test, the test rejects too often: the date was chosen from the data themselves. Tse (2000) gives a Lagrange multiplier test computed from the fitted CCC model alone. The alternative is \(\rho_{ij,t}=\rho_{ij}+\kappa_{ij}\,\varepsilon_{i,t-1}\varepsilon_{j,t-1}\), a correlation that reacts to yesterday's shocks.

                                        Wied, Krämer & Dehling (2012) compare the correlation of the first \(j\) observations, \(\hat\rho_j\), with the full-sample correlation \(\hat\rho_T\), for every \(j\):

                                        \[Q_T=\max_{2\le j\le T}\frac{j}{\sqrt T\,\hat D}\,\big|\hat\rho_j-\hat\rho_T\big|,\]
                                        (6.21)

                                        Here \(\hat D^2\) is the long-run variance of \(\hat\rho\), from the delta method with a Bartlett kernel and bandwidth \(\lfloor T^{1/4}\rfloor\). The estimated break date is the point where the maximum is reached.

                                        If correlation, means and variances are constant, \(Q_T\) converges to \(\sup_{0\le s\le1}|B(s)|\). Here \(B\) is a Brownian bridge and \(s=j/T\). The 5% critical value is 1.358. The test needs constant variances, so it is applied to GARCH-standardised residuals.

                                        Inference for Copula Parameters (1/2): Inverting Kendall's Tau

                                        Slide 81 in the PDF

                                        Kendall's \(\hat\tau\) is a U-statistic, an average over all pairs of observations. Its standard error comes from the Hoeffding projection.

                                        The copula parameter follows by inverting the link \(\tau(\theta)\) of (6.15): \(\hat\theta=g(\hat\tau)\). The standard error of \(\hat\theta\) comes from the delta method (Genest & Rivest, 1993).

                                        For elliptical copulas, \(\tau=\frac2\pi\arcsin\rho\) for every \(\nu\): \(\tau\) identifies \(\rho\), but never \(\nu\).

                                        For JPM and BAC, \(\hat\tau=0.641\) (SE 0.0065) gives \(\hat\rho_\tau=\sin(\pi\hat\tau/2)=0.845\) (SE 0.0055). This is close to the CML estimate \(\hat\rho=0.842\) (SE 0.0053).

                                        Inference for Copula Parameters (2/2): Ranks, Fit and GARCH Filtering

                                        Slide 82 in the PDF

                                        In CML, the ranks are themselves estimated. The inverse Hessian ignores this uncertainty, so its standard errors are too small. The sandwich of Genest, Ghoudi & Rivest (1995) adds the rank terms.

                                        The correction differs from case to case:

                                        • for the Gaussian \(\rho\) of weekly S&P 500–Euro Stoxx 50 returns, the SE rises by 60%, from 0.0086 to 0.0138;
                                        • for the t-copula \(\rho\) of JPM–BAC, the SE rises by 17%, from 0.0046 to 0.0053;
                                        • for \(\nu\), the SE rises by 1%.

                                        The factor of 1.60 signals misspecification. If the Gaussian copula were correct, ranking would multiply the SE by \(\sqrt{1+\rho^2}\), that is 1.27 at the weekly correlation 0.78 (Monte Carlo at \(\rho=0.8\): 1.28). The sandwich stays valid when the copula is misspecified.

                                        Chen & Fan (2006) show that CML on the ranks of estimated residuals has the same limit as on the true innovations. The condition is that the innovations are i.i.d. with copula \(C_\theta\) and GARCH is correctly specified. Unlike DCC, step 1 needs no correction.

                                        Goodness of Fit: The Rosenblatt Test

                                        Slide 83 in the PDF

                                        If \((U,V)\sim C_\theta\), then \(E_1=U\) and \(E_2=h(V\mid U)=\partial C_\theta(U,V)/\partial u\) are independent uniforms. Here \(h\) is the distribution function of \(V\) conditional on \(U\).

                                        The statistic of Genest, Rémillard & Beaudoin (2009) is \(S_n=n\int_{[0,1]^2}\big(D_n(e)-e_1e_2\big)^2de\). It compares the share \(D_n(e)\) of pairs with \(E_1\le e_1\) and \(E_2\le e_2\) with its value \(e_1e_2\) under independence. Large values mean a poor fit.

                                        Because \(\theta\) is estimated, the \(p\)-value comes from a parametric bootstrap: we simulate from \(C_{\hat\theta}\), re-rank, re-estimate and recompute \(S_n\); \(p=(1+\#\{S_n^{(b)}\ge S_n\})/(B+1)\).

                                        For JPM and BAC, \(B=200\) (100 for the t copula). The smallest attainable \(p\)-value is therefore \(1/201\approx0.005\) (\(1/101\approx0.010\) for t).

                                        On GARCH residuals, rank-based tests keep their limit (Rémillard, 2017). With \(T=4{,}202\), the test detects even small departures, but a rejection does not say what the model is missing.

                                        Is the Tail Asymmetry Real?

                                        Slide 84 in the PDF

                                        The estimator of the main text is that of Schmidt & Stadtmüller (2006): \(\hat\lambda_L=\frac nk\hat C_n(\frac kn,\frac kn)\). Here \(n\) is the sample size, \(k\) the number of observations in the tail and \(\hat C_n\) the empirical copula. A larger \(k\) lowers the variance but raises the bias.

                                        The test compares the two tails at a fixed threshold \(q\), \(H_0\colon\lambda_L(q)=\lambda_U(q)\): threshold probabilities, not limits.

                                        Two bootstraps give the uncertainty. The first resamples residual pairs, re-ranked, with \(B=999\). The second uses stationary blocks, with mean lengths of 1.6 and 1.0 days chosen by the rule of Politis & White (2004).

                                        The results:

                                        • at \(q=5\%\) (\(k=210\)), the difference is 0.062, with 95% intervals \([-0.014, 0.148]\) (i.i.d.) and \([-0.014, 0.138]\) (blocks);
                                        • at \(q=1\%\) (\(k=42\)), the difference is 0.095, with intervals \([-0.095, 0.286]\) and \([-0.119, 0.286]\).

                                        All intervals contain zero: the asymmetry is not detected statistically. The test does not validate the t copula, which the global goodness-of-fit test rejects.

                                        Inference on a Correlation, the Delta Method

                                        Slide 90 in the PDF

                                        The correlation is a smooth function \(g\) of five sample means, those of the moments \(m_t=(x_t,y_t,x_t^2,y_t^2,x_ty_t)\). The delta method gives:

                                        \(\sqrt T(\hat\rho-\rho)\to N(0,\nabla g^\top\Omega\nabla g)\).

                                        Here \(\Omega\) is the long-run covariance of \(m_t\). It is estimated by HAC (Newey & West, 1987), that is, robustly to heteroskedasticity and autocorrelation. The influence-function form, (6.4), gives the same result.

                                        The result needs three conditions: stationarity, finite moments of order higher than 4 and weak dependence over time. If the fourth moment is infinite, the Normal limit fails. An ordinary bootstrap does not fix this automatically.

                                        The HAC interval is built on the Fisher scale:

                                        \(\tanh\big(\operatorname{artanh}\hat\rho\pm1.96\,\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}_{\mathrm{HAC}}/(1-\hat\rho^2)\big)\).

                                        On the last 252-day window, ending on 17 September 2026, \(\hat\rho=0.245\). The Fisher interval is \([0.13,\ 0.36]\) and the HAC interval is \([0.07,\ 0.41]\), visibly wider. Over the whole sample, the HAC interval is at most 2.02 times as wide as the Fisher one.

                                        In brief

                                        • \(\hat\rho=g(\hat m)\), with \(\hat m\) the sample mean of \(m_t=(x_t,\,y_t,\,x_t^2,\,y_t^2,\,x_ty_t)\)
                                          • \(g\): the function that turns these five moments into the correlation; \(\nabla g\): its gradient
                                        • Delta method: \(\sqrt T(\hat\rho-\rho)\to N\big(0,\ \nabla g^\top\Omega\,\nabla g\big)\)
                                          • \(\Omega\): the long-run covariance of \(m_t\), estimated by HAC with a Bartlett kernel and \(L\) lags (Newey & West (1987))
                                        • The same form with the daily contributions \(\psi_t\) (influence function): \(\hat\rho-\rho\approx\frac1T\sum_t\psi_t\) of the main text
                                        • Conditions: stationarity, finite moments of order higher than 4 of \((x_t,y_t)\) and weak dependence (mixing)
                                          • with infinite fourth moments the Normal limit fails
                                          • nor does the ordinary bootstrap automatically fix missing moments: this applies to the Forbes–Rigobon bootstrap and to tests on squared losses too
                                        • The HAC interval, built on the Fisher scale: \(\tanh\big(\operatorname{artanh}\hat\rho\pm1.96\,\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}_{\mathrm{HAC}}/(1-\hat\rho^2)\big)\)
                                          • with \(\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}_{\mathrm{HAC}}=\sqrt{\nabla\hat g^\top\hat\Omega\,\nabla\hat g/T}\); \(1/(1-\hat\rho^2)\) is the derivative of \(\operatorname{artanh}\) (delta method)
                                        • The classic Fisher interval replaces \(\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}_{\mathrm{HAC}}/(1-\hat\rho^2)\) by \(1/\sqrt{T-3}\)
                                        • Example: the last 252-day rolling window of the main text, ending on 17 September 2026
                                          • \(\hat\rho=0.245\), Fisher interval [\(0.13\), \(0.36\)], HAC interval [\(0.07\), \(0.41\)]
                                          • largest HAC-to-Fisher width ratio over all windows: \(2.02\)

                                        The VEC and BEKK Models

                                        Slide 91 in the PDF

                                        In the VEC model (Bollerslev, Engle & Wooldridge, 1988), \(c\) is a constant vector and the number of parameters is \(N(N+1)/2\cdot[1+N(N+1)]\).

                                        The BEKK model (Engle & Kroner, 1995) gives a covariance matrix that is positive definite by construction, provided \(CC^\top\) is positive definite. It has \(N(N+1)/2+2N^2\) parameters. These parameters are hard to interpret, and the likelihood becomes flat when \(N\) is large.

                                        DCC has only \(3N+2\) parameters: one univariate GARCH for each variance, plus the two correlation parameters \(a\) and \(b\).

                                        In brief

                                        • VEC (Bollerslev, Engle & Wooldridge (1988)): in the equation of the main text, \(c\) is a constant vector
                                        • VEC: \(N(N+1)/2 \cdot [1+N(N+1)]\) parameters: the vector \(c\) plus the matrices \(A\) and \(B\), each with \([N(N+1)/2]^2\) elements
                                        • BEKK (Engle & Kroner (1995)): positive definite by construction if \(CC^\top\) is positive definite
                                        • BEKK: \(N(N+1)/2+2N^2\) parameters (\(C\) triangular, \(A\) and \(B\) of size \(N\times N\)), hard to interpret
                                          • and the likelihood is flat in high dimension
                                        • DCC: \(3N+2\) parameters, univariate GARCH for each variance plus \(a\) and \(b\); the target \(\bar Q\) is estimated separately

                                        The two DCC steps can be written as stacked estimating equations. The first is \(\sum_t s_{1t}(\theta_1)=0\), for the GARCH parameters and, with correlation targeting, for \(\operatorname{vech}\bar Q\). The second is \(\sum_t s_{2t}(\theta_1,\theta_2)=0\), for \(\theta_2=(a,b)\).

                                        Write \(A_{jk}=\mathbb{E}[\partial s_{jt}/\partial\theta_k^\top]\). Section 6 of Newey & McFadden (1994) gives the correct variance

                                        \(V_2=A_{22}^{-1}\Omega A_{22}^{-1\top}\),

                                        where \(\Omega\) is the long-run variance of the adjusted score \(u_t=s_{2t}-A_{21}A_{11}^{-1}s_{1t}\). The term \(A_{21}\) measures how much the correlation score reacts to the GARCH parameters. Naive standard errors keep only \(s_{2t}\) and omit this error carried over from step 1.

                                        The targeting moment is not a martingale difference, so \(\Omega\) is estimated by HAC. Engle & Sheppard (2001) derive \(V_2\) for DCC. A simpler alternative is to bootstrap the whole two-step procedure.

                                        Copula Densities and Constructions

                                        Slide 96 in the PDF

                                        The density of the Gaussian copula, with \(x=\Phi^{-1}(u)\) and \(y=\Phi^{-1}(v)\), is

                                        \(c=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\rho^2}}\exp\big(-\frac{\rho^2(x^2+y^2)-2\rho xy}{2(1-\rho^2)}\big)\).

                                        Rank Estimators and Their Precision

                                        Slide 98 in the PDF

                                        The estimated Kendall's tau has a Normal limiting distribution. By the Hoeffding projection,

                                        \(\sqrt n(\hat\tau-\tau)\to N\big(0,16\operatorname{Var}[2C(U,V)-U-V]\big)\).

                                        The plug-in standard error replaces \(C\) with the empirical copula. Inverting \(\hat\tau\) gives a robust starting value for maximising the likelihood.

                                        For the CML estimator, Genest, Ghoudi & Rivest (1995) give a sandwich matrix, \(A^{-1}\Sigma A^{-1}\). Here \(A\) is the Fisher information of the copula. \(\Sigma\) is the variance of the score of \(\ln c\), plus two correction terms, \(W_1\) and \(W_2\), which account for the margins being estimated by ranks.

                                        For the Gaussian copula, the CML variance is \((1-\rho^2)^2\). With known margins it would be smaller: \((1-\rho^2)^2/(1+\rho^2)\).

                                        In brief

                                        • \(\hat\tau\) is a U-statistic
                                          • its Hoeffding projection gives \(\sqrt n(\hat\tau-\tau)\to N\big(0,\,16\Var[2C(U,V)-U-V]\big)\)
                                        • Plug-in: \(\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}(\hat\tau)=4\,\mathrm{sd}\big(2\hat C_n(\hat U_i,\hat V_i)-\hat U_i-\hat V_i\big)/\sqrt n\), with \(\hat C_n\) the empirical copula
                                        • Inverting \(\hat\tau\) gives a robust starting value for the likelihood
                                        • CML sandwich (Genest, Ghoudi & Rivest (1995)): \(\sqrt n(\hat\theta-\theta)\to N(0,\,A^{-1}\Sigma A^{-1})\)
                                          • \(A\): the Fisher information of the copula
                                        • \(\Sigma=\Var\big[\dot\ell_\theta(U,V)+W_1(U)+W_2(V)\big]\), \(\dot\ell_\theta\) the score of \(\ln c\)
                                        • \(W_1(u)=\int\!\!\int\mathbf 1\{u\le u'\}\,\partial_{u}\dot\ell_\theta(u',v')\,dC(u',v')\)
                                          • the correction for estimating the margins by ranks; \(W_2\) likewise in \(v\)
                                        • Gaussian copula: variance \((1-\rho^2)^2\) for CML, against \((1-\rho^2)^2/(1+\rho^2)\) with known margins

                                        Schmidt & Stadtmüller (2006) give the conditions under which the estimator \(\hat\lambda_L\) of the chapter body is consistent and asymptotically Normal.                                         COVOL: Limitations and the Data after 2021

                                        Slide 106 in the PDF

                                        The authors point to two limitations. First: after dividing by \(\hat g_{it}\), the squares remain correlated, which suggests a second factor. COVOL with several factors remains an open problem.

                                        The second limitation concerns the number of markets. Under the normalisation \(s^\top s=1\), the loadings shrink as \(N\) grows. The fit between estimated and true loadings, \(R^2_s\), is 0.640 for \(N=50\) and \(T=1000\), against 0.924 for \(N=10\). The test loses power when the loadings are unequal, so the authors recommend \(T\ge1000\).

                                        After the paper's sample, two large common shocks occurred. The first, on 3 April 2025, followed the announcement of US tariffs: \(\hat x_t=16.2\), with a mean return of \(-3.67\%\).
