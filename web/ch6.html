Forbes & Rigobon (2002) show that in a crisis the correlation coefficient can rise even when the link between markets stays the same, simply because volatility has risen. The chapter's question is when markets move together and how sure we can be of it. Diversification depends on the correlation through a single cross term of the portfolio variance. A 60/40 portfolio of SPY and TLT, exchange-traded funds on the S&P 500 and on US Treasury bonds with maturities above 20 years, has a volatility of 10.4% with the 2002–2021 correlation (−0.40) and 13.2% with the 2022–2026 one (0.11). The 252-day correlation of the two funds was positive on 6% of days before 2022 and on 81% of days since January 2022. Its sign depends on the type of shock: growth shocks move stocks and bond prices in opposite directions, inflation shocks hit both.

The first problem is measurement. The Fisher interval assumes i.i.d. data and is too narrow on daily returns, which have fat tails and volatility clustering; the HAC (heteroskedasticity and autocorrelation consistent) interval from the delta method accounts for both. Across time zones, asynchronous closes bias the daily correlation towards zero, and 2-day or weekly returns reduce this bias. For today's covariance, the chapter compares rolling windows, EWMA and the DCC (dynamic conditional correlation) model. For SPY and TLT, DCC(1,1) has \(a+b = 0.986\), and the constant conditional correlation (CCC) is rejected (LR 533.4, bootstrap p 0.005).

In crises, the Forbes–Rigobon bias appears. In 2008 the variance of 2-day S&P 500 returns was 7.5 times its calm-year level; at this level, with a constant \(\beta\), a calm-period correlation of 0.4 is measured as 0.77 in the crisis. After the adjustment, the crisis rises in the correlation with the Euro Stoxx 50 and with the BET are no longer significant, although the adjustment may over-correct when a common shock hits every market. Correlation does not describe dependence in the tails, however: when JPM has one of its 5% worst days, BAC is in its own worst 5% about 61% of the time. Copulas separate the marginal distributions from the dependence structure and allow tail dependence to be measured.

When Do Markets Move Together?

The chapter moves from the volatility of a single asset, the subject of Chapter 5, to the joint behaviour of several assets. The object of study is the covariance matrix \(\Sigma\) of returns and, within it, the correlations. Three uses make it central: the variance of a portfolio, the size of a hedge and the distinction between contagion and ordinary interdependence in crises. Each of them depends on a correlation that is estimated, not observed, and that moves over time.

The first section states why dependence matters and documents the main empirical fact of the chapter: the correlation between US stocks and long-term US Treasury bonds changed sign in 2022.

Why Dependence Matters (1/2): Portfolio Diversification

Slide 4 in the PDF

Diversification (Markowitz, 1952) works through the covariance between assets. For two assets with weight \(w\) in the first and \(1-w\) in the second, volatilities \(\sigma_1\), \(\sigma_2\) and correlation \(\rho\), the portfolio variance is

\[\sigma_p^2 = w^\top\Sigma w = w^2\sigma_1^2+(1-w)^2\sigma_2^2+2w(1-w)\rho\,\sigma_1\sigma_2 .\]
(6.1)

In (6.1) the correlation enters only through the cross term. With positive weights, the portfolio variance rises linearly in \(\rho\), and the volatilities of the two assets fix how strong this effect is.

The example uses SPY, an exchange-traded fund (ETF) on the S&P 500, and TLT, an ETF on US Treasury bonds with maturities above 20 years. Over July 2002 – September 2026 their annualised volatilities are 18.8% and 14.3%. A 60/40 portfolio, rebalanced daily to these weights, has a volatility of 10.4% with the 2002–2021 correlation, \(-0.40\), and 13.2% with the 2022–2026 correlation, \(0.11\). The volatilities are the same in both computations; only the correlation differs.

The sample is split in January 2022, at the start of the inflation shock. This date is a convention for the illustration: the break test in Further Topics dates the change in the correlation earlier, in August 2020.

Why Dependence Matters (2/2): Hedges, Contagion and Moving Covariances

Slide 5 in the PDF

The second use of the correlation is hedging. A position \(S\) is hedged by selling an instrument \(F\); the question is how much value of \(F\) to sell per unit of value of \(S\) so that the variance of the hedged position is minimal. With \(r_S\), \(r_F\) the returns and \(\sigma_S\), \(\sigma_F\) the volatilities of the position and of the hedge, the minimum-variance hedge ratio is

\[h^* = \rho\,\frac{\sigma_S}{\sigma_F},\]
(6.2)

the slope of the regression of \(r_S\) on \(r_F\). Estimated on returns, (6.2) is a ratio of values; the number of units of \(F\) per unit of \(S\) is \(h^*P_S/P_F\), where \(P_S\) and \(P_F\) are the prices, contract size included. The hedge removes the share \(\rho^2\) of the variance. If the correlation moves, yesterday's hedge ratio is wrong today, and a dynamic hedge needs a dynamic correlation \(\rho_t\).

The third use concerns crises. Following Forbes & Rigobon (2002), contagion is a significant increase in cross-market linkages after a shock, while interdependence denotes links present in all states, such as trade, common investors or common macroeconomic factors. Correlations tend to rise exactly when diversification is needed most, and the chapter asks how much of this rise is genuine. All three uses share the feature that is new in this chapter: the covariance matrix at date \(t\), \(\Sigma_t\), moves over time.

In brief

  • Hedging
    • How much value of the hedge \(F\) to sell per unit of value held in the position \(S\) to minimise the variance
    • Minimum-variance hedge ratio \(h^*=\rho\,\sigma_S/\sigma_F\): the slope of the regression of \(r_S\) on \(r_F\). Estimated on returns, it is a ratio of values: units of \(F\) per unit of \(S\) are \(h^*P_S/P_F\) (\(P_S\), \(P_F\): prices, contract size included)
    • \(r_S\), \(r_F\): returns of the position and of the hedge
    • \(\sigma_S\), \(\sigma_F\): their volatilities
    • Effectiveness \(\rho^2\): the share of the variance removed
    • If the correlation moves, yesterday's hedge is wrong today: a dynamic hedge needs a dynamic \(\rho_t\)
  • Contagion and interdependence (Forbes & Rigobon (2002))
    • Contagion: a significant increase in cross-market linkages after a shock
    • Interdependence: links present in all states (trade, common investors, common macro factors)
    • Correlations tend to rise exactly when diversification is needed most
  • New in this chapter: \(\Sigma_t\), the covariance matrix at date \(t\), moves

    Measuring Covariance and Its Uncertainty

    Before modelling a time-varying covariance matrix, the chapter settles three measurement questions:

    1. The data frequency: exchanges close at different times, and daily correlations across time zones are biased.
    2. The uncertainty of an estimated correlation: the classical Fisher interval relies on assumptions that daily returns violate, and an interval based on the delta method with a HAC (heteroskedasticity and autocorrelation consistent) standard error replaces it.
    3. The choice of window: rolling windows and EWMA (exponentially weighted moving average) estimate the current covariance, and their tuning parameter sets the trade-off between speed and noise.

    Asynchronous Trading: A Trap for Daily Correlations

    Slide 9 in the PDF

    The Bucharest Stock Exchange and the euro-area exchanges close hours before New York. US news released after the European close therefore enters European prices only on the next day. When two markets are positively dependent, as equity markets are, this asynchronous trading has two consequences: the same-day correlation is biased towards zero, and the lagged correlation, US yesterday with Europe today, is positive.

    There are two remedies:

    1. A lower frequency: 2-day or weekly returns computed on common trading days, over which the one-day lag weighs less.
    2. Adding the lead–lag term, \(\rho_0+\rho_{+1}\), where \(\rho_0\) is the same-day correlation and \(\rho_{+1}\) the correlation of US returns yesterday with European returns today. The sum is only a rough approximation of the synchronous correlation, valid for a one-day lag, no autocorrelation and equal variances.

    The rule used throughout the chapter is to align prices on common trading days first and to compute returns afterwards.

      Sample Covariance and Inference on a Correlation (1/2): Estimator and Fisher Interval

      Slide 13 in the PDF

      Let \(r_t\in\mathbb{R}^N\) be the vector of the \(N\) returns on day \(t\), \(t=1,\dots,T\), and \(\bar r\) the vector of sample means. The sample covariance matrix and the Pearson correlation of assets \(i\) and \(j\) are

      \[\hat\Sigma=\frac{1}{T-1}\sum_t (r_t-\bar r)(r_t-\bar r)^\top, \qquad \hat\rho_{ij}=\frac{\hat\Sigma_{ij}}{\sqrt{\hat\Sigma_{ii}\hat\Sigma_{jj}}} .\]

      The matrix has \(N(N+1)/2\) distinct elements, 125,250 for \(N=500\) assets, so the number of quantities to estimate grows with the square of the number of assets.

      For a single correlation, the classical inference uses the Fisher transform \(z=\operatorname{artanh}\hat\rho=\tfrac12\ln\frac{1+\hat\rho}{1-\hat\rho}\). For i.i.d. pairs with the bivariate Normal distribution, \(z\) is approximately Normal with a variance that does not depend on \(\rho\):

      \[z\approx N\!\big(\operatorname{artanh}\rho,\ 1/(T-3)\big), \qquad \text{95\% interval: } \tanh\!\big(z\pm1.96/\sqrt{T-3}\big).\]
      (6.3)

      With \(T=252\) days and \(\rho=0.5\), the half-width of the interval (6.3) is about \(0.09\). The interval is asymmetric on the correlation scale, because \(\tanh\) compresses values near \(\pm1\).

      Sample Covariance and Inference on a Correlation (2/2): Delta Method and HAC

      Slide 14 in the PDF

      The delta method writes the estimation error of the correlation as a sample mean of daily contributions. With \(\tilde x_t\), \(\tilde y_t\) the two returns standardised to mean 0 and variance 1,

      \[\hat\rho-\rho\approx\frac1T\sum_t\psi_t, \qquad \psi_t=\tilde x_t\tilde y_t-\frac\rho2\big(\tilde x_t^2+\tilde y_t^2\big),\]
      (6.4)

      where \(\psi_t\) is the contribution of day \(t\) to the error. Volatility clustering does not change this form; it changes only the variance of \(\psi_t\). The standard error (SE) of \(\hat\rho\) follows from the long-run variance of \(\psi_t\), estimated by HAC with a Bartlett kernel and \(L\) lags (Newey & West, 1987). The usual rule of thumb \(L=\lfloor4(T/100)^{2/9}\rfloor\) gives \(L=9\) for \(T=4{,}891\), \(L=6\) for \(T=1{,}182\) and \(L=4\) for a 252-day window.

      For i.i.d. Normal pairs, \(\operatorname{Var}(\psi_t)=(1-\rho^2)^2\), which is the Fisher variance. Fat tails and GARCH effects can change this variance substantially, in either direction; the derivation is in the Appendix. The HAC standard error needs stationarity, weak dependence and finite fourth moments, the last of which Chapter 1 puts in doubt.

      For daily SPY and TLT returns over 2002–2021 (\(T=4{,}891\), \(\hat\rho=-0.403\)), the SE of \(\hat\rho\) is 0.0120 by the Fisher formula, 0.0217 when only the fourth moments are taken into account (\(L=0\)) and 0.0227 with HAC and \(L=9\). Most of the increase comes from the fourth moments, that is, from the fat tails, and little from autocorrelation.

      In brief

      • The delta method writes the estimation error \(\hat\rho-\rho\) as a sample mean of daily contributions \(\psi_t\)
      • Volatility clustering does not change this form, only the variance of \(\psi_t\)

        Rolling Windows

        Slide 17 in the PDF

        The simplest estimator of the current covariance matrix uses the last \(m\) days with equal weights. With the mean set to zero, as is usual for daily data,

        \[\hat\Sigma_t=\frac1m\sum_{s=t-m}^{t-1} r_s r_s^\top ,\]
        (6.5)

        where \(r_s\) is the return vector of day \(s\). The estimator is positive semidefinite: for any weight vector \(v\), \(v^\top\hat\Sigma_tv=\frac1m\sum_s(v^\top r_s)^2\ge0\), so no portfolio has a negative variance. It is positive definite only if the \(m\) return vectors span \(\mathbb{R}^N\), and it is singular whenever \(m<N\).

        Equal weights create the ghost effect: an extreme day enters the estimate with weight \(1/m\) and leaves it abruptly \(m\) days later. The window length sets the trade-off: a short window reacts fast but is noisy, while a long one is stable but detects a change of regime late.

        In brief

        EWMA (RiskMetrics)

        Slide 18 in the PDF

        The EWMA estimator of RiskMetrics (J.P. Morgan/Reuters, 1996) replaces equal weights by weights that decline geometrically with the age of the observation:

        \[\Sigma_t=\lambda\,\Sigma_{t-1}+(1-\lambda)\,r_{t-1}r_{t-1}^\top=(1-\lambda)\sum_{k\ge1}\lambda^{k-1} r_{t-k}r_{t-k}^\top ,\]
        (6.6)

        where \(\lambda\in(0,1)\) is the decay factor and the observation of age \(k\) has weight \((1-\lambda)\lambda^{k-1}\). The usual values are \(\lambda=0.94\) for daily and \(\lambda=0.97\) for monthly data. The half-life of the weights, \(\ln0.5/\ln\lambda\), is 11.2 periods for \(\lambda=0.94\) and 22.8 for \(\lambda=0.97\); the effective sample size, \((1+\lambda)/(1-\lambda)\), is about 32 days for \(\lambda=0.94\).

        The estimate is positive semidefinite and has no ghost effect. Its limitation is that one \(\lambda\) governs all variances and covariances and that there is no mean reversion: (6.6) is an integrated GARCH(1,1) without a constant (Chapter 5). The decay factor plays the role of the window length \(m\), with the same trade-off between reaction speed and noise.

          Section summary

          Daily correlations across time zones are biased towards zero, and lower frequencies or lead–lag terms correct the bias at the cost of precision. On daily returns the Fisher interval is too narrow, mainly because of fat tails, and the delta-method HAC interval is the default, provided fourth moments are finite. Rolling windows and EWMA estimate the current covariance, but their speed is a choice of the analyst.

          Multivariate GARCH and DCC

          This section models a covariance matrix that changes every day. The univariate GARCH model of Chapter 5 extends directly to a matrix, but the number of parameters explodes and positive definiteness is not automatic. The DCC model of Engle (2002) solves both problems by separating volatilities from correlations: each variance follows its own GARCH model, and two parameters drive all the correlations. The section derives the two-step estimator, discusses its consistency and inference, and applies the model to SPY and TLT and to a minimum-variance portfolio.

          From Univariate to Multivariate Volatility (1/2): the Model

          Slide 20 in the PDF

          In Chapter 5, the return of a single asset was \(r_t=\sigma_t z_t\) with \(\sigma_t^2=\omega+\alpha r_{t-1}^2+\beta\sigma_{t-1}^2\). With \(N\) assets, the return vector is written

          \[r_t=H_t^{1/2} z_t, \qquad \mathbb{E}(z_t\mid\mathcal F_{t-1})=0, \quad \operatorname{Var}(z_t\mid\mathcal F_{t-1})=I_N,\]

          so that \(\operatorname{Var}(r_t\mid\mathcal F_{t-1})=H_t\). Here \(z_t\) is the vector of innovations, \(\mathcal F_{t-1}\) the information up to \(t-1\), \(H_t\) the \(N\times N\) conditional covariance matrix, known at \(t-1\) (it is \(\Sigma_t\) of the previous section), \(H_t^{1/2}\) a matrix square root with \(H_t^{1/2}(H_t^{1/2})^\top=H_t\) and \(I_N\) the identity matrix.

          Any model for \(H_t\) must satisfy three requirements:

          1. \(H_t\) must be positive definite at every \(t\), so that every portfolio has a positive variance;
          2. The number of parameters must stay small;
          3. The dynamics must be interpretable.

          The parameters are estimated by maximising the Gaussian quasi-log-likelihood

          \[\ell=-\tfrac12\sum_t\big(N\ln 2\pi+\ln|H_t|+r_t^\top H_t^{-1}r_t\big),\]
          (6.7)

          where \(|H_t|\) is the determinant of \(H_t\). The prefix quasi means that the estimates remain consistent when \(z_t\) is not Normal, provided the model for \(H_t\) is correctly specified.

          Constant and Dynamic Conditional Correlation

          Slide 22 in the PDF

          The DCC model starts from the decomposition of a covariance matrix into volatilities and correlations,

          \[H_t=D_t R_t D_t, \qquad D_t=\operatorname{diag}(\sigma_{1t},\dots,\sigma_{Nt}),\]

          where \(\sigma_{it}\) is the GARCH volatility of asset \(i\), \(R_t\) the conditional correlation matrix and \(\varepsilon_{it}=r_{it}/\sigma_{it}\) the standardised residuals. The constant conditional correlation (CCC) model of Bollerslev (1990) sets \(R_t=R\). The DCC model of Engle (2002) lets the correlations follow a GARCH-type recursion for a proxy matrix \(Q_t\), rescaled into a correlation matrix \(R_t\):

          \[Q_t=(1-a-b)\,\bar Q+a\,\varepsilon_{t-1}\varepsilon_{t-1}^\top+b\,Q_{t-1},\]
          (6.8)
          \[R_t=\operatorname{diag}(Q_t)^{-1/2}\,Q_t\,\operatorname{diag}(Q_t)^{-1/2},\]

          where \(a,b\ge0\) and \(a+b<1\). The parameter \(a\) is the reaction to yesterday's product of shocks, \(b\) the memory and \(a+b\) the persistence. The second equation rescales \(Q_t\) so that its diagonal is 1, which makes \(R_t\) a valid correlation matrix.

          The matrix \(\bar Q\) is the intercept of the recursion, with \(N(N-1)/2\) free elements. Correlation targeting replaces it by the sample covariance of the standardised residuals, \(\hat{\bar Q}=\frac1T\sum_t\varepsilon_t\varepsilon_t^\top\); when this replacement is consistent is the subject of Two-Step Estimation (2/2). Two parameters then govern all \(N(N-1)/2\) correlations. The model is parsimonious, but every pair of assets has the same persistence. Note also that \(Q_t\) is a covariance proxy, not a correlation matrix: \(\mathbb{E}_{t-1}[\varepsilon_t\varepsilon_t^\top]=R_t\ne Q_t\).

          In brief

          • Decomposition: covariance = volatilities \(\times\) correlations \(\times\) volatilities

          • CCC (constant conditional correlation, Bollerslev (1990)): \(R_t=R\) constant
          • DCC (Engle (2002)): a GARCH-type recursion for a proxy matrix \(Q_t\)
            • \(Q_t=(1-a-b)\,\bar Q+a\,\varepsilon_{t-1}\varepsilon_{t-1}^\top+b\,Q_{t-1}\), \(\quad a,b\ge0\), \(a+b<1\)
            • \(R_t=\operatorname{diag}(Q_t)^{-1/2}\,Q_t\,\operatorname{diag}(Q_t)^{-1/2}\)
            • \(\bar Q\): the intercept matrix of the recursion, with \(N(N-1)/2\) free elements. Correlation targeting replaces it by the sample covariance \(\hat{\bar Q}=\frac1T\sum_t\varepsilon_t\varepsilon_t^\top\) (when this is consistent: slide “Two-Step Estimation (2/2)”)

          Two-Step Estimation (1/2): Splitting the Likelihood

          Slide 23 in the PDF

          Substituting \(H_t=D_tR_tD_t\) and \(\varepsilon_t=D_t^{-1}r_t\) into the quasi-log-likelihood (6.7) splits it into a volatility part and a correlation part. The determinant factorises as \(|H_t|=|D_t|^2|R_t|\), so \(\ln|H_t|=2\ln|D_t|+\ln|R_t|\), and the quadratic form becomes \(r_t^\top H_t^{-1}r_t=\varepsilon_t^\top R_t^{-1}\varepsilon_t\). Adding and subtracting \(\varepsilon_t^\top\varepsilon_t=r_t^\top D_t^{-2}r_t\) gives

          \[\ell=\underbrace{-\tfrac12\sum_t\big(N\ln2\pi+2\ln|D_t|+r_t^\top D_t^{-2}r_t\big)}_{\ell_V}\;\underbrace{-\tfrac12\sum_t\big(\ln|R_t|+\varepsilon_t^\top R_t^{-1}\varepsilon_t-\varepsilon_t^\top\varepsilon_t\big)}_{\ell_C} .\]
          (6.9)

          The volatility part \(\ell_V\) depends only on the GARCH parameters. Since \(D_t\) is diagonal, it equals \(\sum_i\big[-\tfrac12\sum_t(\ln2\pi+\ln\sigma_{it}^2+r_{it}^2/\sigma_{it}^2)\big]\), the sum of the \(N\) univariate Gaussian GARCH log-likelihoods. The correlation part \(\ell_C\) depends on \((a,b)\) given the volatilities.

          The split gives the two-step estimator:

          1. Step 1 fits \(N\) univariate GARCH(1,1) models and keeps the volatilities \(\sigma_{it}\) and the standardised residuals \(\varepsilon_{it}\).
          2. Step 2 fixes \(\bar Q\) at the sample covariance of \(\varepsilon_t\) and maximises \(\ell_C\) over \((a,b)\).

          Two-Step Estimation (2/2): Targeting and Inference

          Slide 24 in the PDF

          Targeting with the sample covariance makes the standard DCC estimator inconsistent in general. Taking expectations of the recursion (6.8) and using \(\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon_{t-1}\varepsilon_{t-1}^\top]=\mathbb{E}[R_{t-1}]\), stationarity gives \((1-b)\,\mathbb{E}[Q_t]=(1-a-b)\,\bar Q+a\,\mathbb{E}[R_t]\). The sample covariance \(\hat{\bar Q}\) estimates \(\mathbb{E}[R_t]\), which equals the intercept \(\bar Q\) only if \(\mathbb{E}[Q_t]=\mathbb{E}[R_t]\). That fails in general, because \(R_t\) rescales \(Q_t\) by its own random diagonal. The corrected DCC (cDCC) of Aielli (2013) uses \(\operatorname{diag}(Q_{t-1})^{1/2}\varepsilon_{t-1}\) in place of \(\varepsilon_{t-1}\), so that \(\mathbb{E}[Q_t]=\bar Q\) and targeting remains consistent.

              Crisis Correlations and the Forbes–Rigobon Bias

              Correlations measured during crises are usually higher than in calm periods. This section asks whether such a rise is contagion, a change in the transmission mechanism, or a mechanical consequence of higher volatility in the source market. Forbes & Rigobon (2002) derive the volatility bias of the correlation coefficient and an adjustment for it. The section derives the bias, shows its size, describes the test and its inference, and applies it to the 2008 crisis.

              Contagion or Interdependence?

              Slide 30 in the PDF

              A common finding is that correlations measured during crises exceed those of calm periods. A high crisis correlation is not yet contagion: it must exceed the calm one. Two very different explanations compete. Under contagion, the transmission mechanism between markets changes. Under the volatility bias, the mechanism is unchanged, but a more volatile source market mechanically raises the measured correlation.

              Forbes & Rigobon (2002) show that, once the bias is corrected, most of the apparent contagion in the 1987, 1994 and 1997 crises becomes interdependence. A related finding concerns asymmetry: Longin & Solnik (2001) and Ang & Chen (2002) find that correlations rise in bear markets but not in bull markets, an asymmetry the Normal distribution cannot produce.               The Heteroskedasticity Bias (1/2): Correlation in a Calm Period

              Slide 31 in the PDF

              The bias follows from a linear model of transmission. Let \(x_t\) be the return of the source market and \(y_t\) that of the other market,

              \[y_t=\alpha+\beta x_t+\varepsilon_t, \qquad \operatorname{Cov}(x_t,\varepsilon_t)=0,\]

              with a constant transmission coefficient \(\beta\) and an own shock \(\varepsilon_t\) of constant variance \(\sigma_\varepsilon^2\); \(\sigma_x^2\) is the variance of \(x\). Because \(\varepsilon_t\) is uncorrelated with \(x_t\), \(\operatorname{Cov}(x_t,y_t)=\beta\sigma_x^2\) and \(\operatorname{Var}(y_t)=\beta^2\sigma_x^2+\sigma_\varepsilon^2\).

              The squared correlation is therefore

              \[\rho^2=\frac{\beta^2\sigma_x^2}{\beta^2\sigma_x^2+\sigma_\varepsilon^2}=\frac{k}{1+k}, \qquad k=\frac{\beta^2\sigma_x^2}{\sigma_\varepsilon^2},\]
              (6.10)

              where \(k\) is the signal-to-noise ratio, the variance transmitted from \(x\) relative to the own variance of \(y\). Equivalently, \(k=\rho^2/(1-\rho^2)\). The correlation depends on the variance of the source market only through \(k\), and \(k\) rises with \(\sigma_x^2\) even when \(\beta\) does not change.

              In brief

              • Model: \(y_t=\alpha+\beta x_t+\varepsilon_t\), \(\Cov(x_t,\varepsilon_t)=0\), \(\beta\) and \(\sigma_\varepsilon^2\) constant
                • \(x_t\): return of the source market
                • \(y_t\): return of the other market
                • \(\beta\): transmission coefficient
                • \(\varepsilon_t\): own shock of \(y\), variance \(\sigma_\varepsilon^2\)
                • \(\sigma_x^2\): variance of \(x\)
              • Two moments of the model

              • Correlation: \(\rho^2=\dfrac{\beta^2\sigma_x^2}{\beta^2\sigma_x^2+\sigma_\varepsilon^2}=\Big(1+\dfrac{\sigma_\varepsilon^2}{\beta^2\sigma_x^2}\Big)^{-1}\)
              • The same in one number: the signal-to-noise ratio \(k=\beta^2\sigma_x^2/\sigma_\varepsilon^2\)
                • \(\rho^2=\dfrac{k}{1+k}\), equivalently \(k=\dfrac{\rho^2}{1-\rho^2}\): the correlation depends on \(\sigma_x^2\) only through \(k\)

              The Heteroskedasticity Bias (2/2): From Calm to Crisis

              Slide 32 in the PDF

              In a crisis, denoted by the subscript \(c\), the variance of the source market rises to \(\sigma_{x,c}^2=(1+\delta)\,\sigma_x^2\), where \(\delta>0\) is its relative increase, and nothing else changes. Then \(k_c=(1+\delta)k\), and (6.10) gives \(\rho_c^2=(1+\delta)\rho^2/(1+\delta\rho^2)\), so

              \[\rho_c=\rho\sqrt{\frac{1+\delta}{1+\delta\rho^2}} .\]
              (6.11)

              For \(0<|\rho|<1\), \(|\rho_c|>|\rho|\): the measured correlation rises in absolute value although \(\beta\) is the same. Inverting (6.11) gives the crisis correlation adjusted for the volatility increase,

              \[\rho^*=\frac{\rho_c}{\sqrt{1+\delta(1-\rho_c^2)}} .\]
              (6.12)

              A quick check fixes the orders of magnitude. If the source variance doubles (\(\delta=1\)) and \(\rho=0.5\), then \(k=1/3\), \(k_c=2/3\) and \(\rho_c^2=0.4\), so \(\rho_c=0.632\): the correlation rises by \(0.13\) with no change in \(\beta\). Applying (6.12) gives \(0.632/\sqrt{1+1\cdot(1-0.4)}=0.5\), and the calm value is recovered.

                The Forbes–Rigobon Test and Its Inference

                Slide 34 in the PDF

                The test follows four steps:

                1. Choose the source market \(x\), a calm window and a crisis window.
                2. Use 2-day returns, to absorb the different closing times.
                3. Compute \(\delta=\hat\sigma^2_{x,c}/\hat\sigma^2_{x,calm}-1\) and the adjusted correlation \(\rho^*\) of (6.12).
                4. Test \(H_0\colon\rho^*\le\rho_{calm}\) with Fisher \(z\) statistics, the convention of Forbes & Rigobon (2002).

                The inference needs care. With \(\delta\) fixed, \(\operatorname{Var}(\operatorname{artanh}\rho^*)\approx1/[n_c(1+\delta(1-\rho_c^2))]<1/n_c\), where \(n_c\) is the number of crisis observations, so the Fisher test is conservative. But \(\delta\) is estimated, and 2-day returns overlap. The chapter therefore resamples the calm and crisis windows jointly with a stationary bootstrap, with mean block length 10 days and \(B=1{,}999\) draws, and tests the difference \(D=\rho^*-\rho_{calm}\). The stationary bootstrap resamples blocks of consecutive days with random starts and geometrically distributed lengths, which keeps the serial dependence inside each block.

                The adjustment rests on three assumptions:

                • No omitted common shock: a global factor that hits both markets raises \(\sigma_\varepsilon\) as well as \(\sigma_x\), and the adjustment then over-corrects (Corsetti, Pericoli & Sbracia, 2005).
                • No feedback from \(y\) to \(x\).
                • A residual variance \(\sigma_\varepsilon^2\) that does not change between periods.

                The choice of windows matters too: results can flip when the windows move by a few weeks.

                  Copulas and Tail Dependence

                  Correlation summarises linear dependence by one number. Risk management cares about joint extremes: the probability that two assets fall sharply on the same day. This section first measures dependence in the tails with exceedance correlations, then shows why correlation cannot describe it and introduces copulas, which separate the marginal distributions from the dependence structure. It presents five copula families, tail dependence, the estimation of copulas on GARCH-filtered returns and an application to two US banks, JPMorgan Chase (JPM) and Bank of America (BAC).

                    Why Correlation Is Not Enough

                    Slide 38 in the PDF

                    Embrechts, McNeil & Straumann (2002) list three fallacies about linear correlation:

                    1. Correlation measures only linear dependence and is not invariant to monotone transformations: in general \(\operatorname{Corr}(x,y)\ne\operatorname{Corr}(e^x,e^y)\).
                    2. The marginal distributions and the correlation do not determine the joint distribution, so two portfolios with identical margins and the same \(\rho\) can have very different Value-at-Risk (Chapter 7).
                    3. For given margins not every correlation in \([-1,1]\) is attainable. The attainable range \([\rho_{\min},\rho_{\max}]\) is reached by countermonotone and comonotone pairs.

                    For lognormal variables \(X=e^{\sigma_1Z}\) and \(Y=e^{\pm\sigma_2Z}\), with \(Z\) standard Normal, the plus sign gives the comonotone pair and the minus sign the countermonotone one, and

                    \[\rho_{\max}=\frac{e^{\sigma_1\sigma_2}-1}{\sqrt{(e^{\sigma_1^2}-1)(e^{\sigma_2^2}-1)}},\]

                    with \(e^{-\sigma_1\sigma_2}\) in the numerator for \(\rho_{\min}\). For \(\sigma_1=1\) and \(\sigma_2=3\), \(\rho_{\max}=0.162\) and \(\rho_{\min}=-0.008\): the two variables are perfectly dependent, one being a function of the other, yet almost uncorrelated.

                    Sklar's Theorem: Margins plus Copula

                    Slide 39 in the PDF

                    A copula is a joint distribution function on \([0,1]^d\) with uniform margins, where \(d\) is the number of variables. The theorem of Sklar (1959) states that for any joint distribution \(F\) with margins \(F_1,\dots,F_d\) there is a copula \(C\) such that

                    \[F(x_1,\dots,x_d)=C\big(F_1(x_1),\dots,F_d(x_d)\big),\]
                    (6.14)

                    where \(u_i=F_i(x_i)\in[0,1]\) is the probability level of \(x_i\). The copula is unique if the margins are continuous. Conversely, any copula combined with any margins gives a valid joint distribution. With densities, \(f(x)=c\big(F_1(x_1),\dots,F_d(x_d)\big)\prod_i f_i(x_i)\), where \(c\) is the copula density and \(f_i\) are the marginal densities; the comonotone copula has no density.

                    The decomposition (6.14) assigns the features of the data to two parts. The margins carry the volatility, skewness and fat tails of each asset (Chapters 1 and 5), and the copula carries all the dependence. Because the copula is invariant to increasing transformations of each variable, it does not change when prices are replaced by log prices or returns by their ranks.

                    Three reference copulas bound the others. Independence is \(\Pi(u,v)=uv\), comonotonicity \(M(u,v)=\min(u,v)\) and countermonotonicity \(W(u,v)=\max(u+v-1,0)\). Every copula lies between the Fréchet–Hoeffding bounds, \(W\le C\le M\) (Nelsen, 2006).

                    In brief

                    • A copula is a joint distribution function on \([0,1]^d\) with uniform margins

                      • \(d\): number of variables

                    Rank Correlations: Kendall and Spearman

                    Slide 40 in the PDF

                    Two dependence measures depend only on the copula. Kendall's tau (Kendall, 1938) compares concordant and discordant pairs: for two independent draws \((X_1,Y_1)\) and \((X_2,Y_2)\) of the pair,

                    \[\tau=P\big[(X_1-X_2)(Y_1-Y_2)>0\big]-P\big[(X_1-X_2)(Y_1-Y_2)<0\big]=4\,\mathbb{E}[C(U,V)]-1,\]
                    (6.15)

                    where a pair is concordant when both coordinates move in the same direction. Spearman's rho (Spearman, 1904) is the Pearson correlation of the ranks, with copula form \(\rho_S=12\iint (C(u,v)-uv)\,du\,dv\). Both lie in \([-1,1]\) and equal 1 for \(M\), 0 for \(\Pi\) and \(-1\) for \(W\).

                    For the families of the chapter, (6.15) has closed forms in the copula parameter \(\theta\): \(\tau=\frac2\pi\arcsin\rho\) for the Gaussian and t copulas, \(\theta/(\theta+2)\) for Clayton, \(1-1/\theta\) for Gumbel, and an expression in the Debye function \(D_1\) for Frank. For the Gaussian copula, also \(\rho_S=\frac6\pi\arcsin(\rho/2)\). With \(\rho=0.5\), \(\tau=\frac2\pi\cdot\frac\pi6=1/3\) and \(\rho_S=\frac6\pi\arcsin0.25=0.483\). Inverting these relations gives a simple estimator of \(\theta\) from the sample \(\hat\tau\).

                    \[\lambda_L=\lim_{q\downarrow0}P(V\le q\mid U\le q)=\lim_{q\downarrow0}\frac{C(q,q)}{q},\]
                    (6.16)

                      Estimating Copulas

                      Slide 45 in the PDF

                      There are three approaches. Full maximum likelihood estimates the margins and the copula jointly; it is efficient but computationally demanding and sensitive to misspecification. The method of inference functions for margins (IFM) of Joe (2005) has two steps: fit parametric margins, for example GARCH models with Student t innovations, set \(\hat u_{it}=\hat F_i(x_{it})\) with \(\hat F_i\) the fitted marginal distribution function, and maximise \(\sum_t\ln c(\hat u_{1t},\hat u_{2t};\theta)\), where \(c\) is the copula density.

                      Canonical maximum likelihood (CML) (Genest, Ghoudi & Rivest, 1995) needs no parametric margins. It replaces the margins by pseudo-observations,

                      \[\hat u_{it}=\frac{\operatorname{rank}(x_{it})}{T+1},\]
                      (6.17)

                      and then maximises the copula likelihood. The denominator \(T+1\) keeps \(\hat u\) away from 0 and 1, where the log-density of many copulas is infinite. CML is robust to misspecified margins.

                      For returns, each series is first filtered with GARCH, and CML is applied to the ranks of the standardised residuals; this is the method used from here on. Without filtering, volatility clustering would mix dependence with changing volatility. The static CML inference shown here assumes i.i.d. innovations, which GARCH filtering alone does not guarantee. Most of the families compared are not nested, so the choice uses the information criteria AIC \(=2k-2\ell\) (Akaike) and BIC \(=k\ln T-2\ell\) (Bayesian), with \(k\) the number of parameters and \(\ell\) the maximised log-likelihood; for the Gaussian against the t copula, an LR test calibrated by bootstrap at \(\nu=\infty\) is used in the seminar.

                            A Cautionary Tale: The Gaussian Copula and 2008

                            Slide 50 in the PDF

                            Li (2000) modelled the joint default times of credit portfolios with a Gaussian copula, with one correlation parameter calibrated to market spreads. The model priced whole credit portfolios and became the standard for the tranches of collateralised debt obligations (CDO).

                            The Gaussian copula has zero asymptotic tail dependence. For the most extreme joint defaults it therefore gives lower probabilities than tail-dependent models calibrated to the same data. The correlation was also static and calibrated in calm years. Both weaknesses correspond to lessons of this chapter: the Forbes–Rigobon bias and the DCC evidence show that correlations move between regimes, and the copula section shows that the Gaussian copula cannot produce joint extremes in the limit.

                                    Case Study (1/2): Which Events Shake All Markets at Once?

                                    Slide 57 in the PDF

                                    Engle & Campos-Martins (2023) ask which events move the volatilities of almost all countries and asset classes on the same day, and how large this common shock is. Its daily size is global COVOL. The starting point is a property of a correctly specified DCC model: its residuals \(e_t\) satisfy \(\mathbb{E}_{t-1}(e_te_t^\top)=I\), where \(e_t\) is the vector of the \(N\) DCC residuals of day \(t\) and \(\mathbb{E}_{t-1}\) the expectation given the information at \(t-1\).

                                    Uncorrelated is not independent: the squared residuals can still move together. The paper studies the matrix

                                    \[\Psi_t=\mathbb{E}_{t-1}\big[(e_t^2-\iota)(e_t^2-\iota)^\top\big],\]

                                    where \(e_t^2\) is the element-by-element square and \(\iota\) the vector of ones. An off-diagonal element \(\Psi_{ij,t}\neq0\) means that the squared shocks of assets \(i\) and \(j\) are large on the same days. This is the cross-sectional analogue of ARCH (autoregressive conditional heteroskedasticity) in Chapter 5: squares correlated across assets on one day, rather than over time. Herskovic, Kelly, Lustig & Van Nieuwerburgh (2016) document such a factor in the idiosyncratic volatility of US stocks.

                                    Case Study (2/2): One Common Factor Scales Every Variance

                                    Slide 58 in the PDF

                                    The model assumes that one common factor \(x_t\) scales the variance of every residual:

                                    \[e_{it}=\sqrt{s_ix_t+1-s_i}\;\varepsilon_{it},\]
                                    (6.18)

                                    with \(\varepsilon_{it}\) i.i.d. \(\mathcal N(0,1)\), \(x_t>0\) and \(\mathbb{E}_{t-1}(x_t)=1\). Here \(x_t\) is the squared global COVOL of day \(t\), with \(x_t>1\) on a common shock day, and \(s_i\in[0,1]\) the loading of asset \(i\), the weight of \(x_t\) in its conditional variance \(g_{it}=s_ix_t+1-s_i=1+s_i(x_t-1)\).

                                    In (6.18) only the products \(s_i(x_t-1)\) are identified, so the scale must be fixed by a normalisation. With equal loadings \(s_i=1\), the estimate is the cross-sectional mean of the squared residuals, \(\hat x_t=\frac1N\sum_ie_{it}^2\), on the variance scale of the residuals. With unequal loadings, the estimation alternates maximum likelihood over \(x_t\), one cross-section per day, and over \(s_i\), one time series per asset, under \(s^\top s=1\) and a mean of \(x_t\) equal to 1. Under \(s^\top s=1\) the loadings are relative sizes, not variance shares; equal loadings would be \(s_i=1/\sqrt N\).

                                    The test statistic \(\xi\) is the standardised mean, over days and pairs, of the products \((e_{it}^2-1)(e_{jt}^2-1)\); under \(H_0\colon\Psi_{ij}=0\) for \(i\neq j\) it is standard Normal. The data of the paper are 47 country ETFs listed on the New York Stock Exchange (NYSE), 2000–2021, and 23 US asset-class series; details are in the Appendix.

                                    DCC in Vast Dimensions: Does the Estimation Survive Many Assets?

                                    Slide 61 in the PDF

                                    Pakel, Shephard, Sheppard & Engle (2021) ask whether the dynamic parameters \((a,b)\) of DCC-type models can be estimated on hundreds of assets. The question matters because the VaR of a large portfolio and hedging need the conditional correlations of all assets. The paper's claim, for scalar BEKK and cDCC, is that with correlation targeting the full likelihood gives strongly biased \((\hat a,\hat b)\) as the number of assets \(K\) grows.

                                    The mechanism is the target. \(\bar Q\) has \(K(K-1)/2\) correlations, estimated before \((a,b)\); the number of these incidental parameters grows with \(K\), and their estimation errors accumulate in the likelihood. Every evaluation of the full likelihood also inverts \(T\) matrices of size \(K\times K\), so the cost grows with \(K^3\).

                                    The remedy is the composite likelihood (CL), the sum of the bivariate likelihoods of pairs of assets:

                                    \[\ell_{CL}(a,b)=\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal P}\ell_{ij}(a,b),\]
                                    (6.19)

                                    where each pair uses its own \(2\times2\) block of \(\hat{\bar Q}\) and \(\mathcal P\) contains either all \(K(K-1)/2\) pairs or only the contiguous pairs \((1,2),(2,3),\dots,(K-1,K)\). No large matrix is inverted, and the theory of the paper lets \(K\) grow with \(T\). The data of the paper are the stocks of the S&P 100 and of the S&P 500, daily, 1997–2006.

                                    In brief

                                        Testing Constant Correlation at an Unknown Date

                                        Slide 72 in the PDF

                                        Picking a break date from a chart and then testing it with a fixed-date Fisher \(z\) test is oversized, because the date is itself a function of the data. Tse (2000) gives a Lagrange multiplier test of constant correlation against \(\rho_{ij,t}=\rho_{ij}+\delta_{ij}\,\varepsilon_{i,t-1}\varepsilon_{j,t-1}\), computed from the fitted CCC model alone. Wied, Krämer & Dehling (2012) compare the correlation of the first \(j\) observations, \(\hat\rho_j\), with the full-sample \(\hat\rho_T\):

                                        \[Q_T=\max_{2\le j\le T}\frac{j}{\sqrt T\,\hat D}\,\big|\hat\rho_j-\hat\rho_T\big|,\]
                                        (6.21)

                                        where \(\hat D^2\) is the delta-method long-run variance of \(\hat\rho\), with a Bartlett kernel and bandwidth \(\lfloor T^{1/4}\rfloor\), and the estimated break date is the maximiser. Under constant correlation, means and variances, \(Q_T\) converges to \(\sup_{0\le s\le1}|B(s)|\), with \(B\) a Brownian bridge and \(s=j/T\); the 5% critical value is 1.358. Because constant variances are required, the test is applied to GARCH-standardised residuals.

                                        Inference for Copula Parameters (1/2): Inverting Kendall's Tau

                                        Slide 81 in the PDF

                                        Kendall's \(\hat\tau\) is a U-statistic, an average over all pairs of observations, with a standard error from its Hoeffding projection; the copula parameter follows as \(\hat\theta=g(\hat\tau)\) by the delta method (Genest & Rivest, 1993), where \(g\) inverts the link \(\tau(\theta)\) of (6.15). For elliptical copulas, \(\tau=\frac2\pi\arcsin\rho\) for every \(\nu\), so \(\tau\) identifies \(\rho\) but never \(\nu\). For JPM and BAC, \(\hat\tau=0.641\) (SE 0.0065) gives \(\hat\rho_\tau=\sin(\pi\hat\tau/2)=0.845\) (SE 0.0055), close to the CML estimate \(\hat\rho=0.842\) (SE 0.0053).

                                        Inference for Copula Parameters (2/2): Ranks, Fit and GARCH Filtering

                                        Slide 82 in the PDF

                                        In CML the ranks are themselves estimated, and the inverse Hessian ignores this uncertainty; the sandwich of Genest, Ghoudi & Rivest (1995) adds the rank terms. The SE rises by 60% for the Gaussian \(\rho\) of weekly S&P 500–Euro Stoxx 50 returns (0.0086 to 0.0138), by 17% for the t-copula \(\rho\) of JPM–BAC (0.0046 to 0.0053) and by 1% for \(\nu\). If the Gaussian copula were correct, ranking would multiply the SE by \(\sqrt{1+\rho^2}\), 1.27 at the weekly correlation 0.78 (Monte Carlo at \(\rho=0.8\): 1.28), so the observed factor of 1.60 points to a misspecified Gaussian copula; the sandwich stays valid in this case too. After GARCH filtering, Chen & Fan (2006) show that CML on the ranks of estimated residuals has the same limit as on the true innovations, provided the innovations are i.i.d. with copula \(C_\theta\) and the GARCH model is correctly specified; unlike DCC, no step-1 correction is then needed.

                                        Goodness of Fit: The Rosenblatt Test

                                        Slide 83 in the PDF

                                        If \((U,V)\sim C_\theta\), then \(E_1=U\) and \(E_2=h(V\mid U)=\partial C_\theta(U,V)/\partial u\), where \(h\) is the conditional distribution function of \(V\) given \(U\), are independent uniforms. The statistic of Genest, Rémillard & Beaudoin (2009), \(S_n=n\int_{[0,1]^2}\big(D_n(e)-e_1e_2\big)^2de\), compares the share \(D_n(e)\) of pairs with \(E_1\le e_1\) and \(E_2\le e_2\) with its value \(e_1e_2\) under independence; large values mean a poor fit. Because \(\theta\) is estimated, the \(p\)-value comes from a parametric bootstrap that simulates from \(C_{\hat\theta}\), re-ranks, re-estimates and recomputes \(S_n\), with \(p=(1+\#\{S_n^{(b)}\ge S_n\})/(B+1)\); for JPM and BAC, \(B=200\) (100 for the t copula), so the smallest attainable \(p\)-value is \(1/201\approx0.005\) (\(1/101\approx0.010\) for t). On GARCH residuals, rank-based tests keep their limit (Rémillard, 2017). With \(T=4{,}202\) the test has high power against small departures, and a rejection does not say which feature is missing.

                                        Is the Tail Asymmetry Real?

                                        Slide 84 in the PDF

                                        The estimator of the main text is that of Schmidt & Stadtmüller (2006), \(\hat\lambda_L=\frac nk\hat C_n(\frac kn,\frac kn)\), with \(n\) the sample size, \(k\) the number of observations in the tail and \(\hat C_n\) the empirical copula; a larger \(k\) lowers the variance but raises the bias. The test of \(H_0\colon\lambda_L(q)=\lambda_U(q)\) at a fixed \(q\), a comparison of threshold probabilities rather than limits, uses a bootstrap of residual pairs, re-ranked, with \(B=999\), and a stationary block version with mean blocks of 1.6 and 1.0 days chosen by the rule of Politis & White (2004). At \(q=5\%\) (\(k=210\)) the difference is 0.062, with 95% intervals \([-0.014, 0.148]\) (i.i.d.) and \([-0.014, 0.138]\) (blocks); at \(q=1\%\) (\(k=42\)) it is 0.095, with intervals \([-0.095, 0.286]\) and \([-0.119, 0.286]\). All intervals contain zero, so the asymmetry is not detected statistically; the test does not validate the t specification, which the global goodness-of-fit test rejects.

                                        Slide 92 in the PDF

                                        Copula Densities and Constructions

                                        Slide 96 in the PDF

                                        The Gaussian copula density, with \(x=\Phi^{-1}(u)\) and \(y=\Phi^{-1}(v)\), is \(c=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\rho^2}}\exp\big(-\frac{\rho^2(x^2+y^2)-2\rho xy}{2(1-\rho^2)}\big)\); for the t copula, tail dependence is large for small \(\nu\). The Archimedean generators are \(\varphi(t)=(t^{-\theta}-1)/\theta\) for Clayton, \((-\ln t)^\theta\) for Gumbel and \(-\ln\frac{e^{-\theta t}-1}{e^{-\theta}-1}\) for Frank.
